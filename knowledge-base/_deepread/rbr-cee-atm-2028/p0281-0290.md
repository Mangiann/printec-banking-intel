### p.281 — Russia: banking structure narrative (Russia Page 2)
Continuation of bank-by-bank sanctions/exit narrative.
- **Home Credit & Finance Bank (Hoculpo)** already represented the largest individual share of ATMs by end of 2021; presence strengthened considerably in 2022 (rising from 35% to 42%) as the largest deployer Sberbank neared completion of its long-planned fleet update, removing other ATMs and rolling out Hyosung Innovue machines instead.
- **NCR** and **Diebold Nixdorf** suspended operations in Russia in March 2022 in response to the war. Their machines still represented half of the installed base at end-2022 but this was a significant drop from their 60% combined share at end-2021. Deployers concerned about reliance on manufacturers no longer supplying the market removed some of their NCR and Diebold Nixdorf ATMs, future-proofing supplies by upping numbers from Hyosung Innovue, OKI and GRG instead. Russian manufacturing firms began testing their own ATMs throughout 2022, with the aim of supplying some major deployers with domestic ATMs by 2023.
- Several western providers of software and services also suspended operations in Russia in connection with the war and sanctions, but as deployers mostly remained able to use existing licences, the impact of this was not fully felt in 2022 and shares remained largely stable.
- **HP** remained the largest provider of network central hardware to Russian ATMs, while the most common solution for terminal driving software was SmartVista. The biggest change in the software environment was progress in Sberbank's migration of its application software to a proprietary solution.
- **63% of all ATMs** were replenished in-house in 2022. First-line maintenance was outsourced for **68% of ATMs**, while second-line maintenance is only carried out in-house by Sberbank and Alfa-Bank. The vast majority of deployers operate help-desk services in-house.
- **By 2028, the number of ATMs in Russia is expected to fall to 103,000 units.** Deployers are expected to draw new supplies from Asian and domestic manufacturers, but numbers will decline anyway owing to reducing cash usage in the country and faster uptake of digital payment methods.

### p.283 — Russia: banking structure narrative (Russia Page 4)
- The Russian banking sector suffered since 2014, when the country saw a financial crisis triggered by a fall in the price of oil as well as international economic sanctions imposed against the country due to the annexation of Crimea. Several Russian banks were restricted from receiving long-term loans from international investors and the Russian government was forced to recapitalise the banking sector in late 2014. The consequent devaluation of the rouble caused a sharp rise in inflation and a subsequent two-year recession.
- At the time of writing (May 2023), the Russian banking sector is subject to wide-ranging international sanctions connected to Russia's conflict with Ukraine. The US, EU, UK and Canada froze the foreign assets of the CBRF, which lost access to much of its foreign currency reserves. Many of the country's biggest commercial banks are subject to capital market bans, asset freezes and disconnection from financial messaging service Swift. Banks subject to some of the harshest international sanctions include **VTB Bank, Bank Otkritie, Sovcombank, Novikombank, Promsvyazbank** and **VEB.RF** (banking sanctions list as printed).

### p.284 — Russia: Sberbank narrative (Russia Page 5)
- The largest bank by all measures (ATMs, branches, assets) has historically been **Sberbank**, with **RUB 38.8 trillion (USD 515.3 billion)** at the end of 2021. Sberbank is subject to US, EU and UK blocking sanctions but was originally excluded from the list of banks excluded from Swift to support the continuation of international energy payments. This ended in June 2022 when the EU opted to extend the Swift ban to Sberbank also.
- At end-2022 Sberbank operated **12,244 bank branches** in Russia, down from **13,561 in 2021**. Despite the decrease, Sberbank's branch network remained the largest in the country; next largest operated by **VTB Bank with 1,313 locations at end-2022, compared to 1,404 the previous year**.
- Sberbank and VTB Bank's branch reductions are part of a wider trend in Russia which has seen total branch numbers fall for **five consecutive years** and drop by a further **6% in 2022 to 28,947**. While licence revocations by the CBRF each year also contribute to the country-wide decline, the main factor is the greater shift to digital and remote forms of banking, particularly via app.

### p.285 — Section 3: ATM networks and sharing (Russia Page 6)
- In 1991, shortly before the breakup of the USSR, ATMs were installed in Russia for the first time, located in the Moscow World Trade Centre and offices of American Express. In March 1994, the first ATMs compatible with international payment schemes were installed by Most Bank (now defunct) and Credit Moscow Bank. Growth in ATM numbers strengthened in the 2000s following the recovery from the Russian financial crisis of 1998; in the decade leading up to 2012, an average of over 11,000 new ATMs were installed each year.
- Rising payment cardholding amongst the Russian population has been the key driver of the Russian installed base over the last 20 years. Cardholding has been largely driven by salary credit ("salary cards") agreements between banks and companies whereby one bank gains exclusive rights to issue payment cards for salary payments to all employees of a given organisation. Often these agreements involve banks deploying an ATM on company premises, providing salary card holders convenient access to cash withdrawals and other services. It was previously common for Russian cardholders to withdraw their entire monthly salaries in a single transaction due to a strong preference for cash payments. The picture started to change in the early 2010s as payment cards gained more momentum.
- Banks invested heavily in the development of their self-service channels, partly due to an effort to tackle long queuing times in branches, with those seeking cash withdrawals waiting together with consumers applying for loans during a period of several increased reliance on increased consumer lending. Increased deployment of both ATMs and self-service deposit terminals (SATs) enabled banks to shift many types of everyday transactions away from the banking counter. The biggest change in the software environment was progress in Sberbank's migration of its application software to a proprietary solution.
- By 2018, over 130 processing centres had been opened in Russia; the result of rapid growth in payment card issuance from the late 1990s to 2000s. These mostly belong to the major card-issuing banks which found keeping transaction processing in-house cost-effective and also offered these services to smaller players in the market, for whom scheme membership fees were prohibitive.
- In 2007, Rosbank entered into an alliance with United Card Service (UCS) — now part of Global Payments — under which the bank provided UCS and its members with a Visa and Mastercard bank sponsorship and agent bank processing referrals. UCS also then launched a brand of mutual ATMs in the ORS network alongside deployer. By end-2008, the network accounted for over 3,000 ATMs. The following year, Rosbank announced its intention to create a large nationwide shared ATM network which would make other banks' ATM coverage more competitive with that of Sberbank. The bank chose the UCS platform to accomplish this aim, branded it ORS (United Payment System). The project offered low interchange fee rates and ORS branding across all ATMs; this was attractive to many banks which had been simply unable to bear the costs of expanding their fleets.

### p.287 — Russia: surcharging / interchange / IAD narrative (Russia Page 8)
- In 2017, Visa permitted surcharging cardholders for ATM cash withdrawals for the first time due to a change in its regulations which theoretically enabled Russian acquirers to surcharge cardholders of other banks. According to the regulations, surcharging is only permitted if applied to cards of all schemes acquired by the ATM deployer. This made it impossible in practice for acquirers to levy additional fees as all Russian ATMs are required to accept cards of the domestic scheme, **Mir**, which forbids surcharging.
- Individual Mastercard and Visa policies on interchange fees and surcharging are no longer relevant to the Russian ATM market given the schemes' suspension of operations in the country. The acquiring environment is now entirely managed by **NSPK**, with **UnionPay** the only major international scheme operational in the country.
- In February 2023, **VTB Bank and Otkritie Bank announced a sharing agreement** for their ATM networks (now part of one banking group), giving customers access to free withdrawals and deposits at the ATMs of either bank. A third bank, **Russian National Commercial Bank (RNKB)**, is set to be acquired by the VTB Group in 2023 and is also included in the partnership. RNKB operates only in Crimea (annexed by Russia from Ukraine in 2014, still recognised as part of Ukraine by most of the international community).
- **There are no IADs in the Russian market** due to legislation specifying that only licenced banks are allowed to deploy ATMs.

### p.289 — Russia: deployer narrative (Russia Page 10)
- With the acquisition of Otkritie Bank in December 2022, total **VTB Group ATMs rose to 25,160, up from 20,056** the previous year. **Bank Trust's 90 ATMs** (down from 100 at end-2021), previously part of the wider Otkritie Group, remained outside the VTB Group as the bank remained under CBRF ownership. Rosgosstrakhbank's ATMs were absorbed into the wider Otkritie Bank ATM network, but the bank performed a consolidation of its own ATM numbers overall with a reduction of 50 between end-2021 and end-2022.
- Of all Russian banks, **Sberbank** (also the largest ATM deployer by a significant measure) removed the most machines in 2022, **cutting 3,606 units (equal to 7% of its total fleet at end-2021)**. **Tinkoff Bank added the most new ATMs, increasing its fleet by 500**, part of its long-term expansion strategy and extension to new areas of Russia.

Narrative:
- Between 1995 and 2013, Russia's installed base grew rapidly and consistently, declining for the first time in 2014 due to an economic recession in the country amid falling oil prices and international sanctions. The depressed economic environment coupled with a mass revocation of banking licences by the CBRF caused ATM numbers to continue their decline, falling by small measures every year until 2018. In 2018 and 2019, the Russian installed base grew again, driven by expansion by Sberbank which started offering new services at its ATMs, including cash deposits, payments and transfers.
- The situation changed again in 2020, when ATM numbers fell by 5%; the sharpest decline since 2015, which brought the installed base to its lowest level since 2011. The contraction was caused by banks exiting the market (due to licence revocations or voluntary liquidation), as well as by major banks rationalising their fleets, including Sberbank which removed 4,000 machines, partly driven by COVID-19 and reduced demand for cash. Total ATM numbers declined again in 2021, falling to 120,745 machines, a contraction of 3%. Many banks pursued a strategy of reducing ATMs but enhancing the functionality of the ATMs that remain.
- 2022 saw another pronounced decline in ATM numbers — the installed base contracted by 5% to drop to 114,343 units. The sharp decrease was a symptom of the turbulent year for Russia's banking sector which saw banks sanctioned and western ATM manufacturers exit the market. While deployers remain able to use their existing western-made ATMs and these were not removed en masse, they struggled to access supplies of new machines, inhibiting both replenishments and expansion plans. Longer-term factors which predate the crisis, namely reduced cash usage and bank rationalisation projects, also played a role.

Body text: Greece's installed base showed marginal increase in 2022, rising 1% from the previous year. On a regional level, the country continues to rank highly when comparing its ATM installed base to the size of its economy and number of bank branches. Two-thirds of Greece's ATMs are off-site; the share is attributed to a combination of falling bank branches and high footfall locations such as commercial centres and hotels offering comparatively low-cost revenue streams, though it did fall slightly between 2021 and 2022. Most ATMs are connected by IBP's network central hardware and driven by ACI's BASE24-eps software. Vendor-native application software used across the majority of terminals, with NCR's NDC Enterprise being the most-used solution. All ATMs in Greece run on Windows 10 (consolidation onto a shared driver). Cash management/monitoring software run by KPMG; Trust is the most frequently used cash withdrawal/transaction management solution.

Body text: Greek banking sector is overseen by central bank, the Bank of Greece. The Hellenic Bank Association is responsible for representing the sector's interests. There are 35 credit institutions operating in the country, consisting of five major commercial banks, seven co-operatives, three specialist credit institutions, and 20 foreign-owned banks. The five commercial banks have the most extensive retail branch network in this group. Of the co-operative entities, only Pancreta Bank and Chania Bank operate more than a handful of branches. HSBC is the only foreign-owned bank with significant retail banking presence. Bank branch numbers fell to the thirteenth consecutive year in 2022, decreasing by 5% from 1,538 to 1,453. All major banks rationalised their networks this year, with Piraeus Bank and Eurobank undergoing the highest contractions in absolute numbers. This trend can be partially explained by the wave of mergers and acquisitions that took place in the banking sector since the sovereign debt crisis of 2010, which resulted in unprofitable banks consolidating to remain financially viable. The most recent driver for this trend, however, is the rising use of electronic payment methods and its spillover effect on cost efficiency of physical bank branches. The global financial crisis of 2007 and the Greek sovereign debt crisis of 2010 led to several bailouts from both the IMF and EU as well as a series of stringent austerity measures. A prerequisite of the bailouts was a consolidation of the Greek banking sector. The recapitalisation scheme supervised by Greece's international creditors at the beginning of the 2010s stipulated that Greek banks had to raise at least 10% of their own capital requirements to remain under private ownership. The four largest banks — Piraeus Bank, National Bank of Greece, Alpha Bank and Eurobank — had been recapitalised by 2013, with Eurobank relying entirely on the Hellenic Financial Stability Fund for recapitalisation. The Hellenic Financial Stability Fund was financed by Greece's international lenders to support the country's banking system. A series of mergers and acquisitions followed the reforms, with several institutions declared defunct. Larger banks were proactive in acquiring smaller players who were unable to meet the capital requirements, and these processes subsequently slowed the pace of the recapitalisation programme.

### p.324 — Greece banking structure continued (Greece: Page 4)
Body text only. The Syriza party was elected due to their resistance to the austerity measures imposed on Greece by foreign entities. In 2015, Greece became the first developed country to default on an IMF payment and for several years thereafter; capital controls and limits on the months number and value of cash withdrawals from ATMs were imposed. The first ban (off-site IADs) was made. The exchange of the four largest commercial banks were strict limits on the months number and value of cash withdrawals from banks. Following year, Greece entered the final phase of its bailout programme and in August it had ended that final phase. October 2018 saw the lifting of all restrictions on cash withdrawals for Greek customers holding domestic bank accounts. National Bank of Greece is the largest Greek bank by assets held, with EUR 78 billion (USD 93 billion) at end-2022. It is the second largest bank to acquire a third tranche and the second largest by ATM network. During the intensive consolidation of the Greek banking sector, the banking licence of the third largest bank (Attica) was revoked in 2022. Piraeus Bank held assets worth EUR 76 billion (USD 81 billion) at the end of 2022 and was Greece's largest bank by number of installed ATMs. It was the most active of the major commercial banks during the consolidation of the 2010s; using bank-controlled ATMs gained from the consolidation of the Greek banking sector during the early 2010s, including the highest-profile state-controlled ATEbank in 2013, gaining the majority of state-controlled commercial banks. Alpha Bank is the third largest bank by ATM fleet and the largest by branch network, holding assets worth EUR 59 billion (USD 63 billion) at the end of 2022. It was also active during the period of mergers and acquisitions, acquiring New Hellenic Postbank and Proton Bank from the four largest commercial banks. Eurobank is Greece's fourth largest commercial bank by ATM fleet and third largest by branch network, holding assets worth EUR 39 billion (USD 63 billion) at the end of 2022. It was also among the most active commercial banks during the 2010s consolidation, having acquired New Proton Bank and DTH Bank. Total assets in the Greek banking sector stood at EUR 327 billion (USD 351 billion) at the end of 2022, representing an annual fall of less than 1%. Assets are concentrated in the hands of the four largest commercial banks; Piraeus Bank, National Bank of Greece, Alpha Bank and Eurobank collectively accounted for 86% of the country total at end-2022.

### p.325 — Greece, Section 3: ATM networks, sharing and services; 3.1 ATM networks (Greece: Page 5)
Body text: Greece deployed its first ATM in 1981. This was relatively late compared to other western European markets and was initially slow to expand, with three deployers only collectively exceeding 100 ATMs in 1989. DIAS Interbanking Systems, founded by the Bank of Greece and other domestic credit institutions, was also established that year. Growth in Greece's installed base accelerated after this point, monitored by government initiatives such as allowing the withdrawal of pension funds through the DIAS network. There was a period of sustained growth between 1997 and the end of the 2000s. More than 2,000 ATMs had been installed by end-1997, rose to 5,000 by end-2003, and there was a final peak of almost 7,600 at end-2009. Economic crises — including the financial crisis and the sovereign debt crisis — in conjunction with subsequent austerity policies and bank sector consolidation led to a significant decline in the number of ATMs installed in the market. The installed base resumed in 2013 as it has waited again by a contraction in 2020. Most commercial banks operating in Greece have been DIAS network members since the late 1990s. As a result, all cardholders have been able to use any Greek ATM since 1999, and 13 of the DIAS network's 21 members were ATM deployers at the end of 2022. While co-operative banks are not considered full members, all cardholders can still access ATMs. DIAS previously offered members a full ATM management service called ATM-as-a-Service, available throughout ATM and bank cash management. They stopped offering this service in 2014 as a result of consolidation in the banking sector and DIAS now solely as an intermediary service provider for banks. It is Greece's only shared ATM network and covers 80% of the installed base at end-2022.

- DIAS: 5,733 | 5,836 | 5,867 | 5,839 | 5,817 | 0.4%
(Sources: DIAS, Greek ATM deployers and RBR analysis.)
Body text: The number of ATMs in the DIAS network has expanded at an average rate of under 1% between end-2018 and end-2022. In 2022, the small absolute increase in the number of DIAS network ATMs is solely driven by National Bank of Greece's fleet expansion as all other major banks reduced their ATM estates. Alpha Bank has a proprietary network called DeltaNET, which allows cardholders of other member banks to use Alpha Bank ATMs under the same conditions as Alpha Bank customers for balance enquiries, cash withdrawals, cash deposits, bill payments and funds transfers. At the end of 2021, HSBC remained the only other member of DeltaNET.

Body text: Greece's ATM market expanded by 1% by end-2022, maintaining the momentum seen in the previous year. The majority of this growth is driven by Euronet increasing its fleet size by 8%, which offset the consolidation conducted by most other ATM deployers; National Bank of Greece also expanded their estate this year. The expansion of ATM estates was predominantly limited to off-site locations in 2022, caused by major banks continuing to streamline their branch operations and deployers becoming more aware of the revenue potential off-site locations bring. COVID-19 and its resultant impact on tourism as well as the increasing usage of electronic payment methods have somewhat curtailed the realisation of this potential. Four of the five largest deployers maintained estates of at least 1,000 ATMs by end-2022. Eurobank reduced its fleet to just below this threshold at 996 ATMs. These deployers account for virtually all of Greece's installed base. The two largest deployers — Piraeus Bank and Euronet — account for 26% and 19% of the country's ATMs, respectively.

Body text: Greece's density of ATMs per million people remained at 699 at end-2022, broadly in line with western Europe's 691. Body text: Two-thirds of Greek ATMs were located away from bank branches at end-2022. The off-site share of the installed base has gradually increased in the tracked period, going from 58% in 2018 to 66% in 2022. Conversely, though the unit terminal base observed a slow decline in this time that has continued through to end-2022. There are two key drivers for these trends. Firstly, Euronet's presence in the Greek market has boosted the number of off-site terminals due to their high deployment rate in tourist areas. While some of these were temporarily closed in 2020 due to COVID-19, Euronet has expanded its fleet size since then and is still the largest deployer of off-site ATMs in Greece by some distance. Secondly, branch closures have resulted in pre-existing terminals being withdrawn in addition to reducing the number of possible locations for branch-based ATMs. This development has been accompanied by banks increasingly prioritising their off-site footprints to capitalise on the revenue potential of high footfall areas. Piraeus Bank, Alpha Bank, National Bank of Greece and Eurobank all increased the proportions of their ATM fleets away from bank branches during 2022.

### p.683 — Section 1 continued (market overview, technology & sharing)
Body text only. Key points:
- Nearly all ATMs in Turkey are fitted with anti-skimming devices; white two-thirds of terminals equipped with a push-in security shutter. Note-degradation tech found in a negligible proportion of Turkish ATMs; ~13% support cheque deposit. The vast majority of Turkish ATMs feature now-make-up half the installed base; ~85% of terminals support cassette/capability for visually impaired (audio guidance).
- NCR remained the largest supplier to the Turkish ATM market in 2022, retaining ~50% share; Diebold Nixdorf the next-largest; ~32% own NCR solutions.
- ~75% of Turkish ATMs are located off-site; majority of branch-based ATMs installed through lobbies.
- ~76% of ATMs run on Windows 10 operating system. Virtually all ATMs able to receive software remotely — under 1% of terminals use proprietary remote software-distribution software; ~92% application software, ~42% terminal-driving software.
- ~74% of Turkish installed base communicate via proprietary message protocol; nearly half of terminals have been upgraded to the Windows-10 operating system. Virtually all ATMs able to receive software remotely; second-line maintenance outsourced in most cases to the ATM manufacturer. Help-desk operations carried out in-house by the majority of deployers.
- The Turkish-installed ATM base is forecast to contract slightly during the forecast period to stand at 50,700 at the end of 2028. Whilst an increase in the rate of Turkey's bank deposits over the forecast period (~TRY 1.9 trillion / USD 137 billion) will boost likely to drive deployers to remove ATMs, will up to 20,000 ATMs to be pooled. Last surge in ATMs (~3,100 per year).

### p.685 — Section 2 continued (banking structure narrative)
Body text only:
- At end-2022 there were a total of **58 financial institutions** operating in Turkey, one higher than a year prior, as Türk Ticaret Bank restarted operations after liquidation in 2002. This figure includes 35 deposit banks, 17 development and investment banks, and 6 participation banks. Of the 35 deposit banks in the country, three were state-owned, eight were privately owned domestic banks, 21 were foreign banks, and three operated under the deposit insurance fund.
- At end-2022 total banking-sector assets in Turkey amounted to **TRY 14.3 trillion (USD 768 billion)**. Represents growth of 56% from the previous year in local currency, and a much-less-significant 12% increase when measured in US dollars. This is due to the continued high rates of inflation seen in Turkey and the depreciation of the Turkish lira. The state-owned Ziraat Bank was by far the largest financial institution by way of assets, with a total of TRY 2.1 trillion (USD 112 billion). This equates to 15% of the sector total. VakıfBank and Halkbank — the other two state-owned banks — were the second and fourth largest by assets, making up 12% and 9% of the country total respectively.
- The largest private bank in terms of assets was İşbank; the third-largest overall with TRY ~1.3 trillion (USD 70 billion). The fifth-largest bank — Garanti BBVA. The five largest banks accounted for roughly half of all banking-sector assets at end-2022.
- Spanish BBVA holds a 90% stake in Garanti BBVA; National Bank of Greece sold its ~76% stake in Finansbank to Qatar National Bank (QNB Finansbank — in 2016 whilst UAE's Emirates NBD completed the purchase of DenizBank in 2019. In April 2022 the Italian bank UniCredit completed the sale of its remaining stake in Yapı Kredi, the majority of its 20% share acquired by Turkey's Koç Holdings. Among the other foreign ventures present in the Turkish banking sector are ING from the Netherlands, the United Kingdom's HSBC, and China's ICBC.
- At end-2022 there were **10,997 bank branches** operating in Turkey, representing a fall of 1% from end-2021. İşbank and Garanti BBVA closed the most branches over the course of the year, shuttering 85 and 34 respectively. The three state-owned banks — Ziraat Bank, VakıfBank and Halkbank — all now boast more than 1,000 branches each. The number of branches operated by nearly 1,000 ATMs, ~5% of the installed base, shuttering ~85 branches. The five-largest deployers continued.
- The number of branches operated by participation banks increased by 5% to reach 1,362. Vakıf Katılım and Ziraat Katılım opened 21 and 25 new branches respectively, whilst Emlak Bank opened a further 8. At the end of 2022, participation banks accounted for 12% of branches operating in Turkey, unchanged from the end of 2021. Kuveyt Türk was the largest participation bank in terms of both assets and branch network size. At the end of 2022, it had a total of 443 branches.

### p.686 — Section 3: ATM networks, sharing and services + Figure 3.1 Domestic sharing agreements 2018-2022
3.1: ATM networks and sharing (text): First ATMs installed in Turkey in late 1980s by İşbank, Citibank and Yapı Kredi. Interbank Card Centre (BKM) set up in 1990 by a group of 13 public and private banks. A national ATM switch established in 1993 to settle interbank domestic payment-card authorisation, opening the ATM and POS networks of Turkish banks to customers, supporting Visa/Mastercard. In the 1990s two shared networks — Altın Nokta and Ortak Nokta — were established. İşbank and Ziraat Bank ran their own networks; in 2009 the shared networks and proprietary networks run by İşbank and Ziraat Bank were all united under a single network called **Ortak ATM**. As a result, all Turkish ATMs support transactions from cardholders of all connected banks.
Figure 3.1 "Domestic sharing agreements, 2018-2022" (Sharing agreement | 2018 | 2019 | 2020 | 2021 | 2022 | CAGR; Source: BKM, Turkish ATM deployers and RBR analysis):
- **Ortak ATM: 51,941 | 53,024 | 52,798 | 52,237 | 52,053 | CAGR 0.1%**
Text below table: Since the introduction of Ortak ATM there have been seven sharing agreements between banks, which offer cardholders ATM usage either free of charge, on a reduced fee, or as part of larger agreements. The largest of these sharing agreements involves İşbank, the majority shareholder in TEB, Qatar National Bank-acquired Finansbank — now QNB Finansbank — in 2016, whilst the UAE's Emirates NBD completed the purchase of DenizBank in 2019. In January 2021, the state-owned banks and PTT announced their intention to combine their ATMs under a single brand in order to cut operating costs. At end of 2022, ~3.5 million machines with a shared brand of beginning. As a result, they have been able to rationalise their estates and cut costs while ensuring their customers continue to be provided with access to free or low-cost ATM transactions.
- In January 2021, ING Bank also signed a sharing agreement with İşbank and Akbank, which allows their customers to make transactions at the banks' ATMs free of charge. This has given ING Bank the ability to ration its estate; it removed over 100 ATMs in 2022.
- The Turkish postal service — PTT — also deploys a significant number of ATMs, playing an important role in improving access to financial services in the country.

### p.689 — Section 3 continued (deployer fleet changes) + 3.2 ATM Pooling
Body text only:
- Total number of ATMs in Turkey fell by **184**, standing at **52,053** at end-2022. In absolute terms, İşBank's fleet saw the most significant decline, **315 fewer** machines at end-2022 vs end-2021. The only other banks to remove over 100 ATMs were ING Bank and Şekerbank, as the former continued to rationalise its fleet following the sharing agreement made with İşbank and Akbank in 2021.
- **Akbank added the most ATMs in 2022, +194** over the year. Both QNB Finansbank and Yapı Kredi also added over 100 terminals to their respective fleets in 2022.
- **3.2 ATM Pooling:** Various ATM sharing agreements allow cardholders to use other banks' machines at reduced cost or free. In January 2021 first plans put in place for a formal ATM pooling project, with Turkish state-owned deployers — Ziraat Bank, VakıfBank, Halkbank, Vakıf Katılım, Ziraat Katılım, Emlak Bank, and PTT — confirming intention to launch a joint ATM brand on common software.
- Throughout 2021 and 2022 banks piloted the scheme; at end-2022 roughly **1,500 terminals** were operating under **TAM (ATM Centre of Turkey)**. Over coming years ATMs currently deployed by these individual banks expected to complete migration to this pooling agreement — likely **~20,000 ATMs**, making up **over a third** of the Turkish installed base.

Text: In recent years the growth seen in the Turkish ATM installed base has stalled, and the total number of terminals in the country has declined in each of the past three years. The market reached its peak in 2019, when the number of machines rose to above 53,000. Since then, the market has contracted — albeit at an average of under 1% per year — to stand at a total of 52,053 at the end of 2022.
- In 2022, İşBank — the country's second-largest ATM deployer — removed by far the most terminals of any Turkish bank, as its fleet shrunk by 315. As well as this, ING Bank was again sought to rationalise its estate, removing 131 ATMs from service. Whilst the two banks have reduced the number of proprietary ATMs they operate, they have both significantly increased their reach in recent years, following the three-way agreement made between the two banks and Akbank at the beginning of 2021. As a result, they have been able to rationalise their estates and cut costs, while ensuring their customers continue to be provided with access to free or low-cost ATM transactions.
- Şekerbank removed the next highest number of ATMs, removing 106 machines throughout the year. The stalling growth in the installed base of ATMs in Turkey has been driven by a number of factors. Firstly, the rise of digital banking, which has been encouraged by several major banks in the country, has led to branch closures and the subsequent removal of branch-based ATMs. As well as this, the increase in the number of payments made using cards and digital channels has led to reduced cash demand and fewer cash withdrawals. This trend was accelerated by the emergence of the COVID-19 pandemic, which drove down cash usage as consumers were driven towards seemingly more hygienic payment methods.

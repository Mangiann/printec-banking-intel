# Banking Market Research Source Map

**Purpose:** A compact but complete reference for doing reliable market research when the goal is to understand what banks are working on, where they are spending money, and what they may need in the next 6–12 months — especially across Greece, Eastern Europe, SEE/CEE, and countries where Printec Group operates.

**Source basis:** This document reorganizes the useful content from `Pasted text(1).txt`. It removes interrupted fragments and UI/meta noise, and keeps the research guidance, source hierarchy, tools, and workflows.

---

## 1. Core Principle: Triangulation

Reliable market research depends on **triangulation**.

No single source is trustworthy enough on its own. The most dependable approach combines:

- **Primary research**: data you collect yourself.
- **Secondary research**: existing data from reports, filings, public databases, statistics, news, and disclosures.
- **Qualitative insight**: why people or organizations behave as they do.
- **Quantitative validation**: how common or financially meaningful a signal is.

A finding becomes more reliable when **three independent methods or sources point in the same direction**.

For banking research, triangulation should combine:

1. Bank disclosures and financial reports.
2. Public procurement and tender activity.
3. Regulatory deadlines and policy pressure.
4. Job postings and hiring trends.
5. Vendor/competitor announcements.
6. Central bank and payment statistics.
7. Analyst or specialist market intelligence.

## 2. General Market Research Methods

### 2.1 Customer Interviews

Customer interviews are usually the strongest way to understand **why** people behave as they do.

Key rules:

- Ask about **past behavior**, not hypothetical intent.
- Avoid questions like: “Would you buy this?”
- Ask instead:
  - What do you currently do?
  - What did you last pay for?
  - What frustrated you recently?
  - What workaround are you using today?
- Do not lead the respondent toward your preferred answer.

Useful framework:

- **The Mom Test** by Rob Fitzpatrick.
- Main idea: do not pitch your idea; investigate the problem and the customer’s real past behavior.

For this banking project, direct bank interviews are not available, so interviews must be replaced by indirect evidence.

---

### 2.2 Surveys

Surveys are useful for quantifying what interviews or desk research uncover.

Good uses:

- Measuring how common a pain point is.
- Segmenting the market.
- Testing willingness to pay.
- Validating whether a pattern is widespread.

Reliability depends heavily on sampling:

- A small survey of the true target segment is better than a large random sample.
- Keep surveys short, ideally under 10 minutes.
- Avoid leading questions.
- Add attention checks to filter low-quality responses.

Common tools:

- SurveyMonkey
- Typeform
- Google Forms
- Prolific
- Pollfish

In this banking project, surveys are not the main method because banks cannot be asked directly.

## 3. How AI Should Be Used

AI is useful for **research acceleration**, not as ground truth.

### 3.1 Where AI Helps

AI is strong at:

- Summarizing large volumes of text.
- Extracting recurring themes from reviews, reports, transcripts, and forums.
- Coding open-ended responses into categories.
- Drafting interview guides.
- Drafting unbiased survey questions.
- Analyzing transcripts.
- Building competitor landscapes.
- Creating comparison tables.
- Producing search strategies.
- Synthesizing desk research.
- Identifying patterns across earnings calls, annual reports, procurement notices, and press releases.

For banking research, AI can process:

- Investor presentations.
- Earnings-call transcripts.
- Tender documents.
- Regulatory calendars.
- Central-bank reports.
- Vendor announcements.
- Job postings.
- News articles.
- Analyst-report excerpts.

---

### 3.2 Where AI Should Not Be Trusted

AI should not be used as the data source itself.

Do not rely on AI for:

- Synthetic customer answers.
- Unverified market-size figures.
- Claimed budgets without source links.
- Claimed project timelines without evidence.
- “Likely bank needs” unless supported by signals.

Rule of thumb from the analysis:

> AI may handle 60–70% of the research labor, but 0% of the ground truth.

All factual claims should be traced back to verifiable sources.

## 4. Useful Research Frameworks

### 4.1 TAM / SAM / SOM

Use for market sizing.

Important rule:

- Prefer **bottom-up sizing**.
- Avoid top-down claims like “1% of a giant market.”

Bottom-up example structure:

```text
Number of realistic customers × realistic annual price = serviceable revenue opportunity
```

For banking technology:

```text
Number of banks / branches / ATMs / POS estates / compliance programs × realistic vendor spend = opportunity estimate
```

---

### 4.2 Jobs-to-be-Done

Use to understand what the bank is trying to accomplish.

Examples:

- Reduce branch operating cost.
- Modernize ATM/self-service channels.
- Comply with new regulation.
- Reduce cyber/operational risk.
- Improve card/payment processing.
- Launch instant payments.
- Improve digital onboarding.
- Outsource non-core operations.
- Reduce cash-handling complexity.

---

### 4.3 Porter’s Five Forces

Use when evaluating competition and market attractiveness.

Relevant questions:

- How strong are incumbent vendors?
- Are banks switching vendors or consolidating suppliers?
- Are fintechs entering the space?
- Are banks building in-house?
- Are regulations increasing or reducing vendor dependency?
- Is pricing pressure increasing?

---

### 4.4 Falsifiable Hypotheses

Before researching, define a testable hypothesis.

Example:

```text
Greek banks will increase spending on ATM/self-service accessibility upgrades in the next 6–12 months because of regulatory pressure and branch transformation programs.
```

Then define what evidence would support or weaken the hypothesis.

Good evidence:

- Tender notices.
- Budget allocation in investor materials.
- Hiring for relevant roles.
- Regulatory deadlines.
- Vendor contracts.
- Branch/ATM statistics.

Bad evidence:

- Generic articles.
- AI-generated assumptions.
- Unsourced “market trend” claims.

This prevents confirmation bias.

## 7. Evidence Hierarchy for Banking Market Research

Use this source hierarchy from strongest to weakest evidence.

---

### Layer 1: Hard Evidence — Where the Money Shows Up

#### 7.1 Annual Reports and Investor Presentations

These are the strongest starting sources.

Why they matter:

- They are public and official.
- Listed banks disclose financial performance, strategy, capex, cost targets, and transformation programs.
- Strategy sections often name multi-year programs.
- Investor presentations may include digital transformation budgets, branch reduction, IT spend, cost-to-income targets, and channel strategy.

Banks mentioned in the analysis:

- Alpha Bank
- Eurobank
- National Bank of Greece / NBG
- Piraeus Bank
- Raiffeisen
- Erste
- OTP
- KBC
- UniCredit subsidiaries

What to look for:

- IT spend.
- Capex.
- Opex efficiency.
- Digital transformation.
- Branch optimization.
- ATM strategy.
- Cost-to-income improvement.
- Payments modernization.
- Outsourcing.
- Risk/compliance investments.
- Multi-year transformation programs.

Why this is high reliability:

- Audited or official.
- Numerical.
- Often forward-looking.
- Usually repeated across quarterly updates.

---

#### 7.2 Earnings Call Transcripts

Earnings calls reveal what executives discuss beyond the slides.

Why they matter:

- Analysts ask direct questions about IT spend, costs, branch strategy, and transformation.
- Executives often clarify priorities and timelines.
- Transcripts may contain more candid language than reports.

Where to find them:

- Bank investor relations pages.
- AlphaSense.
- Other financial transcript databases.

Use case:

```text
Search for every mention of ATM outsourcing, branch transformation, IT spend, DORA, instant payments, or cash handling in CEE bank transcripts from the last 12 months.
```

Professional tool mentioned:

- AlphaSense.

---

#### 7.3 Public Procurement and Tender Databases

Procurement databases are especially important in this region.

Why they matter:

- Public institutions, central banks, state-owned banks, postal banks, and partly state-linked entities often publish tenders.
- A tender is a direct signal of future spending.
- It often contains budget, scope, deadlines, eligibility criteria, technical requirements, and vendor categories.

EU and national sources mentioned:

- TED / Tenders Electronic Daily.
- Greece: ΚΗΜΔΗΣ / Diavgeia.
- Romania: SEAP / SICAP.
- Bulgaria: CAIS EOP.
- Serbia: Portal javnih nabavki.
- Other local equivalents by country.

Search dimensions:

- Country.
- Buyer/institution.
- CPV code.
- Banking machinery.
- Payment systems.
- IT services.
- Self-service terminals.
- ATM equipment.
- Maintenance and managed services.
- Security and compliance systems.

Why this is high reliability:

- It is direct evidence of planned spending.
- It often describes exactly what the institution wants to buy.
- It is usually time-bound within the next 6–12 months.

---

#### 7.4 Central Banks and Banking Associations

Central banks and banking associations provide market-level data.

Examples mentioned:

- Bank of Greece.
- ECB Statistical Data Warehouse.
- Hellenic Bank Association.
- Equivalent national central banks and banking associations in each target country.

Useful data:

- Card transaction volumes.
- ATM network size.
- POS network size.
- Cash usage trends.
- Payment instrument adoption.
- Digital banking usage.
- Instant payments adoption.
- Country-level payment statistics.

Why it matters for Printec-type research:

- ATM/POS growth or decline is a direct infrastructure signal.
- Cash usage trends influence cash automation and ATM strategy.
- Payments growth signals demand for acquiring, switching, processing, and related systems.

## 11. Confidence Scoring

Use a simple High / Medium / Low confidence score.

### High Confidence

Multiple strong sources agree, for example:

- Investor presentation mentions the project.
- Tender is published.
- Bank is hiring relevant roles.
- Regulatory deadline supports the timing.

### Medium Confidence

Some evidence exists, but it is incomplete:

- Strategy presentation mentions a theme.
- Vendor says demand is increasing.
- Job postings suggest activity.
- No direct budget or tender found yet.

### Low Confidence

The signal is interesting but weak:

- News article mentions a broad trend.
- AI synthesis suggests a possibility.
- Competitor activity implies but does not prove bank intent.
- No direct bank or procurement evidence.

## 13. Key Research Rules

1. **Do not produce generic market summaries.** Always connect each signal to a possible banking need, vendor opportunity, budget direction, or likely 6–12 month action.
2. **Never treat one source as enough.** Triangulate with at least two or three source types where possible.
3. **Never invent numbers.** If a budget, market size, or deadline is not sourced, mark it as unknown.
4. **Prefer direct evidence.** Tenders, annual reports, investor presentations, central bank data, and regulatory deadlines are stronger than news commentary.
5. **Use AI only for synthesis.** AI should process evidence, not create evidence.
6. **Keep dates.** Every finding should include source date or publication date.
7. **Separate facts from implications.** Facts are what the source says; implications are your interpretation.
8. **Score confidence.** Use High / Medium / Low.
9. **Watch regulatory pressure.** Banking spend is often forced by regulation.
10. **Watch hiring.** Hiring often reveals projects before launch.
11. **Watch vendors.** Vendor earnings and contract wins reveal demand signals.
12. **Start free.** Use investor presentations, procurement portals, central-bank data, and regulatory calendars before buying expensive reports.

*Printec footprint (17 markets) · prepared by the Orchestrator from agents 1–6 and 8*

## Bottom line up front

**Raiffeisen won Addiko, not NLB — so a year of pre-positioning for a five-market integration is pointed at the wrong door, and the decision this forces is whether we work Vienna or work the loser's freed budget.** Raiffeisen Bank International secured 56.16% of the shares tendered to it on 29 July, above the 55% threshold it had been allowed to lower; NLB, which bid €37.00 a share, had only 28.9% of the capital committed nine days earlier and could not close the gap. The integration will now be procured against Raiffeisen's group vendor frameworks in Vienna, which pushes our realistic scope toward decommissioning, migration and site services rather than the strategic platform. The counterweight is that NLB is freed: it will not absorb Addiko Serbia, its growth stays organic, and its own group ATM and cash-deposit tenders were already stated as finalised in 2025 — so the single question that decides whether Serbia and Kosovo are call-off business or closed accounts is whether we sit on that framework. Both banks report half-year results within days of each other in early August.

**Second, and commercially larger: for the first time we can build the cash-recycling case from the banks' own published device data rather than from market averages.** This cycle's disclosure sweep pulled live "find an ATM" feeds across eleven markets in the last days of July and produced per-bank deposit-capability splits that had never existed before — roughly 1,840 cash-out-only sites across Bulgaria's four largest banks, 957 of 1,917 machines in the Czech shared network, 467 of 666 at Erste Croatia, 457 of 568 at Serbia's AikBank. A bank can dismiss a market average. It cannot dismiss its own locator.

## Top 3 moves of the week

Ranked by **newest development first**, restricted to genuinely winnable net-new contests. Deal-size bands are context, not a score, and nothing here estimates our chance of winning anything — that is the sales teams' judgement.

**1 · Bulgaria's four largest banks — the deposit gap, measured from their own data**
*Target:* UBB, DSK, Postbank and UniCredit Bulbank · *Owner:* Bulgaria country lead with Group cash-automation · *By when:* proposals in front of all four before the 1 January 2027 payment deadline, while the euro-changeover project teams are still standing · *The one decision:* whether we lead with one reusable business-case template built on each bank's own published volumes, or four separate country pitches · *Band:* XL

UBB publishes 738 ATM sites of which only 246 take a deposit, and published itself that BGN 406m came in through deposit ATMs against nearly BGN 2.3bn over branch counters in the first five months of euro operation. DSK is starker: just 7 of its 441 standalone ATM sites accept cash. Nationally, €16.4bn a half-year still crosses branch counters. The changeover work is finished, which frees 2026-27 budget, and a statutory duty to keep exchanging leva until 31 December 2026 forces each bank to make a documented decision before then.

**2 · The Czech four-bank shared ATM network — 957 machines that cannot take a deposit**
*Target:* the Komerční banka / Moneta / Air Bank / UniCredit scheme, approached jointly rather than bank by bank · *Owner:* Czech country lead · *By when:* engage before a fifth bank joins either scheme and the platform choice hardens · *The one decision:* whether we sell to the scheme layer (routing, switching, reconciliation, wallet-deposit application) or only to the member banks · *Band:* XL

The pool is over 1,900 machines and growing by joiners — UniCredit added 144 deposit ATMs in February and the network moved 155,000 deposits worth nearly CZK 2bn in January alone — yet roughly half of it still cannot accept cash. We separately established this cycle that Czechia has **no public tender route to ATM business at all**, so this is bought commercially or not at all. One caution: nobody yet knows how many of the ~960 deposit-capable machines genuinely recycle rather than merely accept, and that number decides whether this is an upgrade sale or an installation sale.

**3 · Erste Croatia — 467 withdrawal-only ATMs against a statutory free-withdrawal right**
*Target:* Erste & Steiermärkische Bank · *Owner:* Croatia country lead · *By when:* before the 1 January 2027 interbank obligation, and before the Erste group procurement framework closes the local route · *The one decision:* whether Croatia is a local sale or has to be won through Erste group procurement in Vienna · *Band:* XL

Erste Croatia's own locator returned 666 ATMs on 31 July, only 199 of which accept deposits, plus 97 branches still running a cash desk. From 1 January 2027 holders of Croatia's free basic account get two free monthly withdrawals at **any** bank's machine, and the banks are still negotiating who pays for that. A useful correction sits underneath: the law does not actually force ATM deposit — a bank can comply with tellers only — so the argument is cost, not compliance. Coin handling is expressly carved out of the free package, which makes coin machines a chargeable product in a market with 41 of them installed nationally.

*Two more of the same shape are a day behind and belong in the same campaign, not a separate one:* **BCR Romania's ~780 off-site ATMs** (30 July, XL — the one large unaligned off-site block in a market where Euronet already fills that role for two top-six banks), and **AikBank Serbia's 457 dispense-only machines** (29 July, XL — at a bank converting cash desks roughly once a quarter and refurbishing a branch a fortnight).

## The fuller opportunity list, in plain language

**Ukraine — the tender we have been hunting for two cycles exists, and we were looking in the wrong place.** PrivatBank's bulk recycler purchase is live on **the bank's own procurement platform, not Prozorro**, which is why weekly Prozorro polling never found it. It is for **1,000 machines** — 700 lobby and 300 through-the-wall — not the 800 a board member mentioned to the press, and it has been sitting at the qualification stage since 8 April with no award and no auction date. Two things matter commercially. The specification mandates integration with Diebold Nixdorf's own monitoring layer, and that single clause may decide the whole lot. And no contract was ever published for the bank's 2024 recycler award, so the incumbency we have assumed on the previous round is genuinely unknown — it rests only on a 2025 contract for different machines. Separately, the central bank introduces a new 2,000-hryvnia banknote on 4 September and has admitted the country's ~38,700 acceptance devices will not be ready until December: that is a four-month field-services race, not a deadline.

**Bosnia & Herzegovina — a legal deadline nobody is discussing.** The central bank's rulebook of 10 July makes instant-payment participation **compulsory from 1 January 2027** for any bank that ran a million or more transactions in the first half of this year, with a rolling trigger catching more each year. The rail went live on 20 July with **three banks connected** out of roughly twenty-two, and a fourth joined on 28 July. The rulebook also permits **no initiation channel except a mobile or electronic platform**, so a bank without a working app cannot comply at all. Three-quarters of the country's banking assets sit at institutions that must be live in under six months.

**The supervisor is about to hand us an inventory.** The ECB wrote to every significant institution on 7 July requiring an action plan on AI-enabled cyber threats **filed with each bank's supervisory team by 31 October**, with structural measures that name "updating legacy, unsupported or end-of-life technologies" in terms. That obliges twenty-one named banks across Greece, Cyprus, Austria, Croatia, Slovenia, Slovakia and Bulgaria to write down their own end-of-life technology and commit to a timeline the supervisor then monitors. Ageing self-service estates land inside that inventory whether or not anyone set out to review them. The plans are being drafted now, in August and September.

**Greece — the regulator has put a price on weak transaction monitoring.** The Bank of Greece published its supervision report on 28 July disclosing €2.27m of fines in 2025, of which **€1.89m fell on a single unnamed bank across thirteen anti-money-laundering breaches**, with findings naming weaknesses in the systems used to monitor customer transactions — and it named intensified inspection of exactly those systems as a 2026 priority. Separately, Piraeus is rolling out in-branch appointment and wayfinding kiosks across 368 branches **with no disclosed supplier**, on an IT budget that has stepped up about 40% to roughly €140m a year.

**Hungary — a regulator ordering banks to add cash capacity, which is the opposite of everywhere else.** K&H was fined HUF 60m in December for missing earlier obligations and has been ordered to open additional staffed teller positions in named branches, extend online appointment booking for cash transactions to every branch with a teller, and file **monthly statistically-tested waiting-time reports**. MBH, meanwhile, is the one Hungarian bank buying ahead of the mandate rather than under enforcement: it has named 115 further locations for 2026 on top of a ~500-unit replacement cycle.

**Slovenia — the state is about to put a floor under the ATM estate.** A draft act published 17 July would require an ATM within 5km of every settlement above 1,000 people, 95% of the population within 5km by road, and **at least 60 machines per 100,000 inhabitants**, with the central bank able to order a named bank to install one. The estate is meanwhile shrinking by about sixteen machines a quarter, and the contactless-capable count has not moved at all in two quarters — banks are decommissioning, not upgrading.

**Kosovo, Albania, North Macedonia — one shared date, with an important correction.** The November 2026 instant-payment onboarding window brings in the **central banks** of those three countries, not their commercial banks; no national timetable for the banks themselves has been published. Kosovo is nonetheless already taking every bank through file-format, transaction-limit and message-standard harmonisation, which is where payment-hub, real-time screening and key-management work gets bought. Albania separately has an all-business card-acceptance deadline on 31 December and a draft cap on card interchange that would squeeze acquiring margin in exactly the year banks must deploy more terminals.

**Cyprus — an integration with a hard 2027 anchor and nothing locked in.** Eleven months after the legal merger, Eurobank and Hellenic still run two core systems, two apps and two self-service platforms, with full technical integration targeted for **April 2027** and €150m of Cyprus technology investment behind it. Roughly 156 ATMs have to be harmonised, cards reissued and accessibility gaps closed — and the bank's own annual report concedes only *some* of its Cypriot machines have accessibility features, against 100% voice guidance on its Greek fleet.

## Gaps, and what to use instead

**This picture is not complete, and two markets are effectively dark.**

**(a) Source agents that did not produce a full fresh file this cycle.** Agent 8, the Futurist, **was not re-run at all** and its 3–5 year layer rests on 15 July. Agent 5 (jobs and leadership) covered **Greece only — one market of seventeen**, so sixteen markets have no hiring or leadership signal. Agent 6 (vendors) covered **three competitors only**, so Payten/Asseco, Worldline, Diebold Nixdorf, NCR Atleos, SelfPay, Netopia and Datecs got no dedicated research and everything reported on them arrived as a by-product of the bank-disclosure agent. Agent 4 (statistics) covers non-EU markets only, so ten EU markets have no regulator-statistics output, and the engine reports that the newest statistics file carries no country-trends table and fell back to **15 July** figures.

**(b) Unit-level coverage warnings inherited from the collectors.** Agent 1 emitted an explicit warning that **8 of 42 banks returned nothing** — cut off by system limits rather than researched and found quiet. Named: **Addiko Group, Erste Group, Raiffeisen Group, KBC Group, ProCredit Holding, the combined OTP / Intesa / NLB / UniCredit group unit, every bank in Montenegro, and every bank in North Macedonia.** The consequences are specific: Montenegro and North Macedonia have **zero fresh bank evidence** and their rows rest on statistics and regulation alone; no country-level fleet figure anywhere in this cycle has been checked against group-level disclosure; and there is a real irony in the fact that the cycle's biggest event — Raiffeisen taking Addiko — was captured only as a by-product of three country shards, because both the Raiffeisen and Addiko group units came back empty.

**(c) Markets we cannot monitor at all from outside.** Kosovo's national procurement portal has **no public notice search** — every path requires a registered supplier login — and Albania's is login-only too, which means any public-sector card-acceptance lots created by its May obligation are invisible to us. In both cases the fix is an administrative one: open a supplier account. Slovenská pošta's own procurement portal is likewise unreadable. A long list of primary sources was blocked this cycle — SWIFT's own site for the third consecutive run, the North Macedonian and Kosovan central banks behind bot protection, and the Austrian, Hungarian, Albanian, Cypriot and Greek regulator pages variously returning errors.

**(d) Next-best proxies where a primary was unreachable.** Where the Bank of Greece statistics page failed, the audited bank annual report carried the fleet figures. Where central-bank tables were bot-blocked in North Macedonia, local-language press reporting the same release stood in — but only as percentages, not absolute numbers, which is why an operator with a browser should recover those two tables. Where a national tender portal is closed, the entity's own register worked: both genuinely actionable Kosovan findings this cycle came from the central bank's and a commercial bank's own websites, not from the portal.

**(e) What the independent validator caught, and what it did not reach.** A separate audit pass re-read every forward call against the documents it cites. It cleared roughly half outright and flagged the rest as thin, and several of its catches were real: it found that my own "roughly 1,500 dispense-only sites" in Bulgaria was arithmetically wrong — the four banks' own figures sum to about **1,840** — which is now corrected here and in the table. It also found that the claim a rival holds AikBank's onboarding stack is not supported by any cited document; that the Verification-of-Payee deadline for non-euro markets is 9 July 2027, not the 9 January date that governs receiving instant transfers; that only six Romanian banks carry the full card-scheme merchant set, not "every other large bank"; that the Greek fee-free all-bank ATM concept is a plan provision reported by one regional outlet, not a running pilot; and that Albania and Ukraine **do** appear in the international payments-standards assessment, contrary to a claim that they were absent. Each of those is now caveated on the card itself. **The audit reached 34 of 59 calls — 25 fall back to my own self-report**, so those carry less assurance than the rest.

**(f) One honest limitation on our own process.** The brief asks the Orchestrator to personally re-open a primary source and confirm the key figure on every High-confidence row before it is called decision-grade. With sixty High-confidence rows this cycle, that was not done row by row at this level. What was done is that each country shard verified its claims adversarially against primaries and flagged what it could not confirm — and every such figure is marked in the row itself or in the contradictions table. Treat the flagged ones as unconfirmed until an operator closes them.

## Language coverage

Worth recording because it changed outcomes this week: the research was genuinely conducted in local languages, and three of the most valuable findings would have been invisible otherwise. The Ukrainian-language sweep of PrivatBank's own procurement platform found the 1,000-unit tender that two cycles of English-language Prozorro polling had missed. The Bosnian-language central-bank rulebook carried the compulsory participation date. The Albanian-language drafts carried the interchange caps and the resilience regulation. The previous cycle's flag that Bosnian-language searching was not evidenced **no longer holds**.

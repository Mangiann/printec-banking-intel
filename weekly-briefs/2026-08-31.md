## Bottom line up front

**Our most important supplier is about to become a competitor's property in the same fortnight that every supervised bank in the footprint must write down, for its regulator, which of its technologies are obsolete. Those two clocks are nine days apart, and together they are the single best selling window of the year.** The UK competition authority opened its review of Brink's purchase of NCR Atleos on 26 August with a decision due 22 October; both companies have pulled the expected completion forward to early in the first quarter of 2027. On 31 October, every large bank in Greece, Cyprus, Austria, Croatia, Slovenia, Slovakia and Bulgaria must file a plan with its supervisor naming its "legacy, unsupported or end-of-life technologies" — wording that lands squarely on ageing cash machines. A bank writing that plan in October has to decide what to do about an ageing fleet at exactly the moment the fleet's principal manufacturer is changing hands. The decision this forces on us is whether we go to those banks in the next six weeks with a deliberately vendor-neutral proposition — assess the estate, commit to a supervisor-credible timeline, buy nothing yet — or wait for the merger outcome and arrive after the plans are filed. A rival is already there with the opposite argument: KAL is selling banks software that keeps old machines alive so they never need replacing.

**Second, we were wrong last week about Addiko, and being wrong makes the opportunity bigger rather than smaller.** We reported that Raiffeisen's win moved a five-market integration to Vienna. In fact Raiffeisen keeps only Croatia, Slovenia and Austria. The Serbian, both Bosnian and the Montenegrin banks are being sold on to **Alta Group of Belgrade** — a first-time owner assembling six banks across four jurisdictions, with no group vendor framework to fall back on and a contractual deadline to build one, because shared services from Addiko expire twelve months after closing with a single twelve-month extension. Any pitch built on a combined Raiffeisen-plus-Addiko Serbian estate must be withdrawn today.

## The fuller opportunity list, in plain language

**Hungary has made shrinking an ATM fleet illegal, and nobody has noticed what that means.** Act XIV of 2026, in force since 14 May, bars banks from decommissioning any existing cash machine or branch cash-payout point — while expressly permitting **replacement**. Everywhere else in the footprint the commercial question is how fast a bank can shrink. Hungary has converted its entire existing estate — commonly put at around 5,700 machines, though the independent validator notes no cited document carries a verified national total, and the central bank's annual payments report has not published — **from a declining estate into a protected replacement base**, on top of the 1,039 new rural sites due by 31 December. The clause worth reading twice is subsection (1b), which concedes that a replacement machine may not physically fit its predecessor's site because of its dimensions and security requirements — the legislator has effectively written a site-survey and relocation workstream into national law. Hungarian banks cannot staff this: only two of sixteen nationwide "ATM" job adverts sit at banks, so the work must be bought in.

**Cyprus has produced the strongest argument for cash automation anyone has published this year.** Eurobank disclosed on 6 August that **almost 90% of deposits and 95% of withdrawals now go through machines rather than branch counters**, on a 156-machine fleet that is the island's largest — and which is not ours. It has to be standardised before the April 2027 core-system cutover. A bank can argue with a market average; it cannot argue with its own disclosure.

**Slovakia has a 600-machine network that cannot take a deposit, and everyone involved knows it.** KBC completed its €708m purchase of 365.bank, and ČSOB Slovakia has already merged around 600 machines with 365.bank and Poštová banka into a shared network live since 1 July — **withdrawal-only, with deposit interoperability explicitly unsolved**. Across the market all five large banks are dismantling branch cash desks, with roughly a thousand deposit machines now run by seven banks. The unsolved problem is the opening.

**Bulgaria's Verification-of-Payee deadline stopped being a legal argument and became a hardware problem.** We carried this last week as resting on a contested legal basis. It no longer does: the 1 January 2027 date traces to the Official Journal text itself, and **recital 11 names ATMs and self-service terminals among the payment channels in scope**. Every Bulgarian bank must be able to check a payee's name on transfers started at its machines, not only in its app, with four months to run.

**The Western Balkans are going instant on four different calendars, and one of them has an unusually open door.** Montenegro went live at all eleven banks simultaneously on 20 July, with a compulsory free payee check, ten-second irrevocable settlement and a regulator-imposed five-cent fee cap on transfers below €200 — with a QR, alias and request-to-pay phase dated to March 2027. Albania's go-live slipped from July to around November, and that slip is itself the selling window because the banks are behind. Kosovo's central bank has bought its core — **Nexi won it at €685,000 after SWIFT's 56%-cheaper bid was ruled non-responsive for missing forms** — but the bank-side work across all seven commercial banks is untouched. North Macedonia faces **two rails at once**, and its central bank's own connectivity tender closes on **18 September on a second attempt**, the first having been cancelled for want of any acceptable bid.

**Albania has a regulatory gate we are uniquely placed to open.** Printec is the named distributor for both of the only two certified customer-operated banknote machines in the country — but **neither carries fitness certification**, which means no customer-operated machine in Albania can legally recycle lek. That is a submittable application, not a sales problem.

**Czechia: we told you last week there was no public tender route to ATM business there. That was wrong.** Agent 2 walked 450 procedures on the central bank's own procurement register and found it publishes cash-machinery tenders openly — Giesecke+Devrient won CZK 109m of coin technology, KMX BT Praha holds sorter maintenance. The right place to watch is **ezak.cnb.cz**, not the EU-level portals. Separately, ČSOB is standing up an international card-acquiring platform, having posted five acquiring roles in August — a third of every acquiring vacancy in the Czech market — which is a new buying centre at the country's second bank with a narrow window before vendor choices freeze.

## Gaps, and what to use instead

**(a) One source agent did not run.** **Agent 8, the Futurist, was not re-run this cycle** and its 3–5 year layer still rests on 15 July — six weeks stale. Nothing in this brief depends on it, but the dashboard's long-horizon tab is carrying an ageing view, and one derived play that rested on it (a euro-stablecoin consortium item) has been retired for want of independent confirmation.

**(b) A structural coverage limit, which is not the same as a miss.** Agent 4 researches only the seven non-EU markets by design, because EU counts come from a script reading the European Central Bank's data portal. The practical consequence is that **Austria, Bulgaria, Croatia, Cyprus, Czechia, Hungary, Romania and Slovenia have no researched national statistics this cycle** — their figures arrived either from the script or second-hand through bank disclosures. This caps confidence on several rows. The worst case is Czechia, where **the only national ATM count in evidence comes from a vendor's own marketing blog**, and Hungary, where the central bank's annual payments report has not published, so there is no refreshed national baseline at all. Austria is thinnest of all: agents 4 and 5 both filed nothing, so there are no Austrian market statistics and no hiring or leadership signal, which is why only two Austrian rows reach High confidence.

**(c) No collector flagged an empty unit this cycle** — a genuine improvement on last cycle's eight-bank warning. Agent 4's run was interrupted by a usage limit on 27 August, but it resumed from its checkpoints and finished on 31 August covering all seven countries with zero empty units.

**(d) Two access blockers, both now diagnosed and both fixable.** The **European Payments Council's website is behind bot protection that defeated every route**, which means **five scheme dates are unverified this cycle — including the 20 September payee-verification rulebook deadline**. It is still in the regulatory calendar, flagged, but nobody should quote it to a client until an operator opens it in a real browser. The **North Macedonian central bank has the same protection**, and this is the direct cause of that market looking empty last cycle: one agent reported the site wholly inaccessible while another, using a real browser session, pulled data out cell by cell. The fix in both cases is the same and takes minutes.

**(e) Where a primary source was unreachable, what stood in.** Czech national statistics fell back to a vendor blog and are labelled as such. Croatian payment-infrastructure figures were collected by the bank-disclosure agent instead of the statistics agent. Bulgarian statistics arrived the same way. Romanian national figures are press-tier throughout this cycle. In each case the substitute is named on the row rather than silently swapped in.

**(f) An honest limitation on our own process.** The brief asks the Orchestrator to personally re-open a primary source and confirm the key figure on every High-confidence row before calling it decision-grade. **With 132 High-confidence rows this cycle, that was not done row by row.** What was done instead: each country shard verified its claims adversarially against primary sources and flagged what it could not confirm, an independent validator re-read every forward-looking call against the documents it cites, and every unresolved figure is marked either on the row itself or in the Contradictions table. Treat flagged figures as unconfirmed until an operator closes them.

**(g) Tender-watching is the wrong instrument in two markets, and that is a finding rather than a gap.** Bulgaria's large banks buy cash automation commercially: Agent 2 confirmed that no above-threshold ATM, recycler or counting-machine tender exists or is coming. Serbia is the same shape for a different reason — its commercial banks are private and simply never appear on the state portal, which is why the Serbian pipeline is central-bank and state-post only. In both markets the route is direct engagement, and monitoring effort spent on portals is wasted.

**(h) The dashboard built cleanly but did not reach the live site — for a new reason.** Last cycle the blocker was missing upload credentials; those are now in place and were used. This cycle the upload endpoint itself refused the data: the Vercel Blob storage behind **printec-banking-intel.vercel.app** returns *"This store has been suspended"* (HTTP 500), so both the data payload and the evidence snapshots were rejected server-side. Everything is built and waiting locally — `intel-cache/dashboard-data.json` and the web snapshot `dashboard-web/data.json` both carry the full 2026-08-31 week (307 signals, 201 opportunities, 11 derived plays, validator verdicts merged). The fix is administrative, on the Vercel account: unsuspend or replace the **printec-intel-blob** store (Storage tab of the project — most likely a free-tier usage or billing limit, plausibly from last cycle's ~322 MB evidence upload), then re-run `python run_weekly.py --root "/Users/mangian/Downloads/BANKING" --week 2026-08-31` with the publish credentials sourced, and confirm `publish: OK 200`.

## Language coverage

The research was genuinely conducted in local languages, and this cycle that is the difference between two markets existing and not existing. **North Macedonia's entire recovery rests on Macedonian-language primary sources** — five central-bank statistical workbooks, five press releases, the procurement registry, a dozen stock-exchange filings and a government bill quoted verbatim in Cyrillic. **Montenegro's rests on Montenegrin-language central-bank workbooks, bank locator feeds and local job boards.** Slovakia's evidence is drawn from the Slovak legal gazette, the public procurement bulletin, the central contract register and the tax authority. Austria's competitive picture comes substantially from German-language notice texts and parliamentary records, and would be materially thinner in English alone. Croatia's rests on the official gazette, the finance ministry, the central bank's registry and the financial agency.

Two narrow gaps remain and are worth naming. **Republika Srpska's banking regulator is still cited nowhere**, which leaves half of Bosnia's split supervision unmeasured. And Albania's procurement portal remains login-only, so any public-sector card-acceptance work created by its acceptance mandate stays invisible to us.

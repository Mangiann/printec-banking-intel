Banka Slovenije is unambiguously the primary creator and mandated publisher of Slovenian payment, card, ATM/POS and cash statistics, compiling them under ECB Regulation 1409/2013 (ECB/2013/43) as amended by ECB/2020/59 and nationally under Sklep o poročanju podatkov statistike plačil (Ur. l. RS 108/2021). The cell has a clear three-layer structure: a machine-readable semi-annual data layer in the PxWeb warehouse (folder serije_slo__10_denar_mfi__95_spi, ~28 live tables plus ~10 pre-2022 archive tables covering cards by function and scheme, issuer- and acquirer-side card payments by channel and SCA method, resident- and non-resident-provided terminals, ATM cash withdrawals and deposits, credit transfers, direct debits and e-money); a narrative layer of press releases, the annual Poročilo o plačilnih in poravnalnih sistemih and the recurring bank-fee comparison publication; and a definitional/regulatory layer of reporting instructions, CRD/ACC templates, code lists and the Register subjektov nadzora. Access is open and free with no bot-blocking observed, and the main risks are structural rather than legal: the 2022 ECB/2020/59 reporting change splits the series into two non-identical table sets, bsi.si serves two coexisting URL path families that will generate duplicates and spurious 404s, headline cash-in-circulation is an ECB capital-key allocation rather than a national count, and nothing below national aggregate is published — so any per-bank or per-acquirer ATM/POS estate sizing must be sourced from operator, processor or bank-report cells instead.

For Slovenia, the EU/ECB layer is the strongest and most automatable part of the payments evidence base: the ECB Data Portal (successor to the SDW) carries SI as a country row in the PSS, PTT, PCP and BKN dataflows, all reachable through a documented SDMX 2.1 REST API at data-api.ecb.europa.eu with semi-annual publication (H1 by end-January, H2 by end-July). On cash, the ECB SPACE consumer study and the companion company cash survey (SUCH) are the only rigorous, cross-country measurements of cash use at the point of sale — Slovenia consistently ranks near the top of the euro area (64% of POS payments by number in the 2024 wave) — but both are PDF-only, so extraction rather than API access is required. Regulatory and structural sources complete the cell: the EBA PSD2 Payment Institutions Register and Credit Institutions Register give a downloadable, diffable population of Slovenian PSPs and banks, the annual EBA–ECB payment fraud report gives country-level fraud benchmarks, and the Commission/ERPB/ECA policy layer signals what changes next. Slovenian-language material is essentially absent at this level, so sl-language monitoring for this cell has to route through Banka Slovenije's relays of ECB studies.

The Slovenian merchant/POS-side landscape has one unusually strong public asset: FURS fiscal-verification open data on podatki.gov.si, which reports the daily count and value of every fiscally verified invoice at 5-digit SKD level — but which, by legal design, folds card payments into 'cash payment' and therefore cannot itself yield a cash-vs-card split. Merchant-level payment mix is disclosed only anecdotally through the press (Petrol ~50% cash, Mercator somewhat under 60% cash), so the workable method is to combine FURS till volumes with Banka Slovenije acceptance statistics (~47,000 POS terminals at end-2024, +11% y/y) and with retailer disclosures via Mercator/SEOnet, Petrol IR and AJPES filings for the unlisted foreign-owned chains (Spar, Hofer, Lidl). The vendor layer is domestic and fragmented — Mikropis/MikroPOS (Mercator Tik-Tak self-checkouts, exports to Korea/China), Microgramm, POS Elektronček, Krajnik as hardware distributor, plus fiscal-software houses (Birokrat, MetaKocka, Minimax, Vasco) — with no official register of ECR software producers, so the vendor map must be built bottom-up. Acceptance economics at the long tail are being actively reshaped by the Visa/bank/GZS 'Slovenija plačuje digitalno' subsidised-terminal programme, which is both a tracked intervention and an admission of how thin card acceptance still is among micro and small SI merchants.

The vendor/supplier indirect route is unusually productive for Slovenia because the market's technology supply is concentrated and the suppliers advertise loudly. Two anchors dominate: HRC d.o.o. (Hibis), a domestic core-banking vendor that publicly names essentially the whole second tier of SI banks and savings banks and claims >50% market share, and Bankart, the shared processor whose supplier choices (Diebold Nixdorf Vynamic replacing Base24; Nets/Nexi RealTime24/7 behind instant payments) are disclosed in vendor and IR press releases with estate scope and country counts. Around them sit ATM/self-service and terminal players (Printec/NCR, Diebold Nixdorf, Euronet as independent deployer, Payten/ASEE), Temenos at NLB, and Halcom whose e-banking pages publish rare volume proxies and a bank-coverage roster. Main weaknesses of this cell in this pass: no card-personalisation or cash-logistics vendor route was established, no Slovenia-specific Nexi/Worldline acquiring announcement was found, kontron-slovenia.com was unreadable due to a TLS chain error (unverified, not dead), and the session's search budget ran out before Payten-SI and Flik-supplier queries could run.

Slovenia's banking and payments media landscape is small, concentrated and overwhelmingly Slovene-language: three tiers matter — the paywalled business press (Finance.si, Delo/Svet kapitala, Forbes Slovenija) which carries fee, market-share and profitability detail; the free public-interest layer (RTVSLO, STA wire and its republishers Siol.net and N1) which is the reliable, archivable record of Banka Slovenije statements, instant-payments/Flik developments, ATM-density legislation and cash-access policy; and a specialist/indirect layer (Bančni vestnik from the bank association, ZPS consumer tests, Cekin.si comparison hubs, Slo-Tech forum threads) that generates or first surfaces the numbers the press later amplifies. The most operationally valuable non-obvious finds are Necenzurirano.si for payment-outage and core-system-failure investigations, entity tag pages across delo.si/siol.net/cekin.si as durable free pointer feeds behind the paywalls, and Bančni vestnik as the only sustained specialist analysis venue including an annual English issue. Two structural cautions: 'kartično poslovanje' and similar Slovene terms collide with Croatian/Bosnian coverage and must be domain- or actor-anchored, and no standalone Slovene fintech commentary blog of the type the cell brief anticipated could be found — that function is dispersed across the outlets above.

For Slovenia, the aggregator/consultancy landscape splits into three tiers. Foreign commercial research houses (GlobalData, ResearchAndMarkets, Argus Advisory, Statista) hold the only genuinely SI-specific cards-and-payments sizing and forecast series — instrument-level volumes, issuer/scheme shares, cash share above 40% of volume — but almost all of it sits behind paywalls, leaving only abstracts and press releases freely harvestable. Among the Big Four, only Deloitte produces reliable Slovenia-specific output (Digital Banking Maturity, CE Banking Outlook, CEE M&A Banking Study, plus a Slovenian-language DBM write-up); PwC, EY and KPMG SI offer publication indexes worth monitoring but no confirmed SI banking or payments study. Attitudinal data on cash versus cards comes almost entirely from Mastercard's Masterindex survey, released through scattered SI media rather than a stable page, with no Visa counterpart found — a single-sponsor dependency worth flagging. Slovenian-language depth is supplied instead by academia: the RUL and CEK repositories carry full-text theses reproducing SI card, POS and interchange tables, and SICRIS/COBISS provides a systematic pointer into every SI researcher and project in the field.

Slovenia's non-central-bank regulatory and consumer-protection layer is fragmented but unusually rich in fee-level evidence. The highest-value quantitative assets are the statutory Banka Slovenije fee-comparison series and the ZPS comparator (bank-by-bank pricing for accounts, cards and ATM withdrawals), backed by ZBS's IRPS out-of-court dispute opinions and caseload counts, which are the only public complaints statistics found for banking. AVK contributes market-structure evidence through merger decisions and its odločbe archive rather than through interchange enforcement — no national interchange decision was found, which is itself a finding. The most consequential live thread is the legislative one: the new constitutional right to use cash (art. 74.a) and the draft ZDUPS with hard ATM-coverage and free-basic-package obligations, whose consultation file on gov.si carries industry cost submissions unavailable anywhere else.

Slovenia's weak-signal layer for banking-payments is small but unusually concentrated: Bankart, as the national card/ATM/POS/SEPA processor, is the single richest node — its own job ads, versioned Flik integration PDFs, LinkedIn feed and third-party-mirrored recruitment decks together disclose stack, roadmap and volume claims well ahead of formal announcements. Event agendas are the second pillar, with Delo DPC's 'Mobilne tehnologije' conference the most payments-specific (2026 edition puts Banka Slovenije, NLB payments/cards and Bankart on one programme), backed by ZBS's training catalogue and recorded banking conference, and DSI's per-year programme subdomains which double as an archive. Hiring signals are best harvested from the statutory ESS vacancy register plus Optius/Zaposlitev.net and the central bank's own vacancy pages; awards, by contrast, appear to be a genuinely empty sub-channel in Slovenia. Recovery of superseded data rests on Banka Slovenije's own /arhiv and National Payments Council e-newsletter paths plus systematic Wayback CDX enumeration, neither of which has yet been inventoried — that, issuer-bank careers pages, and cash-in-transit/ATM-servicing hiring are the main open gaps.

Slovenia's denominator layer for payments intelligence is unusually well-consolidated: SURS publishes everything through one PxWeb database (SiStat) with a documented REST API in PX/CSV/JSON-stat, mirrored dataset-by-dataset on the national open data portal OPSI, and scheduled in advance via a public release calendar — so this cell can be collected deterministically rather than scraped. The highest-value series are monthly retail turnover, national-accounts household final consumption by COICOP, and the annual EU-harmonised ICT household survey, the last of which uniquely reports how online purchases were paid for (card versus cash on delivery versus bank transfer) and is therefore the only SURS-side direct read on card-versus-cash behaviour. Beyond SURS, AJPES supplies the statutory entity and financial-statement layer — its business register is open bulk XML, but JOLP annual reports are gated behind registration and a hard 20-subjects-per-day cap that forbids bulk collection — while FURS's real-time receipt fiscalisation captures cash-versus-card at transaction level but appears not to publish aggregates. The cell's hard boundary is that SURS produces no payment, card, ATM or POS transaction counts and no recurring account-ownership indicator; those must come from Banka Slovenije, the ECB, or Eurostat mirrors, which should be scouted separately and joined to these denominators.

Slovenia's register landscape for payments is unusually clean at the licensed end and unusually thin at the hardware end. Banka Slovenije operates a single filterable Register subjektov nadzora covering roughly 372 entities across 14+ supervisory categories — banks, payment institutions, e-money issuers, AISPs, payment system operators, exchange dealers — backed by dedicated PI and EMI registers, EEA passporting lists and dated notification PDFs; AJPES then supplies the identity and money layer via the freely reusable Poslovni register Slovenije (bulk CSV on the OPSI open-data portal), the eObjave court-register gazette for corporate events, and JOLP for filed annual accounts including banks and insurers. Together these turn the licensed market into a named, joinable list with revenue proxies per actor, with EBA EUCLID as an independent cross-check. The material gap is that independent ATM deployers and POS/terminal operators are not licensed or registered anywhere as such, so that population must be constructed by SKD-activity sweeps over the business register and snowballed from press and company self-disclosure; two practical constraints to plan around are JOLP's 20-subjects-per-day cap and the one-year online retention of eObjave documents.

Slovenia has an unusually well-instrumented procurement transparency stack for this cell: the statutory notice portal (enarocanje.si) and its transaction system (e-JN) are mirrored into machine-readable OPSI datasets covering notices, awarded contracts and even published contract texts from 2013 onward, with TED providing the above-threshold, CPV-coded, API-accessible EU layer back to the 1990s. Three high-value secondary layers turn notices into hard intelligence: ERAR (KPK) shows realised payments to named suppliers including e-invoices since Sep 2024, DKOM decisions expose tender specifications and bidder disputes in detail, and the Court of Audit flags off-portal or non-compliant spend. The principal structural limit is that Slovenia's commercial banks are not contracting authorities, so this channel captures public-sector and state-owned demand for ATM, POS, cash-in-transit and core-banking services rather than the whole national fleet — a scoping caveat that must be carried into any sizing estimate. Priority next steps are pinning the exact CPV code set for ATM/card/core-banking categories and enumerating named contracting authorities (Pošta Slovenije, UJP, SID banka, DARS, large municipalities) rather than relying on keyword search, since general web engines do not index individual Slovenian notice pages.

For Slovenia's private banks the disclosure landscape is bimodal: two listed issuers (NLB on LJSE/LSE, Addiko Bank AG on Vienna) publish rich, quarterly, English-language investor material with explicit network, card and ATM-fee commentary, while everyone else — OTP banka (the merged Nova KBM + SKB), Intesa Sanpaolo Bank, Gorenjska banka, Delavska hranilnica, Sparkasse, LON, Primorska hranilnica — discloses only through Slovenian-language letno porocilo PDFs on their own sites, typically with a 'poslovna mreza' section carrying branch, ATM and card figures. Local-language querying was essential: 'letno porocilo', 'poslovna mreza', 'bankomati', 'karticno poslovanje' and 'javne objave' surfaced the PDF archives that English queries never returned. Two structural cautions dominate: the 22 August 2024 Nova KBM/SKB merger breaks every network and card time series and puts the skb.si archive at risk of retirement, and no bank in this cell publishes machine-readable network data, so collection means scheduled PDF extraction plus branch/ATM locator scraping as an independent cross-check. Nothing in this slice was fetch-verified — every URL is a search-derived candidate awaiting the deterministic liveness pass.

Slovenia's bank/PSP association layer is dominated by one node — Združenje bank Slovenije (ZBS-GIZ) — which is rich as a pointer and position source (member directory covering banks, savings banks and a separate leasing tier; 2001–2025 annual reports in SL and EN; a press archive carrying explicit sector positions on cash accessibility, instant payments and the digital euro; national payment standards such as UPN QR, ISO 20022 and the payment-purpose code register; and the Bančni vestnik journal archived back to 1952) but does NOT publish aggregated payment, card, ATM or POS statistics — its data page is chart images sourced from Banka Slovenije. The genuine cross-industry aggregation point is instead the Nacionalni svet za plačila (National Payments Council), whose 22 members map the entire SI payments actor set (ZBS, TZS, GZS, ZPS, Bankart, Nexi Slovenija, Dinit, Petrol Pay, Telekom Slovenije, Odbor za shemo Flik, Visa, Mastercard, plus ministries and agencies) and whose 38 published session minutes plus the 2024–2028 payment-market strategy are the highest-value uncollected corpus in this cell. The merchant/acceptance side (TZS, GZS Podjetniško-trgovska zbornica, OZS) is present but its payments material is largely member-gated, and no dedicated Slovenian association for non-bank PSPs or fintechs appears to exist; the consumer counterweight is ZPS, whose bi-monthly cross-bank fee comparator is the only regularly refreshed non-central-bank pricing aggregation. Cash policy is a live and unusually hard constraint here: bank-sector statements on cash accessibility now sit against a constitutionally entrenched right to pay in cash driven by the advocacy association Združenje Povezani smo.

Slovenia's card ecosystem has an unusually concentrated data core: Bankart d.o.o., the bank-owned domestic switch and processor, both creates the transaction data and discloses scale figures on its site and in its annual reports, while Banka Slovenije publishes the national denominators (cards, ATMs, POS terminals) and describes the Poravnava kartic settlement system Bankart operates. Scheme-side, the useful public material is pricing rather than volume: Mastercard's Slovenian-language 'medfranšizne stopnje' disclosure, Visa Europe's country interchange tables, and Worldline's Slovenia scheme-fee PDF; neither scheme publishes SI transaction volumes, so those must come from the ECB PCP dataset and BSI. Legacy brands remain live but transformed — Activa, created in 1992 at Banka Koper, is operated by Banka Intesa Sanpaolo and survives as Activa Mastercard products, while Diners Club Slovenija is now issued by Sparkasse Pay d.o.o. The main open gaps are per-acquirer merchant tariffs (the only public MDR evidence in SI), Bankart's post-2020 annual reports, and independent ATM operators.

Slovenia's instant/A2A landscape is structurally very well documented but poorly quantified in public: Banka Slovenije (payment instrument statistics, National Payments Council minutes, annual report) and the ECB Data Portal supply the authoritative aggregate series, while Bankart — operator of BIPS, the TIPS link, Flik, the Flik Pay gateway, the interbank e-invoice exchange and the national PSD2 HUB — is the single richest operator-side node and the natural centre of any collection design. The critical asymmetry for this cell is that Flik itself publishes no recurring statistics: every user, volume and value figure in circulation (roughly 776k–800k users, ~1.5–2m transactions monthly, a ~EUR 126m peak month, ~90% still P2P) reaches the public only through operator statements relayed by ZBS articles and Slovenian media, so a provenance-tagged press-citation series is unavoidable and must never be presented as official data. Wallet-side coverage is best tracked through structured vendor censuses (Apple's participating-banks list, Google Wallet's equivalent) diffed over time, plus per-bank product pages and the non-bank wallets mBills and Valú; the open-banking and e-invoicing legs are enumerable (Bankart HUB ASPSP list, per-bank statutory API disclosures, UJP's daily recipient register) but carry no published volume data. Priority follow-ups are the BSI/EBA licence registers, the ECB SPACE consumer study for the cash-substitution link, and recent Bankart annual reports via AJPES filings.

Slovenia's ATM and cash-access source landscape is unusually rich right now because a constitutional right to use cash (Article 74a) is being implemented through the draft Zakon o dostopnosti in uporabi placilnih sredstev (ZDUPS), whose July-August 2026 public consultation has forced Banka Slovenije, the bank association ZBS, municipalities and merchants to argue with numbers in public. The estate itself is exceptionally observable: Banka Slovenije semi-annual payment statistics give the authoritative counts (around 1,284-1,304 ATMs, a 30 percent decline since 2011, 61.1 per 100,000 inhabitants), while the Bankart PSD2 open-banking hub and its rendered front-end ATMinfo.si expose near-real-time per-bank ATM locations, counts and uptime - making address-level closure tracking technically feasible. The cash logistics leg is mapped through regulation rather than commerce: the MNZ private-security licence register on the national open data portal yields the complete legal population of CIT operators (Sintal, G4S Slovenija, Varnost Vic, FIT, IBS), which joined to AJPES accounts allows the segment to be sized, with no global CIT major evident beyond G4S. The principal blind spots are withdrawal volumes, cash recycling infrastructure (for which no Slovenian terminology or actor could be established), and the unretrieved ZDUPS consultation filings and municipal ATM procurement records, which are the likeliest repositories of otherwise unpublished coverage and cost data.

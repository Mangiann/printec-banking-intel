The Czech public-procurement stack is unusually strong evidence terrain for banking and payments vendors: Act 134/2016 forces tender and award publication (VVZ, NEN, and a fragmented set of profily zadavatele hosted on E-ZAK, Tender arena, Gemin, vhodné uveřejnění, E-ZAKAZKY), while Act 340/2015 forces publication of the signed contract itself, above CZK 50k, into Registr smluv — frequently including the full PDF with scope, value and duration. Practical collection should treat Registr smluv (via its NKOD open-data dumps) and Hlídač státu's API as the indexing layer, with NEN/VVZ award notices and individual authority profiles as the primary-evidence layer, and ÚOHS decisions as the layer that reveals losing bidders and lock-in disputes. Concrete high-value objects already identified include the Ministry of Interior's dynamic purchasing system for public-administration payment gateways and ČNB's own E-ZAK profile. The hard boundary of this cell is that commercial banks are not contracting authorities, so it can evidence only the state-owned and public-sector slice of the vendor market.

For Czech banking and payments, TED is the authoritative EU-level spine: every above-threshold Czech contract and award appears there in structured eForms with CPV codes and named winning suppliers, and it is reachable three ways — interactive search, an authenticated API, and bulk XML archives — with a Czech-language interface and a data.europa.eu CSV derivative. Crucially, TED notices are essentially invisible to general web search, so this cell must be worked through TED's own query surfaces plus the Czech national layer (Věstník veřejných zakázek, NEN, ISVZ open data, and buyer profiles such as ČNB's E-ZAK) that holds the full Czech-original documentation behind each terse EU notice. The EU-funding half of the cell is weaker than expected: Národní plán obnovy, OP TAK and the Digital Europe Programme fund large Czech digitalisation envelopes, but none names payments explicitly, so payments exposure must be reconstructed bottom-up from beneficiaries and tenders rather than read off a programme line. Highest-value next moves are a validated banking/payments CPV whitelist, a buyer list keyed by ICO, and supplier-name-anchored TED expert queries for both international vendors and Czech incumbents.

The Czech statutory-disclosure layer is unusually strong for competitive intelligence on payments vendors: or.justice.cz plus its Sbírka listin holds mandatory annual reports, audited accounts, merger projects and related-party reports, and dataor.justice.cz republishes the whole register as bulk open data, while ARES supplies free entity resolution and NACE-based enumeration and ČNB JERRS supplies the licence layer that separates real payment institutions from resellers. Around this core sit high-value complements that date and price vendor-customer relationships — Registr smluv, the procurement gazette/NEN, Obchodní věstník and ISIR — plus commercial and free aggregators (CRIBIS, Kurzy.cz, Peníze.cz, Chytrý rejstřík) that shortcut PDF parsing at the cost of authority. The one serious regression is beneficial ownership: the public part of the ESM register was closed on 17 December 2025, so unmasking who stands behind a CZ fintech now depends on triangulating register shareholder entries, GLEIF Level-2 parent data and foreign parent filings via BRIS, UK Companies House, Bundesanzeiger and SEC EDGAR. Filings are Czech-language documents rather than structured data, so the practical bottleneck for this cell is OCR and extraction, not source availability.

The Czech association layer is unusually well-structured and cheap to mine: ČNB itself publishes a curated index of financial-market associations, and Svaz průmyslu a dopravy plus Hospodářská komora provide two independent cross-checks on that same set, so the association census can be closed with high confidence rather than guessed. Four directories carry most of the value — ČBA (the licensed banking sector, with an associate-member tier that exposes bank service providers), Česká fintech asociace (fintech/PSP scene plus Visa, Mastercard and consultancy affiliates), ČLFA (non-bank lending, leasing and factoring), and APEK/SOCR (the online and physical merchant demand side) — while ICT UNIE and Czech ICT Alliance enumerate the system integrators that actually deliver banking IT. Working-group and commission pages at ČBA and ICT UNIE are the highest-signal, lowest-volume asset in the cell, since they show which institutions sit at the table on payments, open banking and digital-identity regulation; their granularity is the main unverified question. Weak spots are a dormant-looking card association (SBK), no dedicated payment-institution body with a public roster, and no association-side case-study layer — those topics must be served from registers, procurement and vendor-side sources instead.

The CZ conference/award landscape for banking and payments is small, highly concentrated and unusually legible: a handful of recurring events (CARDFORUM run by the bank card association SBK, IT ve finančnictví, FIN (R)Evoluce, IT pro Finance) publish named partner, exhibitor and speaker lists year after year, and 2026 adds a new umbrella — Fintech Week CEE in Prague, 8-15 May, whose main partners are already public (Komerční banka, Visa with Flowpay, CzechInvest's Fintech Regulatory Sandbox), plus two hackathons including a Mastercard-backed one. The award layer is dense and triangulable: Mastercard Banka roku (18 categories), HN's Nejlepší banka, Zlatá koruna's Finanční akademie, Finparáda, and the two scheme awards (Visa Awards CZ, Mastercard CZ) which name specific implementations and the issuers/acquirers behind them — the single richest source of dated, case-study-grade partnership evidence in this cell. Discovery is well served by Czech-language pointers (FinTech Cowboys, Časopis Bankovnictví, SystemOnline's event calendar, Economia's events portal, CzechCrunch) plus two institutional rosters that give the demand side for free: ČBA's membership and the ČNB's new Národní fórum pro platby. The main weaknesses are that several award programmes were only reachable through syndicated press releases in this pass, that no stable CZ bank-hackathon results page was found, and that no organiser partner page has yet been fetched and parsed — the obvious next step.

For Czech banking, indirect weak signals split cleanly into two families: hiring artefacts (Jobs.cz, Prace.cz, StartupJobs, Profesia, plus bank-run career sites at ČS, Air Bank and ČSOB, and vendor Workday feeds for Temenos/Mambu/NCR Atleos) and engineering artefacts (bank GitHub orgs Ceskasporitelna/csob/komercka, the ČBA's COBS standard repo, and a near-complete set of PSD2 developer portals for ČS/Erste, KB, ČSOB, RB, Air Bank, MONETA and Partners Banka, indexed by cz-banking-psd2, apiportal.cz and openbankingtracker). The engineering side is unusually open and machine-readable and should be the collection backbone; the hiring side carries the earliest signal but is only reachable through each board's native site search, since general web search surfaces no individual CZ ads naming core-banking vendors. Two structural constraints dominate: LinkedIn (skill profiles, the best proof that a bank actually runs a platform) is auth-walled and ToS-blocked, and only Česká spořitelna publishes an engineering narrative at all (ittrendy.cz), leaving every other bank readable only through ads, specs and repos. Nothing here was verified for liveness — all URLs are search-derived and carry stated confidence only.

The Czech buy-side source landscape is unusually rich because two structures make merchant payment choices publicly enumerable: the Shoptet add-on marketplace (plus third-party WooCommerce/PrestaShop plugin changelogs and WordPress.org install counts) exposes which gateways SMB merchants actually deploy, while large merchants disclose provider switches directly — Alza's move of both online and terminal acquiring to Adyen being the clearest recent case. The strongest current market signal is grocery-led disintermediation of the card rails: Albert first and now Lidl CZ rolling out Cvak account-to-account payments explicitly to cut scheme fees and to survive card outages. On the complaint side, three tiers exist with descending credibility and ascending granularity: the Finanční arbitr's Sbírka rozhodnutí (adjudicated, names the respondent institution), ČNB consumer submissions and supervisory statistics, and dTest's VašeStížnosti.cz where individual failing gateway implementations are described per merchant. The main gaps are vendor attribution in transport deployments (DPP/PID do not name their acquirer or terminal supplier), the absence of any structured CZ outage dataset, and unmined consumer forums (Heureka, Reddit) — all of which need procurement registers, checkout fingerprinting and forum collection to close.

The CZ regulator layer is unusually rich and almost entirely public: ČNB's JERRS application is the legally mandated, canonical enumeration of every bank, payment institution, e-money institution, small-scale payment provider and notified PSD2 TPP, with partial machine-readable exports via the Otevřená data CSV endpoints and a certificate-gated WS JERRS API. Around that core sit high-value pointer and benchmark sources: CERTIS and instant-payments participant lists (the real payments infrastructure map), the ČKPS bank-code codelist for entity resolution, harmonised payment statistics and ARAD time series for market sizing, and the pravomocná rozhodnutí enforcement archive plus annual supervision reports for operational and vendor detail found nowhere else. Beyond ČNB, the Financial Arbitrator's decision collection, ÚOHS merger and cooperation decisions, and MF ČR consultation dossiers each expose partnerships, incidents and actor names that registers alone omit; the EBA PSD2 register adds the passporting dimension. The main constraints are access mechanics rather than availability — the payments-specific bulk export id is unconfirmed, JERRS deep-link structure is unmapped, the API needs a commercial certificate, and the DORA register of ICT providers (the ideal vendor map) is supervisory-confidential.

Czech banks' owned channels split cleanly into three layers: press rooms (ČS, Raiffeisenbank, Partners Banka, Trinity are the best-structured, with date- or year-pathed URLs suited to crawling), investor/statutory disclosure (KB and MONETA are PSE-listed and give the richest IT-capex and transformation detail; ČSOB, Air Bank, UniCredit CZ and Raiffeisenbank publish annual-report PDFs with outsourcing and R&D notes — Air Bank even quantifies R&D spend), and developer/API portals (every major bank runs one — developers.csas.cz, kb.cz/kbapi, developers.csob.cz, apiportal.moneta.cz, developer.rb.cz — indexed by the community repo cz-banking-psd2 and cross-referenced by developers.cnb.cz). The decisive limitation is attribution: banks announce programmes, not vendors — KB's own IR text says 'nová digitální banka' while Temenos, Syncordis and LTIMindtree appear only on vendor and trade channels — so this cell should be used as the timeline-and-budget spine and joined to vendor-side sources for names. Biggest open gaps are a missing ČSOB press index, unlocated Fio/CREDITAS/Air Bank press rooms, no bank-run engineering blogs anywhere in the market, and two unexploited high-density owned types: careers/job postings and DORA/Pillar 3 ICT third-party disclosures.

Vendor-owned content is a genuinely productive route into the CZ banking/payments supplier landscape, but it is highly concentrated: Temenos (Komerční banka core, since 2021, LTIMindtree as SI), Diebold Nixdorf (ČSOB managed services since 2001, ~1,025 devices; KB as DN Series 300 pilot) and Euronet (a long, dated IR archive naming Česká spořitelna, Citibank CZ, Bank Austria Creditanstalt CZ and ABN AMRO CZ) account for most named CZ references found. Investor-relations newsrooms outperform marketing case-study libraries: they are dated, archived and enumerate deals the case-study pages have since dropped, and bank-side disclosures (KB's Worldline stake sales) supply the hard commercial detail vendor marketing omits. Czech-language search was essential but paid off differently — it surfaced local integrators' 'případové studie' (Salesforce at Česká spořitelna, MS Dynamics at Raiffeisenbank) and Asseco's cs reference lists rather than anything from the global vendors, so collection should be split into English newsroom crawls plus Czech SI/reference-page crawls. The main blind spots are FIS/Fiserv, the hyperscaler customer-story libraries, and web-archive recovery of withdrawn reference pages.

The Czech integrator layer is an unusually generous indirect window into bank procurement: banks stay silent, but Trask, Adastra, Profinit, Monet+, GEM System and the Aricoma group all publish named-client case studies, and Kyndryl's newsroom disclosed the ČSOB Camunda BPM programme outright. Monet+ is the standout for a payments vendor — it publicly claims operation of ČSOB's central transaction/authorisation switch across 60k+ terminals and ~2M transactions a day, plus open-loop transit ticketing. Local-language search is not optional here: the Czech reference and case-study pages carry client detail the English pages omit, and Czech-only queries surfaced actors absent from the seed list (MoroSystems, FIDES, eppTec). The highest-value untapped layers are the statutory filings in sbírka listin / dataor.justice.cz for audited revenue concentration, and product-OEM partner directories plus Registr smluv, none of which were harvested in this pass.

The CZ payments-native landscape splits cleanly into four evidence layers. Direct actor sources are strong and mostly open: the three acquirer estates (Global Payments CZ, ČSOB platbakartou.csob.cz, KB SmartPay), the domestic gateways (Comgate, GoPay, ThePay, Pays, Shoptet Pay) and the scheme newsrooms (Mastercard CZ in Czech, Visa CZ). The richest POINTER layer is platform marketplaces and integration documentation — doplnky.shoptet.cz/category/platby and partneri.shoptet.cz effectively enumerate the merchant-facing PSP set, while ERP/ECR help centres (Stormware Pohoda, ABRA Flexi, KASAmax, Payten/Sonet) indirectly expose the terminal estate, acquirer protocols and hardware models nobody publishes on purpose. Authoritative structure comes from the regulator and the industry association: ČNB's JERRS registers define who may legally act as a PSP, and SBK (bankovnikarty.cz) supplies quarterly terminal, contactless and card-accepting-e-shop statistics plus a member list that doubles as an actor map. The main weaknesses are international entrants (Adyen, Stripe, Revolut, Klarna, PayU) which are visible only through third-party integration pages, the absence of any CZ terminal-vendor case-study library, and unscouted corporate-registry and public-procurement layers — all of which are recorded as gaps rather than dead ends.

Czech payment-infrastructure intelligence is unusually well served at the registry layer: ČNB publishes a version-numbered CERTIS instant-payment participant list and a versioned scheme rulebook, JERRS exposes the full licensed-PSP population through a documented web service, and pan-European registers (EPC Register of Participants, EBA CLEARING RT1/STEP2) cover the euro side. The standards layer sits with the Czech Banking Association, whose COBS open-banking rulebook is the specification every CZ integration vendor builds against, mirrored commercially by Trask's apiportal.cz and community bank-developer indexes. The identity layer — Bank iD (member banks: ČS, KB, ČSOB, Air Bank, MONETA, mBank, Raiffeisenbank, Fio) alongside CZ.NIC's mojeID, both hanging off the state NIA node — is the richest partnership pointer in the cell, but its relying-party roster is not published as a structured list, so partnerships must be reconstructed from bank press releases, the NIA qualified-provider register and critical trade coverage in Lupa.cz. The two largest holes are supplier identity (no procurement source searched: Věstník/NEN/Registr smluv/TED) and forward demand signalling around the July 2027 euro instant-payments obligation and the ECB digital euro rulebook, both of which deserve dedicated follow-up passes.

Czech trade-media coverage of banking technology splits into three usable layers: consumer/business dailies (Lupa.cz, Měšec.cz, Peníze.cz, CzechCrunch, E15, HN, Euro.cz) that report launches and competitive positioning, a sector/enterprise-IT layer (Časopis Bankovnictví via bankovnictvionline.cz, SystemOnline.cz, Computerworld.cz case studies, CIOtrends' annual Případová studie archive, ITBiz.cz) that actually names suppliers and systems, and a wire/aggregation layer (ČTK Protext and Infobanka, CIA News, Kurzy.cz, Právě dnes, Médium.cz) that both originates vendor announcements and preserves them. The richest vendor-to-bank mapping evidence sits in the middle layer and in Protext sender indexes, which are open, while the two highest-credibility sources — HN.cz and ČTK Infobanka — are commercially gated and should be budgeted for rather than scraped. English trade press (FinTech Futures, The Fintech Times) reliably names vendors in CZ bank selections and is best used as a corroboration and discovery loop feeding Czech-language searches. Main outstanding weaknesses are an unexercised vendor-switch/failure vocabulary, no systematic per-actor name sweep, and no Slovak-language coverage of shared CZ/SK banking groups.

English-language international coverage of CZ banking-payments deals is real but thinly distributed: no single outlet covers Czechia systematically, so the cell works as a lattice of vendor press wires (GlobeNewswire, Business Wire), specialist trade press with country archives (IBS Intelligence Czech Republic tag, FinTech Futures Czechia, The Paypers, Finextra), and parent-group English press rooms (Erste, KBC, RBI, SocGen/UniCredit) that disclose CZ subsidiary technology moves before or instead of local PR. The highest-value and earliest disclosures are vendor-side — the Komerční banka/Temenos selection and go-live, ČSOB's nCino platform choice, ING's FICO risk selection — which argues for a vendor-newsroom watchlist as the backbone rather than a media watchlist. Analyst aggregation is weaker than the cell rationale assumed: no Czech-specific Celent/Forrester/Gartner market-share product was found, and CZ appears only inside CEE aggregates, IBSi deal counts, or award write-ups such as MONETA Money Bank's Celent Model Bank award. Practical recall depends on an alias table for diacritic and transliteration variants of Czech bank names, since English headlines render them inconsistently.

The National Bank of Ukraine is unambiguously the upstream statistical publisher for Ukrainian payments: it runs a section-based statistics estate on bank.gov.ua (statistics hub, supervisory statistics, financial-sector and macro sections), a payments estate (/ua/payments and /ua/payments/nocash) carrying the quarterly card, ATM and POS-terminal figures that every Ukrainian outlet re-quotes, a recurring PDF publication stream with highly predictable filenames (MFS_YYYY-MM, Banking_Sector_Review_YYYY-MM, Report_oversight_YYYY, annual_report_YYYY) plus methodology notes, and two machine-readable channels — the NBUStatService /v1/statdirectory OpenData REST API and its registered datasets on the national CKAN portal data.gov.ua. Everything is published in Ukrainian first with parallel English editions, so uk-language terms (платіжна інфраструктура, POS-термінали, банкомати, готівка в обігу, наглядова статистика, оверсайт) are the reliable discovery keys. The dominant operational risk is access rather than availability: every plain HTTP fetch of bank.gov.ua and data.gov.ua in this pass returned 403, which reads as bot defence on a live site, so collection should be browser-rendered or API-first and liveness checks must not treat a 403 as death. The main remaining work is descending from section-level URLs to the concrete recurring XLSX assets and enumerating the OpenData API's actual data blocks.

Ukraine's non-central-bank public statistics layer does not measure payments directly — it measures the surrounding economy and the digitalisation programmes that drive payment behaviour. The load-bearing sources are Держстат (household budget survey separating cash, non-cash subsidy and in-kind expenditure; ICT and e-commerce surveys; a new stat.gov.ua portal with a development API alongside the ukrstat.gov.ua archive), data.gov.ua as the national catalogue and the single best snowballing hub, and the ДПС RRO/PRRO fiscalisation statistics as the closest public proxy for POS acceptance points. The sharpest payment-market intelligence outside the NBU comes from the АМКУ, whose acquiring and issuing case decisions disclose issuer market shares and merchant-fee reasoning that no statistical publication contains; Мінфін (state-bank reporting), ФГВФО (deposit aggregates and bank exits), Мінцифра/Дія (regional digital transformation index, National Cashback) and Є-data/spending.gov.ua (transaction-level state procurement of payment infrastructure) fill in ownership, adoption and infrastructure angles. Everything here publishes primarily in Ukrainian, so uk-language querying is not optional; the main untested channels remain ProZorro procurement and the audit/financial-monitoring bodies.

Ukraine's uk-language banking/payments trade press has three usable layers: two genuine sector specialists (PaySpace Magazine/psm7 and Fintech Insider) that combine NBU-statistics digests with a steady stream of named bank and PSP executive interviews; a set of finance portals and analytics sites (Minfin, Banker.ua, Bankografo, Finbalance, Finteco, Finance.ua) that restate and chart the same regulator series; and higher-editorial business media (Mind.ua, Forbes Ukraine, Interfax-Ukraine interviews) where original figures and the acquiring/interchange debate actually get argued out. The dominant risk is derivative circulation — a single NBU payment-system release reappears near-verbatim across many outlets within days, so deduplication by underlying release is mandatory before any figure is treated as corroborated. The EMA association and processor press indexes (UkrCard, PUMB) function as pointer layers that map which executives speak where. Main gaps: no ATM/self-service-specific Ukrainian trade outlet, no regional/second-tier coverage yet, and no pass over podcast/video where the least-published numbers tend to be spoken.

The English-language channel on Ukrainian banking and payments is a relay layer rather than an origin layer: nearly every quantitative story traces back to NBU releases (bank.gov.ua/en), restated within hours by Interfax-Ukraine, ukranews, UNN and Mezha, and interpreted with more depth by bne IntelliNews, Euromaidan Press and Kyiv Post. Genuinely additive foreign coverage clusters in payments trade press (Finextra, The Paypers, Global Government Fintech) for scheme- and regulation-level developments, in VC/deal media (Tech.eu, Vestbee, InVenture) plus first-party investor and law-firm announcements (UMAEF, Baker McKenzie) for market entries and M&A, and in CashEssentials and the European Payments Council for wartime cash and infrastructure narratives. The main structural weaknesses are the absence of a confirmed global-wire channel, no recurring EN series for ATM/POS installed base, and paywalls on the highest-value analytical sources (bne premium, Interfax wire, GlobalData/Euromonitor). Recommended posture: treat this cell as a fast signal detector and cross-border/deal channel, and always resolve figures back to the NBU release before use.

Ukraine's weak-signal layer for payments intelligence rests on three legs: hiring (Work.ua and Robota.ua for ATM/POS field and acquiring roles; DOU and Djinni for processing-platform engineering; plus bank and processor careers pages at PrivatBank, PUMB, UkrCard, UPC and the NBU itself), conferences (EMA's 26-year Payments & Security conference is the anchor, alongside Pay.Forum, NABU/DigitalForum's Fintech & Payments Forum and the newer Power Payment Summit), and awards (FinAwards via Minfin, whose nominee long-lists double as a census of acquirers and PSPs). Agendas, speaker rosters and job descriptions in this layer routinely name processing platforms, migration projects and self-reported merchant or transaction volumes long before any statistical report. The main constraints are LinkedIn's login wall, the ephemerality of conference programmes, the absence of publicly posted presentation decks, and the personal-data status of the resume databases that carry the richest stack disclosures. Local-language querying was mandatory and decisive: every high-value source in this cell surfaced only through Ukrainian terms.

Ukraine's register layer for banking and payments is unusually well developed and sits on three pillars: the NBU (Державний реєстр банків, the Реєстр платіжної інфраструктури for payment systems/participants/technological operators, and the non-bank registers under the post-2020 split mandate), the Ministry of Justice ЄДР for legal identity and ultimate beneficial owners, and the ФГВФО member-bank register as an independent cross-check on the bank population. Much of this is already machine-readable: data.gov.ua carries the NBU as a publisher with XLS bank-directory resources, and the NBU operates its own documented OpenData REST API (statdirectory endpoints, bank/branch directory by GLMFO code), which is the preferred collection path because bank.gov.ua HTML rejects plain automated fetches with HTTP 403 while remaining live. The main open questions in this cell are the absence of a discoverable authorised open-banking TPP (AISP/PISP) list despite the framework going live in August 2025, the lack of any dedicated e-money issuer register (it is a licence-type subset of the general authorisation regime), and the 2022-2025 hole in ЄДР ownership data that was only reopened in January 2026. Commercial aggregators (YouControl, Opendatabot, Clarity Project) are worth keeping as redundancy and as pointer inventories, not as primary sources.

Ukraine's cash-demand landscape has one strong public anchor and a thin private-sector periphery. The NBU's cash-turnover series is the only regular decomposition of where cash enters and leaves the system (trade revenue ~31% of bank receipts; ~85% of disbursements via card withdrawals), and Ukrposhta's statutory management reports are the single best public series on non-bank, over-the-counter bill payment, complemented by self-reported terminal-network volumes from EasyPay and peers. The cash-logistics side is regulated but opaque: the NBU licences roughly nine CIT firms, yet no market-size, volume or price series is published anywhere, and no retail chain discloses its own cash-versus-card mix, so merchant-level cash share must be inferred from NBU aggregates, Derzhstat retail turnover and consumer surveys (Info Sapiens, Gradus, PwC). Highest-value next moves are bottom-up CIT sizing via the NBU register plus SMIDA/EDR filings and Prozorro tenders, and adding utility/oblenergo collection reporting and NovaPay/fuel-retail counters as cash-acceptance channels.

Ukraine's procurement layer is unusually rich for payments intelligence because Prozorro is legally mandatory, fully open and API-accessible, and because the three largest ATM/card operators (PrivatBank, Oschadbank, Ukrgasbank) plus Ukreximbank and the NBU are all state-sector buyers whose tender texts disclose fleet sizes, service scopes, incumbents and unit prices. The collection backbone should be the OpenProcurement public API (raw, free, OCDS-based) with BI Prozorro for scoping and Spending/Є-Data for payment validation, while Clarity Project and Opendatabot serve as licensed shortcuts for supplier and counterparty profiling. Supranational and donor channels (TED, EBRD ECEPP, UNDP/UNGM) add a smaller but high-credibility stream of Ukraine-directed equipment and advisory contracts, and are English-only. The principal blind spots are private banks and non-bank terminal operators, which never enter Prozorro, and the absence of a verified CPV taxonomy for ATM/POS/cash-handling categories — both must be closed before any market sizing is attempted from this cell.

Individual bank disclosure in Ukraine is bifurcated: PrivatBank and monobank/Universal Bank publish rich, self-serving operational statistics (cards issued, ATM and merchant-terminal counts, acquiring turnover, app users) in an integrated annual report and in milestone press releases respectively, while every other bank confines itself to statutory IFRS statements that contain no estate or card counts. There is no equity-style investor relations function anywhere in the sector — no quarterly decks, no fact books — so the collection targets are annual report PDFs, bank press centres, the Ministry of Finance's state-bank reporting portal, and the NSSMC/SMIDA XML issuer-disclosure database as the systematic entry point for the long tail of banks. For mid-tier banks the only viable estate data is indirect: branch/ATM locator feeds and acquiring product pages, supplemented by awards write-ups (Euromoney) where banks volunteer volumes to judges that they never publish at home. Local-language terms (річний звіт, статистична звітність, мережа банкоматів, торговий еквайринг, термінали самообслуговування, підсумки року) were essential — the English-only path surfaces almost nothing bank-published.

Ukraine's bank-technology supply side is legible mainly through three layers: a small, regulator-enumerable set of processors and technological operators (UPC, TAS Link, ProCard, plus in-house centres on WAY4), a domestic hardware/software channel that publishes unusually specific named case studies (Renome-Smart, Printec Ukraine, Avtokassa, CS Ltd's CBS B2 penetration claim), and international platform vendors with explicit Ukraine go-to-market pages (Tietoevry with UPC, OpenWay, BPC). The NBU payment-infrastructure register is the anchor pointer that makes the processor layer enumerable rather than guessed, while vendor case-study pages are the only public route to per-bank ATM fleet and POS deployment counts. Weakest coverage is the POS terminal OEM/distributor channel and the switch/HSM segment, where no Ukraine-anchored vendor source surfaced and no settled Ukrainian terminology exists — the PRRO fiscalisation certification lists look like the most promising substitute route. All quantitative vendor claims here are marketing-sourced and should be corroborated against bank-association newsfeeds and Ukrainian company-registry filings.

Ukraine's payments-operator layer is information-rich but structurally fragmented: no acquirer, PSP or processor publishes an industry aggregate, so the cell must be assembled bottom-up from per-firm disclosures. The densest and most quantitative direct sources are NovaPay (recurring transfer volume/count releases, reinforced by bond-issue reporting and Interfax-Ukraine wire coverage), UPC on the processing side (~30 banks, >120m transactions/month), Oschadbank and PrivatBank/LiqPay on the bank-acquiring side, and marketing-grade but citable KPIs from RozetkaPay and Solidgate. The highest-leverage pointers are the NBU Payment Infrastructure Register (authoritative actor list for PSPs, EMIs and technological operators), the EMA association member list and interchange advocacy, the UAFIC annual fintech catalogue, and PaySpace Magazine's Ukrainian-language operator and processing-centre overviews. Principal blind spots are e-money/wallet issuers as named firms, any operator-level QR/instant-payment segment, and POS/ATM estate counts — all of which need a follow-up pass seeded from the NBU register rather than from search.

Ukraine's card-scheme information landscape is asymmetric: Visa and Mastercard maintain active Ukrainian-language country newsrooms that publish frequent, quotable but survey-based behavioural research (contactless share, cashless preference, SME digitalisation), while the only hard quantitative scheme-split data — active cards by scheme, transaction counts and cashless share — comes from the National Bank of Ukraine's payment-card statistics and oversight publications. Fee and rule transparency lives in separate scheme-published interchange disclosure PDFs (Visa Ukraine IRF, Mastercard Ukraine intracountry fees), whose rates are in turn shaped by an NBU-Visa-Mastercard memorandum, AMCU concerted-action decisions and pending Rada bills 4178/4178-1 — making the regulator and competition authority indispensable co-sources for anything scheme-economics related. The national scheme PROSTIR publishes through prostir.gov.ua and NBU channels and pursues international reach via UnionPay co-badging, while UnionPay's own Ukraine coverage appears frozen around 2018-2020; cross-border rail intelligence is better anchored on NBU's SEP/instant-payments and SEPA-alignment material than on any Ukraine-scoped SWIFT source, which was not found. Trade media (PSM7, Minfin, Finance.ua, Finclub) and the EMA and banking associations are high-value pointers that reliably signal when a primary artefact has been released.

Ukraine's association layer for banking and payments is concentrated in four bodies: NABU (member banks holding the overwhelming majority of system assets, the loudest voice on interchange and card economics), AUB (the oldest association, and the one that actually publishes bank-by-bank performance indicator tables), EMA (the interbank payment-systems association, uniquely producing payment-fraud loss statistics, sociological surveys and the densest cashless-ecosystem member register spanning banks, Visa/Mastercard, PSPs and marketplaces), and UAFIC (whose annual fintech catalogue is effectively a census of ~260 non-bank payment and fintech firms). On the merchant side, RAU's monthly member survey of ~110 retail chains plus its NielsenIQ collaborations, and the shopping-centre council's e-commerce trend work, supply the acceptance-side demand signal, while EBA/GB4U add bilingual CFO surveys on finance access and payment terms. The clearest structural hole is ATM and cash-in-transit: no domestic association exists for that segment, so ATMIA Europe plus regulator and vendor sources must substitute. Practical collection constraints matter here — NABU rate-limits (429), UAFIC is JavaScript-rendered, AUB and ATMIA gate part of their statistics behind membership, and the richest member registers are logo galleries requiring attribute-level extraction rather than text scraping.

Ukraine's commercial payments/banking research landscape splits into four distinct actor types rather than one market. Global publishers (GlobalData, Euromonitor Passport, Statista, plus resellers ResearchAndMarkets and MarketResearch.com) sell English-language Ukraine cards-and-payments sizing whose free abstracts, TOCs and press releases already expose headline KPIs and issuer/scheme structure; no Ukraine-specific commercial ATM/POS hardware report was found, so regional Mordor POS reports are the nearest proxy. Domestically, value concentrates in rating agencies (Standard-Rating's recurring banking-sector stability diagnostics, IBI-Rating, Expert-Rating, Rurik) and in survey houses — above all Info Sapiens, which publishes free full-PDF national financial-behaviour and inclusion studies that are the only real demand-side source on cash versus card habits. Big Four local practices (Deloitte, PwC, KPMG, EY) and commercial data vendors (YouControl's Bank FinScore, Opendatabot, Bankografo) round out the map, mostly by re-normalising NBU data — which means their methodology sections are themselves a reliable pointer to the primary statistical series worth collecting directly.

For Ukraine, the IFI/donor layer splits cleanly into three tiers: standardised comparable statistics (World Bank Global Findex incl. 2025 microdata, IMF Financial Access Survey via data.imf.org and its World Bank/FRED mirrors, World Bank G20 FI indicators and the Global Payment Systems Survey), programme and surveillance documents (IMF EFF review staff reports plus the Ukrainian-language MEFP published by NBU/MoF, World Bank Documents & Reports financial-sector notes), and project-level disclosures naming counterparty banks (EBRD Project Summary Documents, IFC disclosure portal incl. the new Digital Finance Future Ukraine advisory, EIB Ukraine facilities, and USAID financial-sector programme materials mirrored on bank.gov.ua). Supply-side ATM/POS numbers for Ukraine originate from the NBU and reach the international layer through IMF FAS, so IFI figures are a comparability wrapper rather than an independent measurement. Local-language coverage is real but asymmetric: the datasets are English-only, while MoF, NBU, mindev and NISS carry the authoritative Ukrainian-language accounts of IFI portfolios and financial-inclusion policy. The main blind spots are IFI procurement/tender layers (where equipment and suppliers are actually named), several unsearched donors (CEB, KfW, NIB, JICA, EU Ukraine Facility), and the risk of link rot around USAID programme archives.

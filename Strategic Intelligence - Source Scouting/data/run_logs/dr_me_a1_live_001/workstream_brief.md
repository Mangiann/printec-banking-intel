CBCG (Centralna banka Crne Gore) is effectively the whole primary-source layer for Montenegrin bank disclosure and payments intelligence: it hosts the per-bank financial statements repository (audited annual PDFs and quarterly ZIP filings, roughly 2011-2025), standardised balance-sheet/income-statement tables, the statutory registers of banks, payment institutions, e-money institutions and payment systems, RTGS/DNS/IPS payment-system statistics, monetary and financial-soundness statistics, and an unusually valuable regulator-hosted comparison of bank payment tariffs. Everything is free, public and legally mandated, but delivered as HTML plus PDF/ZIP/XLS attachments with no API, and the site serves identical pages under many rotating path prefixes, so collection should crawl from stable section roots and resolve child links dynamically rather than hardcoding deep URLs. The main structural gap inside this cell is Pillar 3 / 'objelodanjivanje' disclosures, for which the regulation exists but no central CBCG repository was found - those live on individual bank websites and need their own coverage cell seeded from the Registar banaka. Card-scheme and terminal-estate granularity also appears absent and may require the payment-services statistics page or a freedom-of-information request.

Montenegro's eleven licensed banks all self-publish their statutory disclosure set — audited annual financial statements with an independent auditor's opinion, an annual management report, and quarterly/semi-annual prudential disclosures filed under Article 237 of the Zakon o kreditnim institucijama — but they do so as flat PDF listings under 'Izvještaji' / 'O nama' / 'Odnosi sa javnošću', not through anything resembling a Western investor-relations function; there are no earnings calls, decks or IR calendars to collect. The local vocabulary is decisive: 'javno objelodanjivanje podataka' and its variant 'javno objavljivanje podataka' are the Pillar 3 equivalents, and Basel-style English or 'Stub 3' queries return essentially nothing. Depth and structure vary widely — Hipotekarna, Erste and Addiko offer 15+ year archives with predictable file paths, while NLB hides history behind JS accordions and Ziraat's report page is unreachable from its own homepage — so per-bank deep-link seeds plus the CBCG per-bank financial-statement archive (enumerable as .../fin_izv/god{YYYY}/{bank}_{YYYY}.pdf) should be run as a paired primary-and-fallback collection. Two fetch failures (CKB TLS chain error, one Hipotekarna 404 whose parent listing works) are recorded as unverified hypotheses, not dead sources.

Montenegro's statutory filing landscape is centralised but only half-open: CRPS (pretraga.crps.me / eFirma) is the authoritative company and beneficial-owner register and is publicly searchable, while the annual financial statements themselves are filed to the Uprava prihoda i carina via the TaxisPortal/ePrijava system with no confirmed free public per-company lookup. The practical consequence is a two-track collection design — registry identity and ownership scraped or licensed from CRPS/OpenCorporates, and actual financials bought from local redistributors (bilansi.me, boniteti.me, companywall.me) or requested under freedom-of-information. MONSTAT supplies the aggregate layer via its statistical business register and yearbook, though NACE-K financial-sector aggregates appear to originate with the central bank rather than the statistics office. Two live risks: the CRPS portal failed TLS negotiation in this session (unverified, likely bot-defended rather than dead), and a new Zakon o računovodstvu in force since 8 August 2025 may have changed filing and disclosure rules relative to most documentation found.

Montenegro's banking and payments sector is unusually well covered by supranational assessors: the European Commission's annual enlargement report (Chapter 9 financial services, Chapter 4 capital/payments) and the ERP/ECOFIN economic-dialogue cycle set reform benchmarks, the IMF's annual Article IV provides the quantitative banking diagnostic plus forward-looking supervisory and AML messages, and MONEYVAL's fifth-round evaluation and annual follow-ups drive the AML compliance agenda for banks and PSPs. The World Bank, EBRD and EIB add strategy-level and survey-level views — notably the 2015 joint FSAP technical note on payment and settlement infrastructure, EBRD country strategies and Transition Report assessments, and the semi-annual Vienna-Initiative CESEE Bank Lending Survey with a Montenegro slice. All of this is published in English on stable institutional domains with permissive reuse terms, making the cell highly automatable via annual/semi-annual scheduled fetches and the World Bank document APIs; the main weaknesses are the decade-old FSAP baseline, the absence of any payments-specific supranational assessment covering the SEPA/TIPS-Clone era, and the complete lack of sr-language primary documents, which forces local-language monitoring onto national media relays and the eu.me portal.

Montenegro's banking press landscape is small, concentrated and highly derivative: two specialist portals — Bankar.me and Investitor.me — plus the public broadcaster RTCG carry almost all substantive bank-by-bank earnings, fee and licensing reporting, and they build it directly on CBCG data, typically weeks before any English-language pickup. The general dailies (Vijesti, Pobjeda, Dan) and the high-volume portals (CdM, Aktuelno, Borba, Kodex) add investigative angles and act as fast mirrors for bank and ministry press releases, with much of that copy apparently sourced from the MINA national wire, so first-seen timestamping and duplicate detection matter more here than raw source count. Local-language (sr) search was decisively more productive than English across every query pair, and RTCG's numeric article archive plus Monitor's PDF back-issues are the only two channels found that support genuine historical backfill of fee-change and profit timelines. Two caveats shape collection design: Vijesti — the outlet most likely to break supervision and fee stories — was invisible to search in this run and must be crawled directly, and payment-fee coverage is episodic enough that press monitoring alone will not catch tariff changes without pairing it against CBCG's fee-comparability register and the banks' own tariff pages.

Montenegro's indirect weak-signal layer is small but unusually legible: hiring signals concentrate in three tiers — the state bureau ZZZCG, the banking trade portal Bankar.me which republishes bank vacancy notices verbatim, and a handful of bank-owned career pages (CKB, including its TalentLyft ATS board, and Prva banka), with commercial boards (Zaposli.ME, posao.crna.gora.me, Prekoveze, Careerjet) as a corroboration net. Event and sponsorship signals cluster around INFINTECH at Porto Montenegro, MFM Conference in Podgorica and IT SPOT, with the regional Technobank exhibition in Belgrade and the Balkan Payment Forum showing which vendors (notably Payten/Asseco SEE) are courting Western Balkan banks. Recognition and innovation signals come from international award programmes reported through ME media (Global Finance, Euromoney, The Banker) and from the CBCG FinTech Hub hackathon, co-organised with ICT Cortex and the Association of Banks — the latter being the clearest map of the domestic vendor bench (Logate and peers) that ME banks actually buy from. The main structural weaknesses are that LinkedIn, the single richest channel, is ToS-restricted for automated collection; that most ME banks beyond CKB have no discoverable career page; and that non-bank payment institutions are entirely unmapped in this pass.

Montenegro's complaint/enforcement channel rests on two publishing institutions: the Agency for Protection of Competition (AZZK, azzk.me), which posts full-text merger, cartel and dominance decisions plus sectoral analyses as PDFs, and the Central Bank (CBCG), whose Service for the Protection of Users of Financial Services handles complaint escalation and publishes fee-comparability data, complaint reporting bylaws and an annual consumer-protection chapter. Judicial review and commercial litigation are reachable through the sudovi.me court portal (Upravni sud for challenges to AZZK/CBCG acts, Privredni sud for bank disputes and insolvency), supplemented by the market inspectorate's annual reports on gov.me and the consumer NGO CEZAP as an early-warning layer. Two structural weaknesses stand out: there is no dedicated financial ombudsman, and no interchange or card-fee dispute could be found, so payments economics must be reconstructed from the 2021 interbank-fee law, bank tariff sheets and CBCG fee disclosures rather than from adversarial proceedings. Per-institution complaint statistics appear to be collected but not published, making a freedom-of-information request to CBCG the highest-value next action.

For Montenegro, the archive/aggregator layer is unusually load-bearing: individual bank IR pages are shallow and volatile, so the durable copies of historic disclosures sit either with the regulator (CBCG's 'Finansijski izvještaji banaka' and publication archive, which itself reorganises paths) or in the Internet Archive, making Wayback CDX enumeration plus active Save-Page-Now capture the core collection tactic. Independent normalisation comes mainly from outside the country — IMF Article IV/FSI tables, EMIS and TheBanks.eu for bank financials, and the international rating agencies for the sovereign — nearly all of the commercial ones behind registration or subscription, with free ratings aggregators (countryeconomy, FXEmpire, TheGlobalEconomy) serving as the practical event-detection substitute. Domestically, a government open-data portal with a 'Finansije' category exists but its canonical host is unconfirmed, and the CRPS company registry is search-open yet fee-gated for documents. No Montenegrin-domiciled rating agency and no public bank-level ratings for ME subsidiaries were found, so sector verification must lean on CBCG supervisory aggregates and parent-group disclosures.

Montenegro's listed-issuer disclosure channel is real but thin and technically primitive, and it runs on two institutions: Montenegroberza AD (correct domain mnse.me — NOT the repurposed montenegroberza.com, which is now unrelated SEO content) and the Komisija za tržište kapitala (scmn.me), with CKDD (ckdd.me) as the depository holding the share register. In practice all exchange-side issuer information — listing-committee decisions, admissions, capital reductions, delistings, monthly bulletins — flows through one undifferentiated Montenegrin-language news stream at /code/navigate.asp?Id=122, while the regulator contributes machine-readable issuer registers as dated .xls files, approved prospectus PDFs under /images/novosti/, and an annual capital-market report. The intended canonical repository, CRPI/scmnreporting.me, could not be entered (bare shell, certificate alias mismatch, 404 at root) and shareholder lists at CKDD are request-and-fee gated, so periodic reports and ownership structure must currently be reconstructed from individual bank IR sites (CKB quarterly management reports, Hipotekarna banka reports and prospectuses, Prva banka offering documents) supplemented by specialist local press (investitor.me, bankar.me). There are no RSS feeds or APIs anywhere in this cell; collection means Montenegrin-language HTML/PDF/XLS scraping with entity resolution over free-text announcement titles, and English-only search would have missed nearly all of it.

Montenegro's payments population is small, regulator-centric and almost entirely non-disclosing: the Central Bank of Montenegro is the single indispensable source, holding the payment-institution register (seven licensed entities, each with a downloadable registration PDF), the currently empty e-money register, the withdrawn-authorisation list, PSP tariff overviews and quarterly payment-services/e-money statistics. Almost no ME payment institution publishes press releases, annual reports or investor material, so corporate-disclosure coverage must be reconstructed from CBCG register PDFs, CRPS company filings, and the handful of actor sites that do exist (Payspot ME, Montenegro Transfers/Alta group, Capital Transfer MNE via social channels only). The acquiring and card-processing half of the cell has no domestic standalone processor: it is visible through bank acquiring pages (CKB claims the largest POS network, Hipotekarna for e-commerce) and through regional vendor announcements from Payten/Asseco SEE and Monri. Structural change is coming from policy rather than from the actors — SEPA operational membership and PSD2-style alignment — which the CBCG FinTech Hub, EU delegation communications and local wires (MINA Business, Borba) surface earliest.

Montenegro's payment scheme/infrastructure layer is unusually well-documented at the top of the stack and thin below it: the Central Bank of Montenegro (CBCG) is simultaneously the RTGS/DNS operator, the instant-payment (TIPS Clone) operator, the statistics publisher and the SEPA counterparty, so cbcg.me carries the bulk of authoritative disclosures in Montenegrin, with English translations lagging. Upstream, three non-national institutions produce the roadmap signal that CBCG then localises: Banca d'Italia (TIPS Clone operator, onboarding windows), the European Payments Council (SEPA scheme scope, participant register, EPC409-09 country list) and the World Bank/European Commission (financing and indicator targets for the modernization project). Card-scheme coverage is the weak leg — neither Visa nor Mastercard runs a Montenegro press feed, so ME items must be filtered out of regional sites (visasoutheasteurope.com, mastercard.rs Serbian pages) and corroborated through issuing banks; no domestic card switch or processing house was found. Local trade press (bankar.me, investitor.me) is the fastest early-warning layer for go-live timing and fee parameters, but press-reported scheme parameters conflict and must be resolved against CBCG's own rulebook and the Official Gazette, which remain to be pinned down.

Montenegro's non-bank payments scene is small and highly concentrated: two named challengers dominate the public record — PrestoPay (first CBCG-licensed PSD2 payment institution, Oct 2024, with multibanking, P2P and QR bill payment) and 2bi's Digitalni Kiosk (positioned as the country's first wallet, ~60k users, parking/top-up/biller rails). Because these players publish no regulatory filings, the earliest signals live in three channels: their own sites and app-store release notes, the CBCG payment-institution and e-money registers (which enumerate licensed challengers before any press coverage), and a thin local media/cluster layer (Digitalizuj.Me, Bankar.me, RTCG, ICT Cortex's Fintech Committee, NTP's Startup Index). The merchant-facing layer is a separate, under-mapped cohort of e-fiscalisation/POS ISVs (eFiskal, Sekas, Pošta CG PostFiskal, Telekom's AdeoPOS bundle) where card-acceptance and QR integrations are actually decided. Main risks to collection: sr-language queries leak heavily into Serbian and Croatian markets, several vendors announce only on LinkedIn/Facebook behind login walls, and the one available startup aggregator is dominated by forex/crypto shells domiciled in Montenegro rather than genuine domestic payment challengers.

Montenegro's public-procurement route to banking and payments intelligence is anchored on one mandatory national system, CEJN (cejn.gov.me), which by law carries every stage from plan to concluded contract for all budget entities — but it did not yield content to a plain fetch in this run, so item-level discovery will likely require a headless browser or its underlying API rather than web search, which surfaced no individual Montenegrin banking or POS tender at all. Around that core sit high-value satellites: the procurement review commission's decision register at kontrola-nabavki.me (naming bidders and disputed specifications), DRI audit reports that itemise treasury banking and payment costs, and the buyer pages of CBCG and Pošta Crne Gore, the two institutions whose purchases are most reliably payment-relevant. The IFI layer is currently the richest forward pipeline: the World Bank's EUR 8m Financial Infrastructure Modernization / SEPA 'TIPS clone' operation implies a multi-year sequence of instant-payment procurement packages published across Finances One and STEP, while EBRD notices moved wholesale to ECEPP and EBRD project disclosures name the Montenegrin counterparty banks (NLB Podgorica, CKB, Alter Modus) whose expansion precedes technology buying. Commercial aggregators (nabavke.com, javne-nabavke.com, eKapija) are useful redundancy and provide market denominators, but are paywalled and must be verified against the CEJN original before anything is treated as fact.

Montenegro's vendor-side pointer landscape is thin but real, and it is concentrated in the ASEE/Payten orbit: Payten's news feed carries a named SoftPOS go-live at Hipotekarna banka, MoneyGet operates an ME ATM network, and E-mon Podgorica plus bank T&C PDFs (e.g. Ziraat ME) attribute the e-banking stack to Asseco SEE. Beyond that, the traces are ancillary rather than core — Qualys on CKB for security, Agilcon/Salesforce and Gecko HRM on Lovćen banka, One Crna Gora on Hipotekarna's data centre, and local integrators Logate, Čikom and Softing publishing bank-facing references. No public vendor reference of any kind names the core-banking platform of a Montenegrin bank, and no AML vendor names an ME client, so those layers must be reached indirectly via parent-group IR, regulator publications, and technical fingerprints (app package namespaces, T&C PDFs, login-page footers). The practical collection design for this cell is a site-scoped monthly crawl of roughly fifteen vendor domains with an ME-bank-name filter, rather than open-web querying, which returns directory noise for the .me space.

Montenegro's banking sector is overwhelmingly foreign-owned, so the richest, fastest and most comparable Montenegro-level KPIs live in parent-group IR material rather than in local filings: OTP Group (CKB, market leader) and NLB Group (NLB Banka Podgorica) both break Montenegro out as a named segment in quarterly presentations and conference-call decks, while Erste Group and Addiko Group disclose Montenegro through country tables and CRD country-by-country annexes inside their annual reports, and Ziraat Bankasi covers Ziraat Bank Montenegro in its integrated annual report. On the payments side, Asseco SEE's Payment Solutions (Payten) reporting is the main group-level window on Montenegrin card processing and ATM/POS services. Hipotekarna banka's parent AikGroup (Belgrade) is the weakest-covered major link - Serbian-language IR and securities-commission filings must be crawled to close it - and several banks (Universal Capital, Zapad, Prva banka, Adriatic, First Financial) have no reporting foreign parent at all, so this cell structurally cannot cover them. Group IR is English-dominant; local-language material exists only at subsidiary statutory level (CBCG per-bank statements, nlb.me, ckb.me, lovcenbanka.me) and is best used to reconcile group segment figures.

Montenegro's association layer for banking and payments is small, concentrated and almost entirely open-web. Two bodies carry most of the value: Udruženje banaka Crne Gore (ubcg.info - not ubcg.me), which publishes the 11-member bank roster, the Bankar magazine and a legislation tracker, and Privredna komora Crne Gore (komora.me), whose banking/finance/insurance board lists ~23 named institution representatives spanning banks, insurers, the central bank and development institutions, with its monthly Glasnik PDF series acting as the de facto record of board sessions. Around them sit ICT Cortex (the tech cluster that supplies the payments-vendor population and co-hosts CBCG fintech events), the MFIC White Book (the densest published lobbying text on the banking regime, but bot-defended behind a 403), the employers' union UPCG, AmCham, the insurers' bureau NBOCG and the accountancy institutes. Two structural caveats for collection: no association publishes formal committee minutes, so Glasnik plus PKCG announcements plus bankar.me coverage are the substitute; and there is no dedicated fintech or payment-services trade body in Montenegro, so payment-industry positions must be triangulated from UBCG, the chamber's ICT board and ICT Cortex together.

For Slovenia, the global platform vendors do publish their wins, but sparsely: the one strong confirmed anchor is Temenos, whose success-story and newsroom pages name NLB (core upgrade to T24/UniversalSuite R17, retail migration following N Banka) — corroborated independently by FinTech Futures, which also names the SI payments-infrastructure deal between Bankart and Nets. Backbase, Mambu, Finastra, Thought Machine, Finacle and FLEXCUBE surfaced no SI-named reference in this pass and should be treated as monitoring targets, not as evidence of absence. The richer, denser layer is local: HRC (Hibis) names Addiko, Delavska hranilnica and Gorenjska banka as clients, SETCCE enumerates NLB, Sparkasse, Gorenjska banka and Delavska hranilnica in Slovene, and Halcom's bank listings map the corporate e-banking channel bank-by-bank — these local vendor reference pages are the best available public stack map of the SI market. Two indirect channels round it out: buyer-side newsrooms (NLB Group, OTP banka's merger IT-migration releases) that date and validate vendor claims, and Slovene press on failed migrations (BKS Bank) that exposes core replacements no vendor advertises.

Slovenia's payments vendor layer is unusually concentrated: Bankart d.o.o., bank-owned since 1997, is simultaneously the national card issuing/acquiring processor, the ATM/POS estate manager, the payment gateway and PSD2 operator, and the Bank of Slovenia-authorised operator of the SIMP-PS (SEPA) and BIPS (instant) systems on which the Flik scheme rides — making bankart.si plus the Banka Slovenije registers the two anchor sources for this cell. The second visible tier is foreign-owned and traceable mainly through corporate registries rather than press releases: Nexi Slovenija d.o.o. (formerly Intesa Sanpaolo Card, then Nets CEE) processes cards from Ljubljana, and Nets is documented as the technology supplier behind Bankart's central SCT/SCT Inst infrastructure. Below the processor tier, evidence gets indirect — acquirer merchant pages and general-terms PDFs, integrator case studies (SRC's PSD2 platform), core-banking suppliers (HRC/Hibis) and the eNaročanje procurement portal are the practical routes to supplier identity. The single most important unknown is the card management/switch/tokenisation platform actually running inside Bankart and the SI banks: no public source found in this pass names it, and Visa/Mastercard certification news for Slovenia appears to live in scheme partner directories rather than announcements.

Slovenia has no standalone national payments trade fair, so vendor visibility in this cell is split three ways: association events (ZBS Bančna konferenca at Brdo and the members-only posvetovanje o kartičnem poslovanju) that publish speakers but not sponsors; general IT and security conferences (Dnevi slovenske informatike, INFOSEK, NT konferenca, CIO Summit) that do publish tiered sponsor and exhibitor lists and where bank-serving integrators buy presence; and the regional Money Motion in Zagreb, co-founded by ASEE and Monri and sponsored by Nexi, Mastercard, OTP and Payten, which functions as the de facto Adriatic payments expo. Award programmes (DIGGIT, WEBSI, Netko, GZS priznanja za inovacije) are the strongest disclosed vendor-client evidence, since entries name the client, the agency and the technology partner together — DIGGIT even carries an explicit financial-services/insurance/banking category. The main collection risks are logo-image sponsor walls, per-edition deletion of rosters (only DSI keeps clean per-year subdomains), and the fact that the single highest-signal cards event is a closed member consultation best reconstructed from the ZBS terminski plan and annual report.

Slovenian-language press on bank vendor deals is a narrow but high-signal field: one business daily (Finance.si) and one wire (STA) hold most of the deal detail but are both paywalled and near-invisible to open web search, while the free layer - Delo and Dnevnik gospodarstvo, Racunalniske novice, Monitor/Monitor Pro - carries vendor-named project write-ups that are frequently advertorial in origin. The strongest independent find in this pass is investigative outlet Necenzurirano.si, whose documented reporting on the failed BKS Bank core-system migration surfaces exactly the implementation friction that neither bank nor supplier publishes; Zurnal24, Siol.net and 24ur corroborate and date such events. Critically, procurement-vocabulary searches return nothing - Slovenian coverage of supplier changes appears almost exclusively as customer-harm and outage reporting, so collection keyword sets must target failure symptoms alongside contract terms. Recommended next steps are a Finance.si subscription, an STA or clipping-service feed, a full Slovene glossary sweep, and extension to regional outlets and newspaper archives.

For Slovenian banking/payments vendor news, the English-language trade press is genuinely load-bearing rather than supplementary: FinTech Futures (which maintains a Slovenia keyword feed and carried the Oracle/SID Banka and Bankart/Nets stories), Finextra (both editorial and a vendor press-release channel) and The Paypers are the three sources that reliably surface deals, and they are frequently the only public record of them. SeeNews is the regional newswire of record but skews to M&A, ownership and financing rather than technology procurement — valuable as an early-warning signal that a bank is changing hands and will re-tender, not as a vendor-win feed. IBS Intelligence sits behind this as the only systematic vendor-deal register (Sales League Table, Core Banking Landscape reports), while STA English and Bankart's own newsroom form the sl-to-en bridge where announcements originate. A Slovenian-language search using procurement phrasing returned nothing, which corroborates the cell rationale but also flags that the sl anchors need reworking before concluding local coverage is absent.

Slovenia's indirect vendor-signal landscape is unusually concentrated: openbanking.bankart.si acts as a multi-tenant PSD2 hub for most domestic banks (Gorenjska banka, Sparkasse, Delavska hranilnica, OTP SI), while NLB, Addiko, Intesa Sanpaolo and UniCredit run their own or group-level developer portals — so which portal a bank uses is itself a vendor disclosure. Two concrete weak signals surfaced in this pass: a Bankart-hosted API product described as the 'SRC PSD2 API Collection', and an NLB-group Android package id containing 'asseco'. On the recruitment side, Bankart's own job ads are the richest named-technology source found, with Optius, MojeDelo, the state ZRSZ vacancy register, Careerjet and bank careers pages as the collection layer; LinkedIn is the highest-value but access-restricted channel. The main gaps are the absence of any public status page for SI payment infrastructure, no technical-fingerprinting routine yet, and no archival guarantee for ephemeral job ads and release notes.

Competitive-structure intelligence for Slovenia rests on a compact set of legally-mandated registers: AVK merger decisions, Banka Slovenije/ECB qualifying-holding authorisations, ATVP takeover permits and SEOnet regulated disclosures, with SDH/SID covering state disposals and AJPES confirming the resulting ownership. Group-level moves (OTP/Nova KBM/SKB, NLB's Addiko bid, EU-dimension processor deals) surface first in foreign parent newsrooms and EC DG COMP cases, ahead of any Slovenian-language channel. The weakest link is small payment-vendor ownership change, which falls below notification thresholds and is only visible via AJPES/aggregator diffs. Slovenian-language terminology (priglasitev koncentracije, prevzemna ponudba, kvalificiran delež, prodaja deleža, statusne spremembe) is essential — English-only querying misses the primary registers entirely.

Slovenia's ATM and self-service competitive arena is almost entirely undocumented at the transaction level - commercial banks are not contracting authorities, so no ATM tenders or supplier contracts are ever published, and no Slovenian bank was found naming its hardware or service supplier. Intelligence therefore has to be reconstructed from three usable layers: an unusually good installed-base observation layer (ATMinfo.si tracking ~1,046 ATMs per bank in near-real time, upstream of it a Bankart open-banking feed, and per-bank locators that flag deposit-capable units), a local-channel layer where competitors do reveal themselves (Demi Trade servicing NCR and Diebold fleets and distributing CIMA, with NLB/NKBM/SKB/UniCredit shown as customers; Biroservis Majer for cash-handling brands; Euronet running its own independent fleet), and an official layer at Banka Slovenije, which publishes the authoritative counts (1,284 ATMs at end-H2 2024, down 1.9%, ~74% contactless) and mandates reporting by ATM network operators. The decisive forward driver is the Ministry of Finance draft act on payment instruments now in public consultation, which would force cash-point coverage - an ATM within 5 km of any settlement over 1,000 people, with Banka Slovenije able to order a named bank to install one - reversing a shrinking estate into mandated deployment. Global OEM sites are weak for this cell: Glory, Auriga and KEBA show no Slovenian trace, Diebold Nixdorf's regional locations directory appears to have moved, and their local presence must be established via AJPES, distributor catalogues and installed-base evidence rather than their own web properties.

Slovenia's bank-supplier landscape is documented almost entirely on the suppliers' own Slovenian-language sites, and the single richest find is Hrc informacijski inženiring, whose Hibis core-banking platform pages publicly name its bank and savings-bank clients (NLB, OTP banka, Gorenjska banka, Deželna banka Slovenije, Addiko, BKS, Delavska hranilnica, LON, Primorska hranilnica Vipava, RCI Banque, plus HR and RS institutions) and carry datable migration case studies. Alongside it sit Halcom (whose support FAQ effectively publishes its installed base of banks), SRC (banking & finance vertical), Bankart (the bank-owned domestic card and SEPA/instant-payments processor, and therefore both incumbent competitor and gatekeeper), and a second tier of document/e-archive and analytics houses (ZZI/bizBox, Genis, Mikrografija, IN516HT). Because commercial banks here are private and silent, the reliable cross-checks are structural rather than editorial: si.openprocurements.com and the official eJN portal for Banka Slovenije tenders, bizi.si/companywall.si/AJPES for entity and scale resolution, and the ZBS and GZS ZIT association pages as actor-enumeration pointers. The main unexploited seam is vendor PARTNER pages and job advertisements, which are where a domestic integrator inadvertently names the foreign platform behind a deal the bank never announced.

Slovenia's payment-infrastructure layer is unusually concentrated and unusually well-documented by regulators rather than by the operators themselves: Bankart d.o.o. — bank-owned, running card processing plus the SIMP-PS, BIPS and Flik rails — is the single hub, and Banka Slovenije is the authoritative publisher of who operates and participates in each system (upravljavci placilnih sistemov register, National Payments Council materials, annual payment/settlement reports), while also directly operating SISBON and SISBIZ. The key structural finding is that Bankart publishes no partner, client or reference list at all, so its vendor graph must be reconstructed indirectly — from supplier-side case studies (IBM, Nets/Nexi via trade press), owner-bank disclosures (NLB Group), its own /aktualno announcements (e.g. Rekono), job ads, and public-money trails on ERAR and enarocanje.si. The highest-value unresolved pointer is the Flik participant-bank list on flik.si, which every bank and Bankart page defers to and which maps the entire instant-payments participant set. All Slovenian-language searching was productive and mandatory here: the operator, register and scheme vocabulary (upravljavec, udelezenci, placilni promet, procesni center) surfaces material that English queries do not reach.

Slovenia's non-bank payments competition is unusually well documented at the licensing layer: Banka Slovenije's EMI and EEA-passporting registers give an authoritative, diffable universe of local and cross-border PSPs/EMIs, with Paywiser as the flagship domestic licensed EMI. The commercial layer is thin and fragmented — vendor sites (mBills, VALÚ, Paywiser, Eligma/GoCrypto, Bankart/Flik) plus telco press releases carry most partnership and KPI disclosure, since the largest acquirer (Nexi) appears to have no Slovenian newsroom and speaks only through the group and partner banks. Local-language trade and business media (Monitor, Računalniške novice, Forbes Slovenija, Delo) are the practical discovery channel for market entries and merchant tie-ups that English sources never report, and community forums add weak-signal detail on acceptance friction. Priority follow-ups are the gov.si virtual-currency provider register, vendor developer/integration documentation, and clarifying the conflicting signals about mBills' continued operation.

Slovenia's buyer side is a small, highly consolidated field — roughly a dozen banks and savings banks, best enumerated from the Banka Slovenije supervised-entity register and the ZBS membership rather than from search. Every significant institution publishes go-lives in Slovenian on its own site, but in three different shapes: NLB runs a proper dated press-release archive, OTP banka and Gorenjska banka use flat news/article slugs, and Addiko, Delavska hranilnica and the smaller hranilnice bury the same information in 'obvestila za stranke' and dated terms-and-conditions PDFs. The dominant limitation is that these announcements describe the customer benefit and almost never name the technology partner, so this cell should be treated as the authoritative source of date, scope and institution, to be joined against vendor case studies, annual-report outsourcing disclosures and public procurement for attribution. Highest-value additions next are the shared national rails (Bankart, Flik, SI-PASS/Rekono), annual-report IT sections, and Wayback captures of the retired Nova KBM, SKB, Abanka and Sberbank newsrooms before that history disappears.

Slovenia's procurement trace for banking and payments is unusually well laid out: enarocanje.si (Portal javnih naročil, operated by Uradni list) is the statutory publication point for award notices, e-JN runs the live tender pipeline, and TED duplicates all above-threshold awards EU-wide with a free Search API. Crucially, the Ministry of Public Administration republishes the same material as bulk open data on OPSI (notices, award decisions, published contracts, active tenders, annual statistics) and as the Statist analytics app covering awards since 2013, so a durable collector should read the datasets and treat the HTML portals as verification rather than scraping targets. Two indirect layers close the gaps that notices leave: ERAR (KPK) exposes every public-sector payment to a named payee since 2003, catching below-threshold and maintenance spend, and DKOM review decisions expose competitor names, prices and technical arguments that sanitised award notices omit. Key buyers to filter on are Banka Slovenije (which routes everything through enarocanje.si), SID banka, UJP/Ministrstvo za finance — where a confirmed 2020 award contracted NLB for e-Plačila card and online-banking acceptance — plus municipalities and state-owned enterprises such as Pošta Slovenije, with Bankart useful for resolving a named bank back to the actual processing and terminal vendor chain.

Slovenia's actor universe for banking and payments is definable almost entirely from statutory registers, split across three authorities plus two EU layers: Banka Slovenije publishes the master Register subjektov nadzora with 14 licence categories (banks, savings banks, EEA credit institutions and branches, payment institutions, e-money institutions, AISPs, money changers, payment system operators, CSDs) plus dedicated sub-registers for payment institutions, e-money institutions and EEA firms passporting into Slovenia; ATVP covers investment firms, tied agents and MiCA CASPs; AJPES holds the business register (searchable by SKD activity and by connected persons), the transaction-account register RTR, annual reports and the eObjave registration feed, with a free twice-monthly open-data extract on podatki.gov.si. The EBA central PSD2 register and credit-institutions register plus the ECB SSM list are indispensable for the inbound passported firms that never appear in Slovenian media. Machine-readability is uneven: AJPES/OPSI offers clean bulk files and RTR offers a paid XML service, while the Banka Slovenije and ATVP registers appear to require HTML fetch-and-diff, and no register at all enumerates the technology-vendor side, which must be approximated via SKD codes and other cells. Several EU-level URLs here are JS applications and were not fetched in this run, so treat their liveness as unverified rather than assumed.

Slovenia's association layer splits cleanly into a demand-side pointer and several supply-side pointers: ZBS (zbs-giz.si) publishes the definitive roster of banks, savings banks and leasing companies, while GZS's ICT association ZIT publishes a dated, downloadable member-list PDF that is the single richest enumeration of Slovenian ICT vendors, complemented by its sections, board and position-paper pages. Cross-sector chambers — AmCham Slovenia (400+ members, open list), AHK Slovenija (~280 members) and BSCC (~100 members plus published councils) — surface the international vendors that consider Slovenia a market but never appear in local ICT rosters. A second tier of innovation-policy bodies (Slovenska digitalna koalicija, DIH Slovenije/EDIH DIGI-SI, IKT horizontalna mreža SRIP PMiS) and the DSI conference sponsor tiers provide dated, ranked evidence of vendor commitment and bank–vendor co-appearance. The main structural gaps are the absence of any dedicated Slovenian fintech association, probable membership gating on GZS and AHK company data, and the unharvested event/committee programmes where actual bank–vendor working relationships are exposed.

CBCG is unambiguously the creation point for Montenegrin banking and payment statistics: as designated official producer under the Law on Official Statistics it compiles monetary statistics to ECB/IMF methodology and publishes on an advance release calendar. The landscape splits into four layers — machine-readable statistical releases (monthly MFI ZIP, monthly interest rates, monthly RTGS/DNS payment traffic, quarterly financial soundness indicators, and the quarterly payment services and e-money datasets that carry cards, ATM/POS acceptance devices and cash deposit/withdrawal transactions), narrative publications (Bilten, macroeconomic report, financial stability report, bank lending survey), supervisory disclosure (per-bank quarterly and audited financial statements, tariff comparisons), and pointer infrastructure (release calendar, registers, reporting-form annexes, sitemap). Everything is open-access, free and in ZIP or PDF form — there is no API, no SDMX endpoint and no series-level query interface, so collection must be scheduled file harvesting driven by the release calendar with parsers over spreadsheets and PDFs. The main risks are dated filenames that rotate each period, interchangeable URL prefixes that make single-fetch liveness verdicts unreliable, and three known discontinuities (the 2017/2018 payment-traffic methodology break, the mid-2025 Bilten restructuring, and the postponed Q1 2026 FSI release).

Montenegro's payment-system statistics landscape is almost entirely centralised in the Central Bank of Montenegro (CBCG), which publishes a monthly RTGS/DNS realised-payment-traffic release, an annual aggregate, a quarterly ECB-style payment services and e-money dataset covering cards, POS terminals, terminal-type transactions and cash deposits/withdrawals, plus the Bilten monthly bulletin and the annual work report that carries the payment-system oversight narrative. Two structural events dominate the current picture: entry into the SEPA schemes' geographical scope with an operational readiness date of 5 October 2025, and the go-live of the national instant payment system (IPS, a TIPS-clone under an EPC licence) on 20 July 2026 with all eleven banks participating from day one — the latter creating a brand-new but very short instant-payments series. Distribution is scrape-only: HTML pages, PDFs and ZIP archives, with no API and a site whose deep URLs have already moved (one widely indexed statistics URL 404s), so collection must be driven off the statutory statistics release calendar and re-resolved from the statistics index each run. The main weaknesses of the cell are single-publisher concentration — no bank association, no domestic card scheme, no independent national aggregator was found — and the absence of any official SEPA-migration indicator, which is best proxied by counting Montenegrin adherents in the EPC scheme registers, with IMF FAS providing the only comparable independent ATM/POS density benchmark.

Montenegro's payment/card/cash figures reach the public mainly through a short chain: CBCG and MONSTAT releases are picked up by the MINA-Business agency wire, then republished near-verbatim by CdM's economy section and reworked by Vijesti, Pobjeda, RTCG and Bankar.me. CdM, MINA and Bankar.me are the highest-value nodes — CdM for its dense, near-press-release CBCG headline stream, MINA for the earliest timestamp, Bankar.me as the only specialist finance outlet producing its own sector analysis; Gradski.me/PG TV demonstrated that granular card and cash transaction breakdowns do circulate locally in prose form. No Montenegrin outlet publishes a structured dataset, so collection here means figure extraction from article text, always reconciled against cbcg.me. The main practical risks are unresolved seed URLs for Vijesti, Pobjeda and Dan, and heavy cross-border noise from Croatian/Serbian/Bosnian sites on shared Serbian-language payment vocabulary, which makes .me domain restriction mandatory.

For Montenegro's payments market, the EU/IFI layer is unusually rich because the country is a first-mover: it entered SEPA geographical scope in November 2024, reached operational SEPA use in October 2025, and hosts the first live Banca d'Italia TIPS-clone instant-payment deployment. The authoritative assessors are therefore the European Commission (annual enlargement report, Chapter 4 closed provisionally in December 2025; plus the Growth Plan Reform Agenda with conditioned, monitored milestones), the European Payments Council (SEPA scheme-country list and the institution-level Register of Participants), and the ECB/Banca d'Italia pair for instant-payments infrastructure. Domestic-language counterparts — eu.me, gov.me/wapi document repository, CBCG press releases and the Parliament's payment-law record — supply the dated legislative and commitment detail that the EU-side documents summarise. The clear weaknesses of this cell are that no EU or IFI publisher issues Montenegro-specific card/ATM/POS or cash-usage series, and the only deep independent payments-infrastructure diagnostic (the World Bank/IMF FSAP financial-infrastructure technical note) dates from the 2015 FSAP update.

Montenegro's analytical layer for payments is thin and concentrated: the CBCG's own 'Radne studije' series is the single most credible domestic research channel, but its visible catalogue is macro/inflation/climate-oriented with no payments-specific paper identified, so the richest analyst-grade evidence is likely to sit in international demand-side surveys (World Bank Global Findex 2025, which covers Montenegro) and in scheme-commissioned consumer research (Visa Pulse) that reaches the public only through Montenegrin media. Domestic academia contributes through the Podgorica-based, Scopus-indexed Montenegrin Journal of Economics (ELIT), the University of Montenegro Faculty of Economics and its PDF thesis store, and UDG — all of which need repository- and index-level sweeps rather than web search, since no faculty payment study surfaced in local-language queries. The think-tank/consultancy tier (ISSP, DeFacto, CEED) plus the UBCG journal 'Bankar' and the bankar.me analysis series form a practitioner analysis layer that reprocesses CBCG data and occasionally runs original fieldwork, though commercial survey output is client-confidential. Highest-value next moves are IFI Montenegro cuts (EBRD/IMF/EIB/wiiw) and bibliographic-index sweeps in Serbian, including Cyrillic.

Montenegro's indirect and weak-signal layer is unusually workable on the pricing and regulator side and unusually hard on the tender side. The Central Bank publishes statutory, repeatedly-updated fee comparison tables (tarife banaka, basic accounts) plus a payment-institution register and a FinTech Hub with strategy and survey documents - all structured, free and high-credibility. Procurement, by contrast, sits behind a JavaScript-rendered portal (cejn.gov.me) with a legacy archive (portalujn.gov.me), a policy directorate (ujn.gov.me) and several commercial aggregators, one of which (etenderi.me) uniquely indexes State Commission appeal decisions that name bidders and prices; private-bank ATM/POS demand surfaces only via SOE buyers such as Posta Crne Gore and EPCG. The job-ad channel is strong and multi-sourced (ZZZCG statutory register, bankar.me sector feed, zaposli.me/careerjet, individual bank career pages), while events and awards are thin - one recurring domestic fintech conference (Infintech, organised by Logate) plus foreign bank-of-the-year titles reported locally. The two structural risks for any collector are the shared-language contamination from Serbian and Bosnian sources, and the CRPS/CeJN naming confusion in the seed terms.

For Montenegrin card, ATM and POS statistics the landscape is strongly centralised: the Central Bank of Montenegro's quarterly 'Podaci o platnim uslugama i elektronskom novcu' series is effectively the only primary producer, publishing cards issued, card-acceptance devices, and card transactions split by terminal type as per-table ZIP/Excel files compiled from mandatory bank reporting under the Odluka o izvještajima o platnom prometu (Sl. list CG 82/17 with 2026 amendments). Around it sit CBCG's monthly Bilten, annual reports and RTGS/DNS/IPS payment-system statistics, plus per-bank tariffs, registers and financial statements that allow a bank-level view the aggregates omit. International redistribution via the IMF Financial Access Survey (mirrored by the World Bank and FRED) supplies comparable ATM/POS density benchmarks, while World Bank FSAP/CGPSS assessments usefully document the series' own gaps. The main blind spot for this cell is the contactless and e-commerce split, which no located official source publishes; that, plus the absence of any machine-readable API and unenumerated archive depth, are the priorities for the next pass.

Montenegro's cash-cycle intelligence is almost entirely concentrated in one institution — CBCG — but is scattered across four unconnected areas of its site: the currency section (banknotes, coins, counterfeiting, unfit-note replacement), the payment-system key-function pages, the publications series (Bilten, Annual Report, Annual Work Report, audited financial statements), and secondary legislation. The single highest-value asset is the Montenegrin-language quarterly 'Podaci o platnim uslugama i elektronskom novcu' ZIP bundle, which carries cash deposit/withdrawal transactions, card-acceptance-device counts and card transactions by terminal type; it is not linked from the English Statistics page, so English-only discovery misses it — local-language search was decisive here. Because Montenegro is unilaterally euroised, there is no national currency-in-circulation series at all and no cash-usage survey; CiC exists only as IMF-style estimates and cash share must be proxied from ATM-versus-POS turnover. The main uncovered layers are the private cash-handling chain (CIT and cash-processing firms), operator-level ATM attribution given a confirmed independent-ATM-deployer presence, and customs-declared cross-border cash inflows.

Montenegro's demand-side statistical landscape is dominated by one institution — MONSTAT (Uprava za statistiku) — which publishes, in mirrored Montenegrin/English HTML index pages backed by PDF and XLSX files, every denominator a payments analysis needs: annual Eurostat-aligned ICT household and enterprise surveys (with e-commerce and online-purchase indicators), monthly retail and wholesale turnover, monthly tourism arrivals and overnights, the multi-year Household Budget Survey, national accounts, CPI, the 2023 census and the statistical business register. Because ~96% of overnight stays are by foreign tourists, the tourism series is not a nice-to-have but the primary normaliser for a seasonal, non-resident-heavy card market. The fiscal side is thinner than the seed query assumed: the Ministry of Finance publishes budget-execution and public-debt reports rather than a named macroeconomic bulletin, while the highest-value asset in the cell — the Revenue and Customs Administration's mandatory e-fiscalization system, which records every cash and cashless invoice in real time since 2021 — publishes no payment-method statistics and would require a free-access-to-information request. Practical collection should key on MONSTAT's saopštenje/novosti archive index and release calendar rather than its unstable numeric page ids, with Eurostat's Montenegro (geo=ME) series and data.gov.me's CKAN catalogue as the machine-readable fallbacks.

Montenegro's supranational statistical footprint is concentrated in three families: the IMF (Financial Access Survey for ATM/POS and account-access density, Financial Soundness Indicators for banking-sector health, and Article IV country reports such as CR 25/304 for narrative and annex tables), the World Bank group (Global Findex 2025 for demand-side card and digital-payment behaviour, its microdata for custom cuts, WDI/GFDD and Data360 re-hosts, plus remittance datasets), and the EU statistical system (Eurostat enlargement and ICT-usage series, where Montenegro reports via MONSTAT under IPA alignment and internet-banking/e-commerce indicators are the best between-Findex proxy). Two expected sources are effectively absent: BIS CPMI Red Book excludes Montenegro because it is not a CPMI member, and the ECB does not compile euro-substitution statistics for a country where the euro is already legal tender, leaving the ECB relevant only through its CBCG cooperation programme and occasional papers. Practical consequences for collection: card-scheme-level transaction statistics have no supranational home for ME and must come from national publishers; the FAS POS-per-100k series for Montenegro is a hypothesis to be tested directly rather than assumed; and local-language material on these bodies is commentary and relationship description, not the data itself, so sr-language effort in this cell should be minimal. Third-party mirrors (DBnomics, FRED) are worth wiring in as redundancy and revision-detection given the IMF data portal's ongoing URL re-platforming.

Montenegro's sector self-reporting layer is centred on one actor, Udruženje banaka Crne Gore (UBCG), whose live property is ubcg.info (not ubcg.me) and whose membership covers all 11 licensed banks — making its member page a high-value pointer into every bank-level disclosure cell. UBCG does not publish a statistical bulletin; its substantive output is the long-running practitioner quarterly 'Bankar' (issues from at least no. 16/2011 to no. 72/2026, all as PDFs under wp-content/uploads), plus annual work programmes/activity reviews, a SEPA section, card-fraud education from its payment-card misuse commission, and a dense news feed of instant-payment and PSD3/IPR workshops. Because association-side numeric aggregates are scarce, the practical statistical yield in this cell comes indirectly: EBF's annual Montenegro Facts & Figures, the Chamber of Economy's banking/finance/insurance board sessions, the INFINTECH conference in Tivat, and the bankar.me trade portal that quotes figures from decks and association statements. Main risks for a collector are the 404 /publikacije/ menu link, image-only PDFs requiring OCR, and the noisy 'UBCG' acronym that also denotes the librarians' association.

Bank-level card, ATM, POS and digital-channel numbers in Montenegro exist almost exclusively inside each bank's own Montenegrin-language annual report ('godišnji izvještaj o poslovanju'), audited statements, tariff schedules ('tarifnik'/'tarife i naknade') and securities prospectuses — no Montenegrin bank publishes an investor presentation. The strongest direct sources are CKB (OTP), NLB Banka, Hipotekarna banka, Erste Bank ME and Lovćen banka, each with a dedicated reports page; tail banks (Ziraat, Universal Capital, Zapad) appear to disclose only via the regulator. Three pointer layers make harvesting tractable: the CBCG registar banaka and its per-bank financial-statement and tariff comparators, the Montenegroberza/SCMN issuer filings (where bond prospectuses carry unusually rich network and card detail), and bankar.me's recurring bank-by-bank sector analyses. POS estate figures remain the weakest link — they surface as marketing claims and locator listings rather than reported series, so bottom-up POS sizing will require triangulation.

Montenegro's non-bank payments landscape is small, fully enumerable, and anchored on one authoritative pointer: the CBCG Register of Payment Institutions, which lists seven licensees (Montenegro Transfers, Capital Transfer MNE, Moneta MNE, PaySpot ME, Alta Pay Group, PrestoPay, and Business integration and business intelligence d.o.o.), while the parallel Register of E-money Institutions is explicitly empty — there is currently no domestic e-money licensee. Quantitatively, the cell rests on CBCG's quarterly 'Podaci o platnim uslugama i elektronskom novcu' release, which covers cards issued, acceptance devices, transactions by service and terminal type, contactless, cross-border card use and cash deposits/withdrawals in downloadable archives, supplemented by the RTGS/DNS payment-system statistics and the newly live SEPA and instant-payment rails. The licensees themselves publish service catalogues, tariffs, agent-network coverage and open-banking bank-connection lists (PrestoPay's multibanking pages are unusually revealing) but no transaction volumes, so firm-level sizing must come from CRPS-filed financial statements and, if located, CBCG's payment-institution financial reports. The main open risks are two register entities with no discoverable web presence, an unscouted card-acquiring and gateway layer, and unresolved group ownership across the two dominant remittance agents.

Montenegro's supplier-and-scheme layer is dominated by two publishing actors: Mastercard, which runs a genuine country programme (MasterIndex, an annual ME cardholder survey since 2009, plus country milestone releases split across mastercard.me and mastercard.rs), and Payten/Asseco SEE, which is simultaneously the terminal and ATM service provider to ME banks, an independent ATM deployer via MoneyGet Crna Gora, and a WSE-listed issuer whose management reports explicitly name Montenegro as a key POS/ATM maintenance market. Visa, by contrast, publishes almost nothing country-specific for ME and is badly obscured in search by travel-visa noise, while AllSecure and Halcom leave no ME-specific press trail at all — AllSecure's Montenegrin footprint is visible only through merchant checkout boilerplate naming it alongside Hipotekarna banka as acquirer. The most productive routes are therefore vendor-to-bank pointers (Payten's SoftPOS-at-Hipotekarna release, Asseco case studies, ASEE filings), the local trade portal bankar.me which republishes vendor and scheme PR that never reaches vendor English sites, and bottom-up harvesting of merchant payment-terms pages and ATM locators. The main unmapped layer is card processing and switching for ME banks, and no ATM hardware OEM was found to name a Montenegrin customer — the hardware channel appears entirely intermediated through Payten.

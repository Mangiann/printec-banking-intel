Hungary's listed/bond-issuing bank IR estate is unusually concentrated and unusually well-documented: OTP alone (otpbank.hu HU IR + otpgroup.info EN IR + OTP Jelzálogbank) supplies annual and integrated reports, quarterly flash reports, presentations, a published financial calendar and a year-indexed announcement archive, making it the single densest node in the landscape. MBH is the second pillar but its material is fragmented across four domains (mbhbank.hu, mbhbank.com, mbhbefektetesibank.hu, mbhjelzalogbank.hu) and distributed in ESEF/signed-ZIP form, with the HUF 500bn base prospectus and the EUR EMTN investor presentations carrying the richest strategy detail; GRÁNIT, listed on BÉT since November 2024, adds a compact but complete HU-only challenger-bank disclosure set. Two statutory funnels — the BÉT KIBINFO disclosure database (stable /newkibdata PDF paths) and the MNB-run OAM at kozzetetelek.mnb.hu — catch everything these issuers file and should anchor the collection design, with issuer sites used for presentations and richer formatting. The main open work is mechanical rather than discovery: mapping the OAM and BÉT per-issuer query endpoints, and sweeping the foreign-parent Hungarian subsidiaries that also issue bonds.

The Hungarian statutory-disclosure estate of foreign-owned unlisted bank subsidiaries is real but deliberately unadvertised: each bank keeps a thin cluster of Hungarian-only pages — 'nyilvánosságra hozandó információk' (K&H), 'hivatalalos-kozzetetelek' and 'éves jelentések' under vállalatirányítás (Erste), 'pénzügyi adatok / jelentések' inside the press room (Raiffeisen), a DAM PDF tree under Rólunk/befektetők (UniCredit), year-structured document paths with no index (CIB), and a single 'pénzügyi adatok' page (KDB) — with filename patterns that are highly enumerable once found. The two branch-model players, Oberbank HU and Cofidis, publish essentially nothing financial themselves, so the register layer is not optional: e-beszámoló (Igazságügyi Minisztérium) is the authoritative deposit point for every entity, kozzetetelek.mnb.hu mirrors bank documents that are WAF-blocked at source, and the MNB Aranykönyv gives institution-level balance sheet and P&L lines as structured xlsx/time series — the single highest-leverage source in the cell. Practical risks are mechanical rather than legal: kh.hu returned 403 to automated fetching (bot defence, presumed live), several institutions have no verified index page behind their PDFs, and the pointer portals use opaque ids or form-driven search. English coverage is thin to the point of unusable — Hungarian query terms are load-bearing for this whole cell.

Hungary's association layer is unusually well-suited to source seeding: Magyar Bankszövetség publishes an open, ~67-entry member directory with per-institution detail pages alongside a PDF report archive, and it is mirrored by sub-sector registers at Magyar Lízingszövetség (~36 members plus a statistics series back to 2000), Magyar Faktoring Szövetség, MAKISZ, MABISZ (whose Magyar Biztosítók Évkönyve is the closest thing to a true sector yearbook), HVCA and the Magyar Fintech Szövetség. Together these constitute a near-complete curated register of credible HU banks, leasing houses, factoring firms, insurers and fintech vendors, with IVSZ and AmCham adding the technology-supplier and foreign-branch actors the domestic bodies under-represent. The GVH decision archive (1997–2026, searchable by party and case number) is the strongest indirect-disclosure source in the cell, surfacing market-share and pricing detail from bank merger clearances that appears nowhere else. Main caveats: the Bankszövetség periodic sector report series appears to stop at 2018, and the two most valuable fintech/ICT member rosters (hunfintech.com, ivsz.hu) are likely JS-rendered and need a headless collector before they can be enumerated.

Hungarian financial trade press functions as a genuine aggregation and timestamping layer over bank disclosures rather than mere commentary: Portfolio.hu (bank, befektetés and elemzés desks, plus its recurring bank/insurer league table keyed to the 31 May annual-accounts deadline) is the anchor, with Világgazdaság, hvg.hu, Economx, Napi.hu and Index providing same-day corroboration and, crucially, coverage of unlisted and foreign-owned subsidiaries that publish nothing in English. A second tier of comparison/analytics shops (Bankmonitor, Bank360) and the Magyar Bankszövetség press feed sits upstream of much of this reporting and is worth tracking as an early-signal layer, while Forbes.hu's Bank Power Ranking supplies a rare structured read on digital and payments maturity that no regulator publishes. English coverage is thin — Budapest Business Journal is the practical bridge — and no press-maintained earnings calendar was found, so date discovery must lean on 'konszenzus' preview articles or on primary exchange calendars outside this cell. All URLs here are search-derived or path-inferred and are offered unverified for the deterministic liveness pass; paywall depth at Portfolio, hvg and vg.hu remains unmeasured and should be tested before extractors are built.

Hungary's corporate press-release landscape for banking and payments has a clear spine: MTI's paid OS wire (os.mti.hu) as the canonical carrier, the free Helló Sajtó! portal as a readable secondary wire with per-issuer and per-agency taxonomies, and the Magyar Bankszövetség site as both an industry wire and a ready-made member actor map. Every major bank runs its own 'sajtószoba' or 'közlemények' page — OTP, MBH, Erste, K&H, Raiffeisen, CIB — with Raiffeisen additionally publishing 'rendszerfejlesztés' operational notices that leak infrastructure work directly, and OTP splitting free-form news from year-partitioned regulated közlemények. Indirect layers add real value: MNB and GVH press rooms name specific institutions, NKFIH publishes bank R&D and AI-infrastructure detail no bank PR would volunteer, and FinTechZone/Origo/HVG per-company tag pages preserve items that corporate newsrooms drop. The two weakest points are the payment-service providers, whose first-party press channels went unlocated despite dominating HU e-commerce volume, and the machine-readability layer — no RSS feed and no corporate LinkedIn/YouTube newsroom was confirmed anywhere in this cell.

Third-party opinion on Hungarian banks splits cleanly into three streams. Rating agencies concentrate on a handful of entities - Moody's and Scope on OTP and MBH, Fitch on the foreign-owned subsidiaries (Erste Bank Hungary, K&H, CIB) and state policy banks (MFB, Eximbank) - and their analytical text is largely gated, so the practical collection route is ESMA's free European Rating Platform plus issuer-republished action PDFs (OTP hosts Moody's releases verbatim on otpgroup.info) with Hungarian press such as Portfolio.hu and vg.hu as an early-warning tripwire. Auditor opinion is the more complete stream: every Hungarian credit institution files signed independent auditor's reports with its statutory accounts on e-beszamolo.im.gov.hu, which is the only disclosure-grade third-party document for banks nobody rates, and the Audit Public Oversight Authority plus MKVK supply the quality and actor-mapping layer around it. Big4 sector research is real but uneven - Deloitte (CE Banking Outlook, 'Bank ma', M&A kitekintő), KPMG (Vállalathitelezési Hangulatindex, Banking CEO Outlook) and PwC (pénzügyi vezetői felmérés, digitális pénzügyi körkép) publish recurring, methodology-attached Hungarian surveys, while no equivalent EY Hungary banking series was found. Rating agencies publish nothing in Hungarian, so local-language coverage of this cell necessarily comes from issuer disclosures and the domestic financial press.

Hungary's sell-side and investor-event stream for banking is small, concentrated and — unusually — largely reconstructable from public records despite the research itself being client-gated. The domestic analyst layer is essentially Concorde, Erste Befektetési, Equilor and OTP's own Global Markets desk, supplemented by ~20 international houses that OTP itself enumerates on its IR analyst page along with downloadable quarterly and annual consensus files; separately, Hungarian issuers file broker target prices to the exchange as formal 'rendkívüli tájékoztatás részvényelemzésről' disclosures on bet.hu/kib_hirek, which is the single highest-leverage lawful route to sell-side signal. The event channel runs through BÉT's own Kibocsátói Fórum, Befektetői Klub and Xmatch programmes (note: 'Kibocsátói Nap' is not the actual name used) plus Portfolio's Hitelezés / Financial IT / Future of Finance conference circuit, both of which publish dated agendas and speaker lists in HU and EN. The weak point is analyst Q&A: OTP earnings-call transcripts were found only on paywalled third-party aggregators, with no issuer-hosted webcast archive located, and the payments sector has no dedicated sell-side channel at all.

For Hungarian bank and PSP disclosures, the durable cross-border layer has three tiers: EU-level regulator datasets and registers (EBA transparency exercise CSVs, EBA credit-institution and PSD2 registers, ECB supervised-entity lists, BRIS, and ESAP from 2027) which are free, English and machine-readable but cover only the EU-facing slice of the Hungarian market; national statutory archives (MNB Kozzetetelek as the official appointed mechanism, e-beszamolo for statutory accounts, BET for issuer news) which are complete but Hungarian-only and form-driven; and commercial aggregators (Opten Merlegtar, Dun & Bradstreet Hungary ex-Bisnode, Ceginfo, Ceginformacio, EMIS, Orbis Bank Focus) which normalise and translate but are paywalled with unverified Hungary coverage. Because the MKB/Budapest Bank/Takarekbank consolidation into MBH erased whole IR estates, web archives are load-bearing rather than supplementary: the Wayback Machine is the practical archive of retired Hungarian bank IR pages, while the national OSZK Webarchivum holds richer six-monthly snapshots but serves most of them only through a closed research interface. The main risks in this cell are that archival depth for the retired domains is unmeasured, that EU-level registers are widely over-assumed to cover non-euro Hungary fully, and that MBH's own re-hosted legacy archives are themselves volatile and should be harvested and self-archived now.

For Hungary's foreign-owned banks the analytical layer genuinely sits at the parent: KBC (K&H, inside the International Markets business unit), Erste Group (Hungary as a named geographic segment), RBI (Hungary as a Central Europe country segment, with unusually explicit clearing/settlement/payment commission commentary) and UniCredit (Hungary inside the CEE division perimeter) each publish English-language quarterly reports and presentations on a fixed calendar, plus Intesa Sanpaolo's quarterly press releases covering CIB Bank within its International Subsidiary Banks Division. Two of the six named actors do not hold: Magyar Cetelem passed from BNP Paribas Personal Finance to Cofidis / Credit Mutuel Alliance Federale in October 2024, and no Hungarian entity surfaced in Santander Consumer Finance's disclosed geographies — both should be re-scoped rather than monitored as-is. No parent publishes in Hungarian, so local-language coverage in this cell comes from the subsidiaries' own statutory report pages (kh.hu, raiffeisen.hu) and from Portfolio.hu / Privatbankar.hu, which convert the Vienna and Brussels releases into HUF-denominated Hungary-only stories within hours. Practical collection risk is link stability rather than access: KBC and RBI serve result PDFs from CDN paths carrying rotating cache tokens, so assets must be resolved from IR index pages at collection time instead of templated.

Hungary concentrates statutory disclosure in a small, highly enumerable set of state-run repositories: the MNB's officially appointed mechanism at kozzetetelek.mnb.hu (all regulated issuer information incl. Pillar 3 and inside information), the Ministry of Justice e-beszámoló portal (annual accounts of literally every Zrt/Kft, hence the whole long tail of PSPs, ATM operators and vendor subsidiaries), the Cégközlöny gazette and e-cégjegyzék for corporate events and identity, and BÉT for listed-issuer announcements. Two MNB assets multiply the leverage: Intézménykereső, the authoritative register of every licensed bank, payment institution and e-money issuer, and the Aranykönyv, which publishes per-institution standardised financials back to roughly 2003 in xlsx and interactive form — a ready-made comparable dataset that avoids filing-level parsing. The binding constraint is machine access, not availability: none of the core repositories documents an API, RSS or bulk export, e-beszámoló sells its bulk corpus commercially, and several hosts (Cégközlöny, njt) exist under competing domains, so canonical-endpoint resolution and a robots/terms review must precede any collector build. Local-language terms are mandatory throughout — közzététel, szabályozott információ, nyilvánosságra hozatal, beszámoló/mérleg letétbe helyezés, kibocsátói hírek — since English mirrors, where they exist, are partial.

The MNB is by far the densest single node in the Hungarian banking/payments source landscape for institution-level disclosure data, and it splits cleanly across three hosts: statisztika.mnb.hu carries the quantitative datasets (Aranykönyv/Golden Book with per-institution audited balance sheet and P&L series from 2003, quarterly credit-institution tables and their archive, and payment statistics), www.mnb.hu carries the rules and events (disclosure recommendation 7/2022 superseding 9/2021, reporting templates, licensing guidance, supervisory decisions search, supervisory news and press releases), and intezmenykereso.mnb.hu carries the authoritative register of every licensed bank, payment institution and e-money issuer — the master actor list that feeds every other cell in this run. The critical structural finding is that MNB centralises the disclosure REQUIREMENTS but not the disclosure DOCUMENTS: individual Pillar 3 / nyilvánosságra hozatal reports live on each bank's own site, so this cell supplies the enumeration list and the template schema while the issuer cells must collect the PDFs. Main open risks are technical rather than availability-related: both the interactive Aranykönyv and the register are JavaScript apps whose data endpoints and download URL patterns still need a network-trace pass, MNB has demonstrably migrated statistical URLs across hosts (a 307 redirect from the old /en/supervision/time-series path), and it is unconfirmed whether the financial datasets and the register share a joinable institution identifier.

Hungarian PSPs, EMIs and payment fintechs disclose in two disconnected modes and almost never in a bank-shaped one: audited Hungarian-language annual accounts filed once a year to the state e-beszámoló portal, and irregular, marketing-driven press releases or media-relayed claims about volumes, customers and funding — no quarterly IR cadence was found anywhere in this cell. The reliable spine is therefore governmental: MNB's Intézménykereső and payment-institution/EMI registers to enumerate and name the actors, e-beszámoló for their audited financials, and MNB payment statistics as the market denominator against which self-reported growth claims (SimplePay, B-Payment, Revolut HU) can be sanity-checked. The narrative layer sits with issuer press centres (Barion's is the strongest example) and a small, dense Hungarian trade press — FinTechZone, Portfolio, Forbes.hu, vg.hu, Telex — which is often the only channel where English-first or passported players such as Revolut, Wise, PayU and Pay10 say anything Hungary-specific at all. The main structural risk is cross-border entities: they serve Hungary heavily while filing next to nothing locally, so home-state group reporting must be wired in alongside the domestic registers.

Hungary's payment and post-trade infrastructure is a small, state-adjacent set of operators — GIRO Zrt. (BKR/GIROInstant, MNB-owned), KELER Zrt. and KELER KSZF Zrt. (CSD and CCP), Magyar Posta Zrt. with its internal Posta Elszámoló Központ, and VIBER which the MNB operates itself — and each of the corporate entities maintains a well-structured Hungarian-language document repository carrying audited annual accounts, narrative annual reports and statutory közérdekű adatok disclosures. The highest-value layer sits above the operators: the MNB's annual Fizetési rendszer jelentés and the statisztika.mnb.hu interbank turnover series are the authoritative consolidated statements on VIBER, BKR and KELER volumes, availability and incidents, and are the only reporting VIBER has at all. Two structural caveats shape collection: PEK is an internal Magyar Posta unit with no standalone reporting, and GIRO's narrative éves jelentés series appears to stop at 2023 while its statutory beszámoló continues to 2025. Scheme rulebooks and tariff schedules plus the Közbeszerzési Értesítő procurement gazette are the strongest forward-looking, procurement-relevant signals, since they surface pricing changes and modernisation spend well before the annual reports narrate them.

Hungarian disclosure by the scheme/acquirer/processor layer splits cleanly into three tiers: English-language regulated filings of listed parents (Euronet, Global Payments, Worldline, Nexi, Visa/Mastercard) that name Hungary only in passing; Hungarian-language corporate press rooms of the local operating arms (euronetatms.hu, visa.hu, globalpayments.com/hu-hu, worldline.com/hu-hu, erstebank.hu) that carry the real operational detail; and the statutory accounts on the Ministry of Justice e-beszamolo portal, which is the only official route to Hungary-level revenue for actors that never break the country out. Euronet is the standout: founded as a Hungarian company, still running a Budapest processing centre, and with a 30-year filing trail in EDGAR. The acquiring structure is an outsourcing relationship (Erste Bank Hungary to Global Payments Europe since 2014) rather than the CEE equity JV that covers Czechia/Slovakia/Romania, so collection must target the Hungarian bank's press room alongside the acquirer's. The main weaknesses are the absence of a Hungarian Mastercard press room, no located Hungarian domestic interchange schedules, and unresolved legal-entity identifiers for the Worldline and Global Payments Hungarian arms.

Hungary's self-service and banking-IT vendor landscape is only partly visible through classic vendor reference marketing: the two largest ATM OEMs (Diebold Nixdorf, NCR Atleos) publish no Hungary-specific case study, so their HU footprint must be inferred from local legal entities (Diebold Nixdorf Kft., Vecsés, reg. 01-09-690680) and their statutory accounts. The richest public traces are elsewhere: Euronet's SEC filings and HU press room quantify the Hungarian ATM estate and name bank outsourcing deals (e.g. Budapest Bank's ~145-ATM 10-year contract), GVH proceedings expose its market conduct, and municipal council documents publish actual 'kihelyezett ATM' service contracts naming both the bank (MBH) and its ATM operating partner. Overlaying all of this, MNB decree 19/2025 assigns named banks a settlement-by-settlement ATM installation obligation with escalating fines, creating a dated, bank-attributed demand pipeline that HU financial media track article by article. On the software/integration side, 4iG's BÉT and IR disclosures and Dorsum's HU blog and Portfolio.hu interviews are the practical substitute for absent bank procurement disclosure.

Hungary's state-owned, promotional and specialist lending segment splits cleanly into two disclosure regimes. A small set of actors — MFB, EXIM/Eximbank, Garantiqa, MBH Jelzálogbank, Gránit and MagNet — self-publish audited IFRS annual reports and Pillar-3 disclosures on their own sites and, because most of them are bond issuers, mirror those filings into the BÉT/BSE KIB repository, which is the single most efficient harvesting point for the cell. Everything else — KAVOSZ, the leasing and consumer-finance arms, the Cofidis branch, and the smaller pénzügiy vállalkozások — publishes nothing readable itself and is reachable only through the state registries: the Ministry of Justice e-beszamolo portal for the statutory accounts and the MNB Intézménykereső plus the Aranykönyv time series for the actor map and comparable per-entity figures. Coverage is overwhelmingly Hungarian-only outside MFB and EXIM, and the cooperative-sector legacy archives (mtb.hu, mirrored on mbhbefektetesibank.hu) should be captured soon because those hosts are being wound down post-merger.

Montenegro's banking/payments supplier landscape is unusually concentrated: the Asseco/ASEE family (Payten for ATM/POS supply and field service, e-mon for e-banking channels) accounts for most of the country's payments infrastructure and, helpfully, publishes bank-named references - Payten's news feed and the emon24.net client list together map most of the eleven ME banks to specific products. Genuinely Montenegrin actors cluster in two layers: bank-adjacent software houses with published case studies (Logate with CX360/Connect, 2BI which is itself a CBCG-licensed payment institution, Cikom as the veteran systems integrator) and a merchant-side POS/fiscalisation tier (VG Group/Adeo, eFiskal, SEKAS, POS4). No ME-domiciled core banking product vendor and no ME card personalisation bureau were found, which pushes that intelligence to regional vendors and bank-side disclosures. The strongest pointer assets for maintaining this cell are the ICT Cortex member directory, the MFIC member profiles and the CBCG financial-service-provider registers, with ME bank e-banking notice pages serving as the best independent, dated confirmation of vendor switches.

Montenegro's vendor-side evidence is concentrated in two regional suppliers rather than the global tier-1s: Payten (Asseco Group) publishes both a named ME implementation (SoftPOS/MojPOS at Hipotekarna banka, 2022) and a quantified installed base (present since 2006, >350 ATMs and >7,000 POS maintained, partnerships with nine of eleven banks), while ASEE publishes a named core-banking win (ASEBA PUB 2000 at Ziraat Bank Montenegro, live July 2015) and maintains a Podgorica entity. Printec and Euronet both run local Montenegro presences with their own country sites, Euronet's in Serbian. No Diebold Nixdorf, NCR Atleos, Temenos, Oracle, Finastra, BPC, OpenWay, Worldline or Nexi page naming Montenegro was found in this pass, which is itself a finding: the ME market appears to be reached through regional integrators and parent-bank group systems rather than direct global-vendor contracts. The richest forward-looking vendor-attribution surface is the CBCG's World Bank-financed TIPS Clone programme, whose open tenders and forthcoming award notices will name suppliers before any vendor press release does.

Montenegro's conference landscape for banking/payments is small and therefore highly legible: essentially one dedicated event (INFINTECH, run by local vendor Logate at Porto Montenegro/Tivat, with a public tiered sponsor wall and a 30+ speaker roster of regional bank and payments executives), one regulator-hosted event (the Capital Market Commission's 'Montenegro Financial Markets' conference, co-organised with the Ministry of Finance and CBCG, with Visa as platinum sponsor), and a set of adjacent national events whose sponsor lists reliably include banks and their IT suppliers (IT SPOT in Podgorica, Forum izvršnih direktora, Spark.me in Budva). Regional reach is best covered by Money Motion in Zagreb — which publishes exhibitor and speaker lists and keeps per-year archive subdomains — and BANCEE, since group-level decisions at OTP/NLB/Erste/RBI cascade into their Montenegrin subsidiaries. Discovery in local language depends on bankar.me, investitor.me, MINA, CdM and Digitalizuj.Me, since ME events publish 'sponzori'/'partneri' pages rather than exhibitor lists and smaller gatherings never get an English write-up. The two structural weaknesses are that no Montenegrin-run industry award with a published shortlist exists (award signal must be imported from The Banker/Global Finance plus the winning bank's own release) and that event sites keep no past-edition archive, so year-over-year sponsor diffing depends on the Internet Archive and on OCR of logo walls.

Montenegro's sr-language business-media layer is small, concentrated and genuinely additive: Bankar.me is the only specialised banking outlet and its interview and conference sections are where bank executives and CBCG officials name systems, platforms and suppliers, while MINA Business is the agency wire that timestamps announcements first and Investitor.me and eKapija ME add analytic and deal-shaped, semi-structured coverage. The general dailies and portals (Vijesti, Pobjeda, Dan, CdM, Portal Analitika, RTCG) form a corroboration tier that reliably captures infrastructure milestones — SEPA accession, the July 2026 TIPS Clone instant-payments launch across all eleven banks, the 2025-2029 FinTech Strategy — but rarely attributes vendors, and several of them republish bank PR verbatim, so duplicate detection is required before treating multi-site coverage as independent confirmation. Two practical constraints shape collection: Bankar.me returned a 403 to automated fetching and needs a browser-style collector rather than being written off as dead, and eKapija's premium tender and company data sits behind a commercial ToS that favours a subscription over scraping. The main open gaps are direct confirmation of vijesti.me's structure, an inventory of domestic integrators beyond Logate, and bank LinkedIn pages as a likely faster-than-press channel for go-live and partnership signals.

Montenegro's banking-technology supply side is documented almost entirely outside Montenegro: the durable sources are regional vendor sites (ASEE/asee.io, Payten, Comtrade System Integration, Croatian integrators such as Combis and KING ICT) where ME appears only inside multi-country claim lists, and regional bank-group corporate/IR sites (NLB Group, OTP/CKB) where a platform decision taken in Ljubljana, Budapest or Belgrade surfaces before the Podgorica subsidiary mentions it. Because standalone ME case studies are essentially absent, the highest-yield collection methods in this cell are indirect: mobile-app package-ID attribution (hr.asseco.*, mne.asseco.*, com.payten.nlb.podgorica, com.abctechgroup.adriatic), vendor customer lists that can be diffed over time (Halcom's list currently shows no Montenegrin bank), and Chamber-of-Commerce registrations that reveal which regional integrators keep a Podgorica legal vehicle. Local-language media (eKapija ME/RS, Bankar.me, Netokracija, Biznis u regionu, B92 Biz region desk) are the best pointer layer and consistently outperform English-language search, which is badly polluted by the unrelated 'Adriatic Bank' name. Main open gaps: no Croatian-language pass, no Bosnian vendor leg, and no procurement/tender source yet attached to this cell.

Montenegro's weak-signal layer is anchored by one genuinely strong asset: the Komisija za zaštitu prava's searchable rješenja database at kontrola-nabavki.me, which is filterable by naručilac, žalilac and predmet nabavke and is the only public window into contested tenders and losing bidders — backed by the Privredni sud / pravosudje.me decision portals for supplier contract disputes and by CRPS for resolving vendor legal entities. The job-ad channel has good containers (ZZZCG's statutory SRM register with deep archives, bank-run ATS pages such as CKB's, and Bankar.me's banking-specific job feed) but this run found no ME ad actually naming a core-banking product, so the incumbency-from-job-ads hypothesis remains unproven and needs a dictionary-driven crawl of those archives rather than general web search. LinkedIn — the cell's other named pillar — is auth-walled and ToS-restricted, so vendor-tags-client posts require a manual analyst seat; vendor newsrooms (Asseco SEE naming NLB Montenegro, Temenos on NLB group, Printec's ME footprint page) are the compliant substitute. Two high-yield sources were queued but unreached because the session's search budget ran out: Upravni sud review of procurement appeals, and the CEJN/ESJN award record.

The Montenegrin advisory/audit/analyst layer is small and highly concentrated: the ICAM register shows ~37 licensed audit firms of which only Deloitte DOO, Ernst & Young Montenegro DOO and PricewaterhouseCoopers DOO are Big-4 (no KPMG entity observed), with BDO, Crowe MNE, HLB Mont Audit and TPA forming the mid-tier. The richest and most reliably recurring intelligence is not the advisers' own marketing but the audited outputs they sign: the CBCG repository of bank financial statements with independent auditor reports, plus CBCG's own audited accounts and Financial Stability Report, which expose auditor identity, software/intangible capex and payment-infrastructure projects. Above that sits the IFI layer - EC enlargement reports (SWD 2025), IMF Article IV (explicitly naming SEPA and TIPS Clone), and a EUR 8m World Bank digital-payments modernisation loan whose procurement trail is the single most direct route to naming competitor vendors. Coverage of EBRD and of credit-rating bank reports remains inferred rather than evidenced because the session's web-search budget was exhausted, and those two components should be re-scouted first in any follow-up run.

For Montenegro, the durable record of vendor wins is split across three channels: the Wayback Machine (the only route to deleted vendor reference/case-study pages and defunct-bank sites, best driven by CDX host enumeration rather than search engines), Službeni list Crne Gore plus CBCG's own payment-system regulation and archive pages (the authoritative, dated legal trail behind each compliance-driven procurement wave), and the state digital-agenda stack — the 2022-2026 Strategija digitalne transformacije, gov.me's document repository, and the NS NAT / ePlaćanje / eKontrola e-payment systems run by the Ministarstvo javne uprave. Critically, the government's own NS NAT page names no implementer, confirming that supplier identity must be recovered from ujn.gov.me procurement awards, CRPS company records and donor (EU/UNDP) tender documents rather than from counterpart communications. The main structural risks are that Wayback is currently an unverified single point of failure (no domestic .me web archive was confirmed) and that the vendor/defunct-bank domain list needed to drive archive enumeration does not yet exist.

Montenegro's ten commercial banks are all privately held, so no procurement record exists and — confirmed across every query in this pass — not one bank press release names its technology supplier in prose. What the banks' own channels do reliably provide is the fact and date of a launch: CKB and Hipotekarna both run enumerable news archives (novosti-detalj?nid=<int> and /obavjestenja/saopstenje-za-javnost-<int>), Erste ME and Adriatic Bank maintain explicit press/news sections, and audited annual statements expose IT capex through the 'nematerijalna ulaganja / softver' notes. Attribution has to come from adjacent artifacts, and those turned out to be far richer than the newsrooms: Android package ids alone tie Asseco to Prva banka, Ziraat ME and NLB ProKlik, Logate to Hipotekarna, Fleka to Lovćen and Hipotekarna, and ProvisionPay to Hipotekarna's softPOS — while Logate's own CX360 page names six of the ten banks outright and the ASEE-owned e-mon service centre exposes the shared corporate e-banking layer. Practical guidance for collection: scope every sr query with 'Crna Gora'/'Podgorica' because novosti, vijesti and Nova Banka all collide with major regional media and banks; skip Hipotekarna's /pretraga/ endpoint, which is reflecting injected SEO spam; and treat the CKB TLS failure and the two 404s recorded here as fetcher artifacts, not dead sources.

Montenegro's private-bank procurement leaves almost no standing public trail: no Montenegrin bank — domestic or group-owned — was found to publish a 'nabavke/dobavljači' page, and the one confirmed CKB 'javni poziv' surfaced on Facebook rather than a website. The observable route therefore splits three ways: upward to group parents, where OTP (szállítóknak, vendorman@otpbank.hu, phased SAP Ariba, DORA supplier training) and Erste Group Procurement (Vienna, whole-group spend, SAQ/SPS/SSS document set, with erstebank.rs showing the regional subsidiary pattern of published invitations plus a vendor database) are concrete and monitorable while NLB shows no external sourcing gateway at all; sideways to commercial aggregators, where the ME-native eTenderi.me (alerts, competitor-win analytics, OCR'd appeal-decision archive) and eKapija (explicitly covers commercial-company tenders, twice-daily bulletins) are the strongest, with TenderiLive regional and the global English aggregators unproven for private tenders; and outward to institutional channels — CBCG's own tenderi-i-oglasi, the CEJN state portal as baseline, the Chamber of Economy, and EBRD's ECEPP for donor-financed bank contracts. Two of the best local sources (etenderi.me, bankar.me) blocked automated fetching and need browser-grade collection; neither should be treated as dead.

Montenegro's public-procurement evidence base is centralised and legally mandated but technically awkward: CeJN (cejn.gov.me) is the single authoritative record of plans, notices, award decisions and contracts for every state buyer including CBCG, Ministarstvo finansija/Trezor and Posta Crne Gore, yet it serves as a JavaScript SPA that returns no HTML to a plain fetch, so collection needs headless browsing or a reverse-engineered API. Around it sit useful satellites: the commercial mirror nabavke.com, which republishes CeJN documents at stable, directly fetchable URLs and is currently the only search-engine-visible route to concrete award PDFs; the appeal body kontrola-nabavki.me, whose rjesenja expose bidder names, specifications and prices in far more detail than the award notices; and DRI audit reports, which independently itemise state IT and licence spend. Two structural blind spots dominate: the highest-value payments work is moving into IFI-financed channels outside CeJN (the 2025 World Bank EUR 8m SEPA/TIPS-clone financial-infrastructure loan), and a growing share of state IT is awarded through no-tender intergovernmental sporazumi that never generate an award record at all. No ATM- or card-specific ME award was surfaced in this pass, which is itself a finding to resolve by in-portal keyword search before assuming the public slice covers bank hardware.

Montenegro's supranational financial-infrastructure procurement is concentrated in three channels: TED/EU Delegation Podgorica for IPA service contracts, the World Bank (Projects API, Documents & Reports, and STEP-derived contract-award datasets) for the flagship EUR 8m Financial Infrastructure Modernization / SEPA TIPS-clone project, and the WBIF-EBRD-EIB technical-assistance layer that writes tender documents 12-24 months ahead of national procurement. EBRD, UNDP and KfW are live but low-yield for financial-sector systems specifically: EBRD's visible ME awards are transport/PIU, UNDP's are government ICT hardware, and KfW's are energy. Award-level vendor attribution is published almost exclusively in English by the funders, so Montenegrin/Serbian sources (CBCG press releases, gov.me/mif EU funds pages, ujn.gov.me 'EU tenderi') are best used for beneficiary-side confirmation and timing rather than as the award record. The main readiness gap is machine access: the TED Search API query and a Finances One Montenegro contract-awards export must be built and validated before systematic collection begins.

Montenegro's payments-native landscape is small and bank-anchored: card acceptance is sold by the acquiring arms of the ~11 licensed banks (CKB/OTP with the largest POS estate, Hipotekarna with a well-documented e-commerce gateway, Erste ME, Addiko), while the processing and gateway layer is supplied largely from the region — Payten d.o.o. Montenegro (Asseco SEE) in Podgorica, its group sibling Monri, plus CorvusPay and other SEE gateways, with NLB Podgorica observed using Ixopay. The highest-value trackable sources are the CBCG registers (payment institutions, e-money institutions, payment systems) and CBCG payment-services statistics, which give an authoritative and diff-able roster and market-size series, complemented by bank product/tariff pages, the Mastercard ME acquiring-bank list, local integrators such as Softing, the CEFTA-backed ecommerce4all.me e-payment module, and trade media bankar.me and investitor.me. Vendor-published Montenegro case studies are essentially absent, so merchant-win intelligence must be reconstructed from media announcements, integrator reference pages and checkout-page gateway fingerprints; the main open gaps are the remaining banks' acquiring pages, public procurement, and the company registry.

Montenegro's scheme-and-infrastructure layer is unusually concentrated: CBCG is the owner and sole operator of the only interbank payment infrastructure (RTGS + DNS on one platform, 20+ years old), and as of 20 July 2026 it also operates a national instant payment scheme built as a TIPS Clone supplied by Banca d'Italia on behalf of the Eurosystem, with ECB approval and World Bank support, launched with all 11 banks simultaneously. On the euro-integration axis, Montenegro entered SEPA geographic scope in November 2024 and went operationally live in October 2025, making the EPC Registers of Participants and the UBCG's NASO-style coordination role high-value, structured trackers of per-bank readiness. Card-side, no domestic switch or national scheme exists — ATM/POS routing runs on Visa and Mastercard rails — so the single highest-leverage vendor signals are CBCG's own procurement notices and the Banca d'Italia/ECB TIPS Clone programme channels, while the per-bank processor map remains unattributed and is the main open gap.

Montenegro's licensed-actor index is unusually clean and concentrated: the Central Bank of Montenegro (cbcg.me) publishes, under statute, a bank register plus four payment-side registers (payment systems, payment institutions and agents, e-money institutions and agents, and revoked authorisations) and a non-bank financial-service-provider index covering leasing, factoring, microcredit institutions and guarantee funds — together these enumerate essentially every regulated banking and payments actor in the country. Legal-entity resolution, ownership and activity codes then come from CRPS, the Central Register of Business Entities, via pretraga.crps.me and the eFirma portal (with a beneficial-ownership register since Aug 2021), while MONSTAT's Statistical Business Register supplies the KD 2010 / NACE Rev.2 population counts needed to size the vendor and financial-sector universe. Two structural cautions dominate: everything is HTML pages and PDFs with no confirmed bulk extract or API, and a second company-register front-end (irms.tax.gov.me, JavaScript-rendered, tax administration) runs in parallel with CRPS, so the canonical endpoint should be settled before building collection. Card processors have no register of their own and must be inferred from payment-system operator entries plus CRPS activity codes; SCMN and ANO registers are known to exist but were not pinned to a URL in this pass.

Montenegro's association layer is small, highly concentrated and unusually productive for source purposes: UBCG (11-12 member banks, EBF associate) plus the Chamber of Economy's two relevant sectoral boards — banking/other financial organisations/insurance, and ICT — together enumerate essentially the whole buyer and local-supplier population, and both publish named rosters. The supply side is best captured by ICT Cortex, whose ~40-company membership visibly includes payments-relevant firms (PayTen, POS4.me, Financial Solutions, VG eFISKAL, Logate, Data Design), with Digitalizuj.Me and ITspot as community/event proxies. Position-paper and newsletter intelligence concentrates in three PDF serials — UBCG's 'Bankar' magazine, PKCG's 'Glasnik', and MFIC's annual White Book — which are open, archived and worth systematic harvesting. The main limitation is that no board minutes are published and no fintech/payments trade body exists, so vendor-presentation signals must be recovered from bulletin write-ups, event pages and bankar.me coverage rather than from primary association records.

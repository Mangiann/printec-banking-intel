The Central Bank of the Republic of Kosovo (BQK / CBK / Centralna banka Republike Kosovo) is a genuinely single-point canonical source for XK A4: by law it compiles monetary, financial and external-sector statistics, and it publishes them through a small, stable and trilingual (sq/sr/en) set of channels — the Time Series database, the Monthly Statistics Bulletin (issue-numbered, running since 2001 and now in the 280s), the monthly financial-system information report, the quarterly macro assessment, the annual Financial Stability Report, and the Annual Report whose payment-system chapter carries national SPN volumes. Card, ATM and POS data — the core of this cell's payments topics — are not exposed as a standalone dataset; they live inside the bulletin and annual report tables plus one dedicated thematic publication, 'Përdorimi i kartelave në Kosovë', which is the single richest card/ATM/POS document found. Collection has one concrete technical constraint: every HTML route on bqk-kos.org sits behind a Cloudflare challenge (all HTML fetches 403'd), while PDF and other static files served cleanly at HTTP 200, so a two-stage pipeline — headless-browser link discovery, plain-HTTP bulk file download, respecting the robots.txt Crawl-delay of 10 — is the right design. Downstream re-publishers (IMF Financial Access Survey for comparable ATM/POS density, IMF staff and technical-assistance reports for both annex tables and an independent verdict on CBK data quality) should be used to benchmark and validate, never to replace, the CBK primary.

Kosovo's payment-systems statistics are overwhelmingly a single-publisher landscape: the CBK's Payment Systems Department (Departamenti i Sistemeve të Pagesave) publishes them through a departmental hub distinct from the main statistics section, with the recurring 'Use of Bank Cards in Kosovo' study as the richest card/ATM/POS artefact, the CBK Annual Report and Financial Stability Report carrying interbank system (SPN/KIPS) volumes, and National Payments Council minutes often releasing headline infrastructure numbers first. Everything found is PDF-shaped; the plausible spreadsheet home (Seritë kohore) and the legacy /repository/docs/SistemiIPagesave/ quarterly folder could not be inspected because bqk-kos.org returned HTTP 403 to every fetch — a bot-defence hypothesis, not a liveness verdict, and the main design constraint for this cell. Independent cross-checks exist but are thin: IMF FAS (with FRED/DBnomics mirrors) gives a structured annual ATM/POS series for XK, Global Findex 2025 gives demand-side cash and digital-payment behaviour, and five commercial banks' annual reports allow a bottom-up rebuild of terminal and card fleets. The notable holes are the absence of any scheme/processor-side disclosure, an unconfirmed Kosovo Banking Association domain, and weakly-sourced e-banking/mobile-banking user counts; note also that the local acronym is SPN/KIPS, not the 'KIS/ATS' shorthand in the cell brief.

Kosovo's ATM/POS and card-infrastructure vendor landscape is documented almost entirely by regional integrators rather than global OEMs: Printec (ATM fleet renewal for BPB Bank), the Asseco SEE/Payten group (registered Kosovo entity in Prishtina, regional POS/ATM managed services), Halcom (Raiffeisen Bank Kosovo digital channel), and Compass Plus, whose TranzWare platform underpins Quipu's Kosovo card processing and personalisation centre — the single richest quantified Kosovo reference found, with dated contactless issuance volumes. Global OEMs (Diebold Nixdorf, NCR Atleos) and the major switch vendors (BPC, OpenWay, Nexi, Tietoevry) publish nothing Kosovo-specific, and no independent ATM deployer appears to operate in the market, implying a wholly bank-owned ATM estate. Local-language discovery is asymmetric: Albanian terms surface the fiscal-device/merchant-terminal supplier layer (ATK's authorised-supplier register, STIKK/KIESA directories, CACTTUS), while Serbian-language searching yielded nothing Kosovo-relevant for this cell. Collection should be browser-based (Printec and ASEE both bot-defended or consent-gated to plain fetchers) and should pair vendor newsrooms with Kosovo bank product pages and the Electronic Payments International Kosovo tag as a pre-filtered tripwire.

Kosovo's Albanian-language media landscape for banking and payments is dominated by a small set of originating outlets — Buletini Ekonomik (the most systematic monthly restatement of BQK commercial-bank statistics), Telegrafi, KOHA.net, KosovaPress and the investigative KALLXO.com — surrounded by a very large republication layer (Lajmi.net, local portals, Albania-based mirrors such as Monitor.al, Shqiptarja.com, Albeu, JOQ) that recycles the same figures verbatim. Nearly all quantitative reporting traces back to BQK's Albanian press releases and its 'Përdorimi i kartelave në Kosovë' publication, so BQK's /news/ feed should be treated as the provenance anchor and every media figure matched to it before use. The genuinely additive journalistic value in this cell is event dating, named-official quotes, bank-level product and fee changes (visible in banks' own Albanian 'çmimorja' PDFs), and investigative detail on fraud and AML that aggregates never carry. Main outstanding weaknesses: broadcast economy desks and roughly a dozen further Albanian portals are unconfirmed, Ekonomia Online's domain is unverified, and Serbian-language Kosovo coverage returned nothing in this pass.

The Serbian-language payments landscape for Kosovo is split between two institutional poles — Belgrade (NBS payment-system statistics and its KiM press/Q&A record, the Kancelarija za KiM payout announcements, and Banka Poštanska štedionica as the last dinar-operating bank with crossing-point branches) and Pristina (CBK, which publishes its statistics, Financial Stability and Annual Reports in a full Serbian-language edition at bqk-kos.org?lang=sr). Between them sits a dense but journalistic middle layer: KoSSev from North Mitrovica, Radio KIM for the central enclaves, Kosovo Online, RFE/RL's Serbian service, Danas, Vreme and Politika, plus the Mitrovica-based New Social Initiative as the only in-north analytic voice found. The decisive limitation is that no source publishes payment, card, ATM or POS data disaggregated to Serbian-majority municipalities, and no one quantifies parallel or hand-carried cash channels at all — so this cell yields rich qualitative and institutional evidence but must build its numbers from proxies (branch and ATM footprints, withdrawal caps, beneficiary counts) rather than from a dataset. Coverage also clusters on the 2024 dinar-ban episode and needs a deliberate 2025-2026 refresh.

Kosovo's research layer on cash and payments rests on three legs: the BQK's own working-paper series (highest authority, bot-defended, and the usual place where new payment/inclusion indicators appear first), independent think tanks led by Riinvest and GAP (the only survey-based estimates of informality and undeclared income, which serve as the de-facto proxy for cash intensity), and a long tail of Albanian-language university theses — chiefly the openly harvestable UBT Knowledge Center and the Mitrovica thesis portal — that contain small original surveys on card and e-banking usage. English-language peer-reviewed work is thin but real, concentrated in a small cluster of Kosovo academics publishing cashless-adoption and internet-banking surveys, best tracked through RePEc and author pages rather than journal by journal. The clearest structural weakness is Serbian-language coverage: the north-Kosovo dinar-to-euro cash story is documented mainly by community NGOs and media rather than by indexed research, so it needs a dedicated sr-language discovery pass.

For Kosovo, the remittance and physical-cash landscape is anchored by one dominant official source — the Central Bank of Kosovo (BQK), which publishes balance-of-payments remittance series, a monthly release combining remittances with non-bank financial institution statistics (giving the crucial bank vs MTO channel split), a currency-in-circulation page for the unilaterally-used euro, the register of licensed financial institutions, and multilingual (sq/sr/en) payment-system studies and strategy. Around it sit commercially-published actor sources: Unioni Financiar Prishtinë as the Western Union principal agent operating the 170+ point UnionNet network, MoneyGram's Kosovo agent locator, and the Posta e Kosovës counter network, which together define where remittance cash physically lands. International benchmarks (World Bank Remittance Prices Worldwide for the Germany-Kosovo corridor, KNOMAD, UNDP/IOM diaspora and remittance surveys) supply pricing and the survey evidence that informal hand-carried cash dominates formal channels — the part of cash circulation that banking statistics never see. The weakest link by far is cash logistics: no register of licensed private security or cash-in-transit operators is published, no Kosovo CIT operator was identified, and Albanian-language search terms for the sector do not yet work, so that sub-cell needs a dedicated terminology pass plus an access-to-public-documents request to MPBAP.

Kosovo's weak-signal layer is real but thin and asymmetric. The strongest, most automatable channel is Albanian-language job advertising — KosovaJob and its network carry dated bank vacancies for card operations, payment-systems support and payments departments that name internal units and platforms — followed by BQK's own oblique outputs: the complaints division and portal, the board-decision notices that record licensing and disciplinary measures, and the annual report's complaint and enforcement tables. Event and award channels exist but are regional rather than national: there is no standing Kosovo payments conference, so agenda leakage must be harvested from the BQK regional conference, KosICT and the Balkan Payment Forum, while The Banker, Global Finance, Euromoney and EBRD award citations supply an annual, externally validated metric drop (partly paywalled). The binding constraints are that the two richest channels — executive LinkedIn activity and award citations — are login- or paywall-gated and therefore need manual or licensed collection, and that Serbian-language coverage for Kosovo is effectively absent, leaving the north-Kosovo segment unobserved.

Kosovo's demand-side statistical landscape is highly centralised in one actor, the Agjencia e Statistikave të Kosovës (ASK / Agencija za statistiku Kosova), which publishes trilingually (sq/sr/en) across three technically distinct surfaces: a CMS release site (ask.rks-gov.net), a GUID-addressed PDF store (askapi.rks-gov.net) and a PxWeb warehouse (askdata.rks-gov.net) whose 26 subject folders include Household Budget Survey, ICT in Households and Enterprise, Income and Living Conditions, Structural business statistics and the Statistical business register — the last of these being the merchant-universe denominator for POS penetration work. The PxWeb layer is the automation target (standard JSON-stat API, CSV/XLSX export); the CMS layer should be crawled only for release detection via the Kalendari i Publikimeve, since its folder navigation is postback-based. The critical caveat for this run is that ASK appears to measure what households consume and whether they shop online, but not which instrument they pay with, so ASK supplies denominators and behavioural context for cash-versus-card analysis rather than a direct cash-usage measurement; ABEF's shift to a two-to-three-year cycle after 2015 further limits its use as a high-frequency series. Adjacent administrative actors — ARBK for the upstream business register (plus the civic arbk.org mirror), and the not-yet-swept ATK fiscal-cash-register and ARKEP telecom reporting — are the most promising extensions of this cell.

Kosovo's commercial banks are, for this cell, a small and enumerable publisher set — roughly eight to ten licensed institutions, of which seven maintain their own web-published report archives (Raiffeisen 2010-2025, ProCredit 2013-2025, NLB 2012-2026 including quarterly interims, TEB, BKT Kosova, BPB and Banka Ekonomike). Disclosure quality is uneven but the upside is real: TEB's 2024 annual report was text-extracted in this run and states a total of 5,593 POS terminals and 34 branches (up from 30), exactly the per-institution granularity the CBK publishes only in aggregate. There is no Pillar-3 layer in Kosovo and no Serbian-language bank-level reporting, so the corpus is sq/en narrative annual reports plus audited IFRS notes, usefully supplemented by branch/ATM locator pages as an independent, higher-frequency count of network size. Practical collection risks are technical rather than legal: bkt-ks.com presents an unverifiable TLS chain and ProCredit's Albanian path has been renamed, both of which would be misread as dead sources by a naive liveness check.

Kosovo's majority foreign-owned banking market is documented far better abroad than at home: RBI (Vienna), ProCredit Holding (Frankfurt) and NLB (Ljubljana) all publish recurring, dated, country-level Kosovo figures — RBI via segment/country tables and CRD Art. 89 country-by-country disclosure, ProCredit via a per-country key-figures table, NLB via quarterly results decks with Kosovo market share and digital-channel KPIs. The Turkish/Albanian chain (TEB A.S. and KAP filings; Ziraat's integrated report; BKT Albania and Calik Holding) is thinner and narrative rather than tabular, but is the only public window on two significant card-issuing and acquiring banks. Card, ATM/POS and digital-channel detail is concentrated not in the parent filings but in the group-template subsidiary annual reports (Raiffeisen Kosovo, NLB Prishtina, ProCredit Kosovo, TEB Kosovo), which should be the collection backbone, with parent filings used for comparability and validation. Everything found is PDF/HTML narrative — no group-level Kosovo data feed exists, and cash-usage indicators are absent from group sources entirely.

Kosovo's non-bank payments landscape is regulator-centric: BQK is simultaneously the licensing authority, the register keeper and effectively the only publisher of quantitative payments data (its 'Përdorimi i kartelave në Kosovë' report and payment-system time series carry the card, POS, ATM and digital-wallet series). A real non-bank layer exists and is growing under an EU-aligned framework — Paysera Kosova as the first e-money institution, OneFor, Swinto/Velanis and Numarics clustered in the FIKS fintech grouping, with Quipu as the documented card processing and personalisation centre — but, contrary to the cell hypothesis, these actors publish essentially no recurring volume statistics of their own, only episodic launch and partner-press claims. No independent non-bank ATM deployer was found; ATM estates remain bank-owned, so the non-bank story here is wallets, e-money, remittance corridors and outsourced processing rather than terminal ownership. Practical collection should therefore anchor on BQK (register editions, card-usage report, sr-language variants), use ARBK and FIKS to enumerate and disambiguate actors, and treat all actor-level figures as unverified corporate claims.

Kosovo's actor universe for banking and payments is anchored by three pointer families: the BQK licensing register (a hub page plus dated 'Lista e institucioneve financiare' PDFs that name every bank, MFI, NBFI, FX bureau, payment and e-money institution and insurer), the industry associations (Kosovo Banking Association for banks, AMIK for microfinance, BKS for insurance, STIKK for the ICT/fintech vendor layer), and ARBK, the sole official business registry, reachable in bulk mainly through third-party mirrors. Both top-value domains (bqk-kos.org and bankassoc-kos.com) returned 403 to automated fetching while remaining heavily indexed, so collection for this cell must be browser-based rather than plain HTTP. Card/ATM/POS series exist but are embedded in BQK annual and financial-stability reports rather than in a standalone payment-participants register, and Serbian-language coverage is limited to document-level translations of Albanian originals.

For Kosovo, the IFI layer is the only source of harmonised, cross-country-comparable and machine-readable payments/inclusion data: IMF FAS supplies the supply-side access series (ATMs, branches, accounts, and Kosovo participated heavily in the fintech pilot variables), while World Bank Global Findex supplies the only demand-side account/card/digital-payment and cash-usage measurement, with Enterprise Surveys and the EBRD-EIB-WBG BEEPS adding the firm side and the EIB CESEE Bank Lending Survey adding a semi-annual forward-looking read that explicitly covers Kosovo. The single richest Kosovo-specific artefact found is the World Bank's February 2022 'Retail Payment Costs and Savings in Kosovo' study, which measured cash cost and usage from household, merchant, bank and MTO surveys — but it is ageing and has no confirmed successor. Access is mostly open and automatable (WB Indicators API, Data360, FRED/DBnomics mirrors of FAS), with two exceptions: microdata requires registration, and EBRD's BEPS bank-level data is permanently confidential. Local-language coverage of this cell is structurally absent — IFIs publish only in English for Kosovo, Albanian material appears only where domestic bodies such as BQK re-quote IFI figures, and Serbian-language searching yields nothing at all for this cell.

For Kosovo's payment and cash statistics, the EU/European layer is a validation, framing and conditionality layer rather than a data-production layer: the annual European Commission Kosovo Report (published in EN, SQ and SR via EEAS), the DG ECFIN Economic Reform Programme assessments, the SAA Sub-committee on Economic and Financial Issues and Statistics, and the Growth Plan/Reform Agenda milestones together give a compulsory, dated, comparable narrative record of payment-services alignment, SEPA/TIPS progress and statistical capacity. Eurostat covers Kosovo only through the enlargement-country products and through statistical-capacity assessments (the 2017 peer review and improvement actions), which matter chiefly for judging the reliability of Kosovo-origin numbers. Two structural absences define the cell: the ECB and Eurosystem publish no Kosovo payment, card, ATM/POS or cash-circulation series because euroisation is unilateral and unagreed, and Kosovo has no MONEYVAL mutual evaluation because it is not a Council of Europe member — the Council of Europe PECK II assessment applying FATF/MONEYVAL methodology is the substitute for cash-intensity and AML findings. The most decisive single fact to track is the European Payments Council's SEPA geographical-scope list, where regional peers have been admitted and Kosovo appears not yet to have been; EBRD Transition Report country assessments are the best European source of concrete retail-payment policy detail (National Retail Payments Strategy targets, cash-transaction caps).

Kosovo's public-procurement channel is unusually well consolidated for this cell: one mandatory national portal (e-Prokurimi, run by KRPP/PPRC) carries every contract, award and signed-contract notice, and — critically — the same corpus is exposed as an Open Contracting (OCDS) API and bulk download at ocdskrpp.rks-gov.net covering roughly 2016 to 2026, which should be the primary harvesting route rather than web search. Around that core sit three useful layers: buyer-side document stores (BQK/CBK's own tender page for payment-system and cash-handling procurement, Posta e Kosovës, Dogana, AQP's framework contracts, municipalities under kk.rks-gov.net), accountability layers that expose contract detail the notices omit (the OSHP review-body decisions database, its Demokracia Plus mirror, and National Audit Office reports), and civil-society/media mirrors (prokurimihapur.org in sq/sr/en, KALLXO). The donor and IFI leg is real but fragmented — EBRD ECEPP, World Bank projects/Finances One, UNDP and IOM Kosovo each run their own notices — while TED is structurally near-empty for Kosovo since it is outside the EU directives. Main risks to plan for: item-level ATM/POS notices are effectively unfindable via search engines, full technical dossiers may be gated behind economic-operator registration, and portal/notice availability is not durable, so notices must be archived at discovery.

Hungary's public-sector banking/payments procurement has an unusually clean primary spine: EKR (ekr.gov.hu) is the legally mandatory platform where every Kbt. procedure is run, and the Közbeszerzési Értesítő on kozbeszerzes.hu is the official gazette publishing all resulting notices, backed by a structured hirdetmény search, the CoRe contract register (post-2018), the legacy KBA archive (pre-2018) and the Döntőbizottság remedy-decision database. Above-threshold notices are duplicated on TED in structured eForms XML, which is by far the most machine-readable route, while below-threshold banking, POS and cash-handling contracts exist only in Hungarian-language HTML search results and daily signed PDF issues with no public API — the central collection challenge for this cell. Two mandatory central purchasing channels sit beside EKR and would be missed by notice-only monitoring: KEF (kozbeszerzes.gov.hu) for government framework agreements and DKÜ Zrt. for state IT procurement, whose framework call-offs surface mainly at contract level. A commercial monitoring layer (tender-ertesito.hu, khf.hu, kozbeszerzesmonitor.hu) and an open-data layer (OpenTender HU, the now-stale Red Flags) are useful cross-checks but are derived sources and subscription- or licence-constrained; EKR itself returned a maintenance page during this pass and remains unverified rather than unavailable.

The EU-level layer for Hungarian banking/payments procurement is dominated by one authoritative, machine-readable source — TED, now in eForms format — which duplicates all above-threshold Hungarian notices in structured XML and is reachable through a free, documented Search API plus daily/monthly bulk packages; discovery of individual Hungarian notices via general web search proved ineffective, so collection here must be API-first. A second, distinct layer is EU-funded programme procurement: palyazat.gov.hu (Széchenyi Terv Plusz 2021-2027 and Hungary's RRP) with its procurement section and daily-updated supported-project register, mirrored at EU level by Kohesio, the Cohesion Open Data Platform, the Financial Transparency System and the EU Funding & Tenders Portal. A third, less visible but commercially important channel is EU-cofinanced financial instruments distributed through selected commercial-bank intermediaries under MFB's GINOP Plusz credit programmes, whose intermediary-selection procedures were not found published as classic tenders and need a separate route. Main risks to coverage are CPV under-coding (banking contracts hiding under IT/logistics codes), the absence of a verified national-to-TED notice ID crosswalk, and unverified freshness of the third-party Opentender aggregator.

Hungary's procurement-remedies and audit layer is unusually well institutionalised and almost entirely Hungarian-language. The core is the Közbeszerzési Döntőbizottság (KDB), whose full-text decisions and pending remedy applications are published on dontobizottsag.kozbeszerzes.hu and mirrored into the Közbeszerzési Hatóság's structured 'Döntőbizottsági és bírósági határozatok' database, which offers roughly twenty filters including contracting authority, bidder, subject matter and CPV code — the practical entry point for isolating banking and payment-service tenders. Judicial review sits exclusively with the Fővárosi Törvényszék (judgments republished as PDF attachments on KDB case pages) and onward at the Kúria and in the Bírósági Határozatok Gyűjteménye, while the audit and competition flanks are covered by ÁSZ reports, the Integritás Hatóság's system-wide annual integrity analysis, EUTAF for EU-funded programmes, and the GVH decision database, which holds the landmark Vj-018/2008 interchange-fee cartel case against 22 banks plus Visa and Mastercard and the 2019 card-acceptance sector inquiry. The main constraint is access rather than availability: no API, feed or bulk export was found for any of the primary decision repositories, and individual banking-sector decisions are not surfaced by general web search, so this cell must be collected by direct, form-driven querying of the KDB and GVH databases supplemented by NGO aggregators (Tenderbajnok, K-Monitor, Átlátszó) as lead generators.

Hungary's commercial tender-aggregation market is horizontal, crowded at the low end, and concentrated at the top: Tender Service Group's Tender-Értesítő (which the separately branded khf.hu now appears to redirect into) is the clear premium player, claiming 1400+ monitored sources, private-sector coverage, winner/price intelligence and an API, with TenderMonitor, Napitender, KözbeszGuru and InnoTender occupying cheaper or consultancy-led niches. The single most valuable non-commercial source for this cell is Opentender.eu, run by the Hungary-based Government Transparency Institute, because it openly republishes cleaned Hungarian contracting data drawn from both TED and the national Közbeszerzési Hatóság portal and therefore reaches below the EU threshold without a paywall; Datlab is its CEE commercial counterpart. Global country-slice aggregators (TendersOnTime, Tendersinfo, HungaryTenders, Tender Impulse, BidsFactory) and pan-EU platforms (Mercell, TenderAlerts.eu, Tenderio, Jorpex) are broad but almost certainly TED-derived, and none documented Hungarian below-threshold depth. Critically, no aggregator anywhere in this cell specialises in banking or payments for Hungary, and commercial bank procurement remains a near-total blind spot - sector filtering must be built in-house on CPV codes and named buyers, and private-sector deal flow must come from indirect-signal cells instead.

Hungary's business/IT trade-press layer for banking and payments contracts is concentrated in a small, well-defined set: Portfolio.hu and Világgazdaság for bank strategy and deal context, Bitport.hu (plus ComputerTrends, the successor to the now-defunct Computerworld Hungary, and HWSW) for IT supplier and procurement specifics, and FinTechZone/FinTechRadar for payments, card acceptance and named core-banking vendor selections. A distinct investigative tier — G7 at Telex, Átlátszó's Közpénz desk, HVG, with Transparency International Hungary supplying the analytical base — is where single-bidder structures, collusion suspicion and unnamed losing bidders actually surface, ahead of or beyond formal notices. Municipal banking mandates travel through a different channel entirely: PR wires (ProfitLine, Helló Sajtó), county/local outlets such as Civishír, and the municipalities' own 'bankváltás' notices, which are often the earliest primary confirmation of an awarded account-holding mandate. The main weaknesses of this cell are the thin, PR-mediated coverage of private-bank payments sourcing and the incomplete regional press panel; both should be closed before this cell is relied on for award-level intelligence.

Hungary's banking/payments association layer is compact and highly concentrated: Magyar Bankszövetség holds the authoritative ~40-member buyer-side roster (with per-member detail pages and working groups), while the supplier side is split across Magyar Fintech Szövetség (HFA/MAFISZ) for fintechs and IVSZ for IT integrators, with AmCham Hungary and DUIHK (~900 members) covering the multinational vendors that Hungarian-only registers miss. No dedicated payments-only trade association exists — that convening role is filled commercially by Portfolio (Banking Technology, Hitelezés), IIR Hungary (Kártyapiac & Digitális fizetés) and FinTechZone (FinTechShow / TechShow X.), whose tiered sponsor and exhibitor pages are the single richest signal in this cell because sponsorship tier is a paid, ranked proxy for who is actively selling into Hungarian banks. The main practical obstacles are rendering rather than access: rosters and sponsor tiers are public but often client-side rendered or split onto per-event /tamogatok tabs, so a headless collection pass plus a name-normalisation join to EKR/TED award notices is the required next step; LinkedIn pages carry useful event and partner signal but are login-walled and ToS-restricted, so they should stay manual-review only.

Hungary's bank/payments procurement is only thinly visible in formal notices, so the indirect layer carries real weight: vendor newsrooms (Dorsum's MBH migration write-up), trade press (FinTech Futures on OTP/iGCB, Portfolio.hu on bank IT spend and the KVR fraud-detection rollout) and the Portfolio Financial IT conference agenda together act as an unofficial award log and actor map. Hiring signals are reachable but fragmented - Profession.hu plus white-labelled *.karrierportal.hu career sites (MKB/MBH, GIRO) and MNB's toborzas portal are the tractable, ToS-safe feeds, while the highest-yield sources (LinkedIn, Indeed, Glassdoor) are login-walled and need a manual analyst routine rather than automation. Capex fingerprints are well anchored: listed-bank integrated annual reports sit as open PDFs on bet.hu and otpbank.hu with a published financial calendar, though whether they name IT vendors rather than only aggregate intangible capex is still untested. Main gaps are actor and vendor breadth (most banks and all major integrators still lack attached feeds) and the absence of any payments-hardware subcontracting portal, which pushes second-tier supplier discovery onto the subcontractor fields of kozbeszerzes.hu result notices.

For Hungary's foreign-owned banks, procurement authority is demonstrably located outside Hungary: Erste Bank Hungary publicly states that a group procurement unit is its EXCLUSIVE procurement service provider and that tenders run on the Erste Group Procurement online platform, while RBI runs a centralised Vienna Group Procurement function and UniCredit reportedly consolidated supplier tendering onto SAP Ariba at group level. None of these group channels publish award notices, so the only genuinely open, structured feeds in this cell are the IFI portals — EBRD's ECEPP (with EBRD Hungary Project Summary Documents as an early-warning layer) and the EIB/EIF supplier portal plus EU Funding & Tenders Portal / TED. The most productive indirect route is regulatory: MNB recommendation 7/2020 forces Hungarian institutions to keep outsourcing registers naming the external provider AND its parent company, and banks' business rules must list outsourced activities and their performers — making group-decided vendor relationships partially visible from the Hungarian side. Local-language searching works well for 'beszerzés/beszállító/kiszervezés/csoportszintű' but there is no Hungarian handle for cross-border framework awards, so detection must run primarily in German, Italian, Dutch and English against the parent groups.

Hungary's state-owned and state-influenced financial buyers publish on three overlapping layers: their own statutory 'közérdekű adatok / gazdálkodási adatok' disclosure pages (MNB, MFB, EXIM, Diákhitel, KELER, Kincstár), the national EKR platform plus the Közbeszerzési Értesítő/KBA gazette, and TED for above-threshold procedures — with Magyar Posta additionally running a genuine supplier-facing tender page whose documentation is gated behind SAP Ariba. The highest-value payments infrastructure buyer, GIRO Zrt. (BKR/AFR operator, MNB-owned), appears to publish no tender page of its own, so its vendor pipeline must be inferred from EKR filings, business rules and trade-press reporting. A structural complication is DKÜ (Korm. rendelet 301/2018), the central purchasing body that absorbs much government-sector IT procurement into framework agreements, meaning buyer-name-filtered feeds will under-count unless DKÜ frameworks are tracked in parallel. All meaningful content is Hungarian-language; English surfaces exist only on TED and partial EXIM/EN pages and should never be the primary collection route.

For non-financial Hungarian public buyers, the procurement trail is layered: EKR is where procedures actually run, the Közbeszerzési Értesítő is the national notice of record, CoRe holds the concluded contracts (and thus renewal windows), and TED covers only the largest above-threshold cases such as BKK's e-ticket programme. The high-volume, low-value end — small municipalities, hospitals and schools buying account services or a handful of POS terminals — largely escapes all four and surfaces instead in képviselő-testületi előterjesztések, per-buyer közérdekű adatok pages and annual közbeszerzési tervek, which are fragmented across thousands of Hungarian-only sites with no central index. Two structural forces must be tracked alongside the notices: the Treasury's decentralised municipal account-keeping (194/2025. Korm. rendelet, live since Oct 2025, free to municipalities) is shrinking the commercial municipal-account market, while EFER/SZEÜSZ centralises card and e-payment acceptance so that many bodies join a state rail instead of tendering acquiring. Commercial aggregators (KHF.hu, Tender-Értesítő) are worth a subscription as a completeness cross-check rather than as a primary feed.

Hungary's systemic private banks buy entirely outside the public procurement system, and only three of the nine maintain a genuinely vendor-facing public presence: OTP (a full szállítóknak hub with self-registration to vendorman@otpbank.hu, prequalification, a partner code of ethics and a gradual SAP Ariba rollout), Erste Bank Hungary (an unusually informative CSR page naming the Erste Group Hungarian procurement unit as exclusive procurement provider, with supplier counts and an EUR 100k CSR threshold, sourcing run on the Erste Group Procurements platform), and Raiffeisen (a published procurement GTC PDF). CIB and K&H disclose procurement principles only inside CSR/sustainability documents, MBH only inside its code of ethics, while UniCredit, Gránit and the Magyar Bankholding legacy names yield nothing vendor-facing at all — and no bank in the cell publishes open RFx, so real tender traffic lives on invitation-only e-sourcing platforms. The practical collection strategy is therefore: monitor the handful of onboarding/policy pages and their PDFs for change; identify the platform each bank sources on; and fill the award-level blind spot indirectly through MFB's published EKR summaries as a category benchmark, EKR/Közbeszerzési Értesítő bidder records, MNB's outsourcing regime, registry data and vendor-side announcements. Note a major vocabulary trap: 'beszállítói' on Hungarian bank sites usually means supply-chain-finance products sold to clients, not the bank's own purchasing.

For Hungarian bank and retailer technology deals, the seller is almost always the discloser: private-sector awards surface in vendor newsrooms (Tietoevry, Oracle, Intellect/iGCB, Euronet, Diebold Nixdorf, GK), in vendor customer-reference libraries (Asseco Case Study Book, Comarch, Broadcom PDF case studies), and in Hungarian-language 'referenciáink'/blog pages of domestic suppliers (4iG, DSS Consulting, Mindspire, Smartsector). There is no Hungary-scoped aggregator of such announcements, so coverage must be assembled vendor-by-vendor and swept via pointer feeds (FinTech Futures Hungary keyword page, IBS Intelligence, Financial IT, The Paypers, ATM Marketplace, Bitport.hu). Hungarian-language yield was materially lower than English because local vendors publish wins under reference/case-study vocabulary rather than 'sajtóközlemény'; the query set must be rebuilt around 'referenciáink', 'esettanulmány', 'ügyfeleink' and Hungarian bank aliases (MKB/Budapest Bank/Takarék → MBH). Biggest remaining holes are fraud/AML and open-banking vendors, and the retailer-side POS/self-checkout vendor landscape.

Hungary's integrator layer is unusually legible on the public-buyer side and unusually opaque on the bank side. Public-sector deals are traceable end-to-end through a three-tier stack — EKR and the Közbeszerzési Értesítő for notices, the DKÜ framework portal for who is pre-qualified to sell to the state at all, and TED/OpenTender for structured supplier-level history — with NISZ's HUF 5M+ supplier register and 4iG's BÉT filings giving line-item and revenue-segment corroboration. Commercial bank engagements have no equivalent register: the highest-value substitute discovered is the mandatory outsourcing annex (kiszervezett tevékenységek) that HU banks bury in their ÁSZF document trees, which names actual IT suppliers, backed by vendor reference pages (Dorsum, T-Systems, Asseco CE), the HFA/Infoklaszter member directories for actor discovery, and e-beszámoló filings to size firms whose revenue jumps betray an unannounced win. Hungarian-language search was essential and decisive — the DKÜ framework portal, the NISZ register, the outsourcing annex and the Átlátszó/K-Monitor analytical layer are effectively invisible to English-only queries — while CEE-headquartered integrators (Asseco, Comarch) require Polish/English collection instead.

Hungary's payments-procurement intelligence rests on three official backbones — EKR, the Közbeszerzési Értesítő and TED — which carry public-sector VPOS/POS/acquiring tenders and award notices (a live 2025 VPOS tender by the national toll operator confirms the pattern), plus MNB's PSD2 TPP data and Intézménykereső as the authoritative actor registers. The qvik/AFR layer is scheme-driven rather than tender-driven: MNB's qvik hub and GIRO Zrt. set the rules, while SimplePay/OTP Mobil, Barion and a small set of sub-aggregators announce integrations and merchant wins on their own sites and in FinTechZone before any registry reflects them. Two structural blind spots dominate: there is no public HU acquirer/scheme-certification roster and no public qvik participant directory, and private merchant acquiring RFPs never surface until a vendor press release. Collection should therefore combine API-level procurement monitoring with diffing of acquirer ÁSZF documents, provider developer docs and HU trade-press feeds.

Hungary's register layer for banking and payments is unusually well-consolidated: MNB's Intézménykereső, market-participant search, intermediary register, open-banking PSP list and Aranykönyv together enumerate and size every licensed bank, payment institution, e-money issuer and agent, with the EBA PSD2 central register providing the same universe in bulk-downloadable form. Identity and money then resolve through the Ministry of Justice stack — e-Cégjegyzék for legal identity, e-beszámoló for annual reports whose notes often disclose IT capex, outsourcing and contract values, and Cégközlöny for corporate-event flow — supplemented by NAV's free tax-status databases for validation and bidder eligibility. None of these expose a documented API: expect polite HTML collection, an emailed grouped-query form for beszámoló, or a licensed dataset from the Céginformációs Szolgálat or OPTEN, and note that the free company-register tier explicitly forbids bulk acquisition. For actual contract evidence outside formal tender portals, the üvegzseb regime (net HUF 5m+ contracts, indexed at kozadat.hu but authoritative on each institution's own disclosure page) is the register-side complement to the procurement cells.

Hungary's contract-transparency layer has three tiers: a statutory national one (kozadat.hu / Központi Információs Közadat-nyilvántartás for all contracts at or above HUF 5 million, EKR for procedures and notices, the Közbeszerzési Hatóság's CoRe repository for signed contracts, plus KEF framework agreements and DKÜ for state IT buys), an EU tier (TED bulk XML/OCDS, which reliably captures above-threshold banking and payment awards and is by far the most machine-readable), and a civic-archive tier (K-Monitor's tagged database and aHáló network graph, Transparency International's Tenderbajnok, RedFlags, and Átlátszó's KiMitTud FOI archive) that cleans, links and preserves what the official portals bury or amend. For this sector the decisive caveat is legal: bank secrecy under the Hpt. is treated as lex specialis to the Info Act and has been used successfully to withhold entire contracts with financial institutions, and as of August 2026 the MNB is refusing its own 5 M Ft contract list with a NAIH complaint pending — so silence in the registers is not evidence of no deal. Practically, no confirmed public bulk endpoint was found for the Hungarian national systems, so collection should lean on TED/Opentender for structured history, scripted querying of kozadat.hu, EKR and CoRe for national coverage, and KiMitTud FOI requests as an active channel where proactive publication fails.

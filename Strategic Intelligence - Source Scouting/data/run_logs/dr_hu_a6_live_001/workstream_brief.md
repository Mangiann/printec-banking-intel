Hungary's public-procurement record for banking and payment technology is concentrated in three official layers: EKR (ekr.gov.hu) as the mandatory transaction platform, with dedicated public surfaces for procedure search, award notices (eredménytájékoztató) and a public contract register; the Közbeszerzési Hatóság estate (kozbeszerzes.hu) carrying the daily Közbeszerzési Értesítő gazette, notice search, KBA/CoRe registers and the Döntőbizottság remedy decisions; and TED for everything above EU threshold in structured form. The Döntőbizottság decisions are the sleeper asset in this cell — unlike award notices they name the losing challengers and quote technical specs and scores. Two structural limits shape everything: private commercial banks are not contracting authorities and so never appear here, and KEF framework call-offs can consummate central-government purchases without an individual award notice. Practical collection is filter-driven rather than source-driven, so the immediate blockers are taxonomic (fixing the CPV code set) and lexical (deriving real Hungarian notice phrasing from observed notices instead of translated seed terms), plus supplier-name normalisation before any award counting.

For Hungary, the EU-level procurement layer is dominated by one authoritative spine — TED, reachable through its HTML portal in both English and Hungarian, a no-auth Search API, and daily/monthly XML bulk packages that preserve award history national portals purge. Around it sit three useful rings: a research aggregator layer (Opentender/DIGIWHIST, OCP registry) that fuses TED with below-threshold Hungarian data from the Közbeszerzési Hatóság; the national originals (Közbeszerzési Értesítő and EKR) where the Hungarian-language technical annexes and product specs actually live; and EU-funded award databases (Kohesio, the Financial Transparency System, and Hungary's own Támogatott Projektkereső plus KÖFOP tables) that expose digitalisation projects and beneficiaries never tendered EU-wide. The main structural caveat is that a CPV-keyed slice is only as good as the CPV codes Hungarian buyers actually use — 66133000 produced no verified Hungarian example in this pass, and ATM procurement appears to sit outside public tendering entirely — so the CPV set should be derived empirically from the TED API before any collector is fixed. Commercial banks remain a deliberate blind spot of this cell and must be covered elsewhere.

Hungarian hiring signals for banking and payments split into three tiers: first-party employer ATS (OTP, MBH, Erste, K&H, Raiffeisen, plus the two highest-signal infrastructure bodies GIRO Zrt. and MNB's toborzas.mnb.hu), the national job boards (profession.hu dominant, then cvonline.hu, jobline.hu, the IT-niche dreamjobs.hu and hwsw.hu), and aggregators (Indeed HU, Jooble, Jobmonitor, Állástérkép) that are best used as discovery pointers back to the canonical ad. Vendor-side entry signals are almost entirely English and live on global ATS instances (Temenos Workday, Diebold Nixdorf Oracle CX) and LinkedIn rather than any HU-localised career microsite, which is the main asymmetry in this cell. Consultancies and body-shops (Mindspire, Core Solutions, Hays, Randstad) act as pointer sources that convert anonymous demand into named programmes, with Mindspire's core-banking transformation blog unusually explicit. The binding constraint is ephemerality plus access: ads expire within weeks and the two richest vendor sources are auth-walled, so this cell only works as a scheduled snapshot-and-diff pipeline over the open sources with a manual analyst lane for LinkedIn and Glassdoor.

Hungary's conference-and-awards layer is unusually legible: two commercial organisers dominate and both publish structured sponsor tiers — Portfolio.hu (Banking Technology, Financial IT, Future of Finance) with a stable /program, /eloadok, /tamogatok URL pattern going back to 2017, and FinTechZone's *TechShow family (FinTechShow, BankTechShow, PayTechShow, TechShow X in Oct 2026) which additionally publishes a separate kiállítók/exhibitor tier where smaller payments and POS vendors appear. Payment-specific coverage comes from IIR Hungary's Kártyapiac series and businessevents.hu's AFR 2.0 Digital Payment conference, while merchant-side POS and cash-register vendors surface instead in HUNGEXPO trade-fair exhibitor directories such as Sirha Budapest. The awards layer is anchored by Mastercard's Év Bankja (segment winners 2025: OTP overall, Gránit in Innovation, K&H in Beyond Banking), IVSZ's Gyurós Tibor-díj (which explicitly awards 'digitalisation project of the year', naming supplier and client), the Magyar Innovációs Nagydíj, and Az Év Honlapja, with MNB's Lámfalussy Lectures and Budapest Eurasia Forum supplying the regulator-adjacent network. Sponsor-page structure is confirmed but company-level rosters are largely uncollected — these URLs are targets, and none should be treated as verified-live until the deterministic pass runs.

Hungary's company-register and filings layer is unusually strong for vendor intelligence: registration and annual-accounts publication are legally mandatory, so every vendor's HU legal entity, owners, TEÁOR/NACE activity and audited revenue sit in two free state portals — e-cégjegyzék (register, with the paid occsz certificate channel) and e-beszamolo.im.gov.hu (beszámoló search and PDF download) — with Cégközlöny carrying court-ordered formation, change and liquidation notices that flag quiet market entries and exits first. On top of this sits a mature commercial layer (Opten Cégtár and the free Nemzeti Cégtár, Dun & Bradstreet Hungary ex-Bisnode, Céginfo.hu, iCégtár) that adds ownership link-networks and parent-company resolution, plus verification layers at NAV (VAT/enforcement/debt status) and KSH (statisztikai számjel, TEÁOR), and BRIS via the EU e-Justice portal for cross-border parentage. Spot checks confirm the sector's actors resolve cleanly here — Diebold Nixdorf Kft. (ex-Wincor Nixdorf, Vecsés), NCR Magyarország Kft., and a Diebold self-service entity in végelszámolás — while Worldline/Nexi did not surface under those names, underscoring that alias, legacy-name and fióktelep handling is essential. The main constraints are that there is no free bulk export (the Ministry sells the filings dataset), no documented public API, and the UBO register is closed by law, so plan for licensed data or polite form-driven collection in Hungarian.

For Hungarian non-bank self-service and payment-accepting buyers the source landscape splits cleanly in two. State-linked buyers (Magyar Posta, BKK, MÁV-START/Volánbusz, municipal parking companies, hospitals) are well documented through mandatory channels - EKR, the Közbeszerzési Értesítő, üvegzseb contract disclosures and their own press rooms - which name winning suppliers, quantities and contract values, and these should be the backbone of collection. Private buyers (Tesco, SPAR, Lidl, Auchan, MOL, FOXPOST/Packeta) are visible only through retailer press releases, Hungarian trade media (Trade magazin, Store Insider, economx/vg/index) and vendor or integrator reference pages, which quantify installed base but almost never name the supplier - the main evidence gap in this cell. Two regulatory registers, NAV's e-pénztárgép distribution licences and the metrology authority's cash-register approvals, act as a de facto whitelist of who may legally sell checkout hardware in Hungary and are the most under-used structured source here. Key uncovered areas remain healthcare, utilities, TED at EU level, and cash-handling as distinct from card acceptance.

Hungary's subsidy transparency layer is unusually rich for this cell: a single national register (palyazat.gov.hu Támogatott projektkereső, ~529k records, daily-updated from EMIR/EUPR/IIER, with an export function) plus EU-level mirrors (Kohesio, Cohesion Open Data, the State Aid Transparency Award Module) make beneficiaries, amounts and project scope of GINOP/GINOP Plusz, DIMOP Plusz, KÖFOP and RRF digitalisation projects systematically enumerable. The highest-leverage vendor source is the MKIK 'Vállalkozz digitálisan!' accredited supplier catalogue, because DIMOP Plusz SME digitalisation calls force purchases from it — effectively a state-maintained roster of eligible IT and payment-adjacent suppliers — complemented by MFB's published list of financial intermediaries that distribute the combined loan-plus-grant instruments, which doubles as a map of which banks sit in the EU-money channel. The structural weakness is that grant registers name the beneficiary and never the implementing supplier, so private-sector projects must be joined to procurement notices, vendor case studies or press to identify who actually built the system. Local-language search was essential and decisive here: the accreditation catalogue, the MFB intermediary requirement, the DIMOP managing authority site and the Treasury/NAV project disclosures surfaced only through Hungarian queries, while English queries reached the EU-level aggregators.

English-language international trade and analyst coverage of Hungary is real but thin and event-driven: Finextra (especially its vendor press-release channel), FinTech Futures' Hungary keyword feed, IBS Intelligence's news and case-study library, The Paypers, Retail Banker International and PYMNTS between them capture most major Hungarian bank technology selections, but almost exclusively for OTP Bank and Erste Bank Hungary. The distinctive value of the cell is that these outlets reprint vendor-supplied scope and module detail that Hungarian domestic media omit, so the highest-yield tactic is snowballing each trade article upstream to the originating vendor newsroom. For market sizing, no free source exists: GlobalData, Euromonitor, Mordor and Verified Market Research all gate Hungary payments/POS figures behind licences, though Mordor's public companies page usefully leaks a named Hungarian POS terminal vendor roster. This cell must be treated as an aggregation and translation layer over other cells, never as a standalone view of the Hungarian market.

MNB is by far the richest single publisher for Hungarian banking and payments intelligence, and it operates in three distinct layers: statutory registers (Intézménykereső, the open-banking ASPSP/TPP directory, cross-border passporting lists) that enumerate every licensed payment institution, e-money issuer and agent; statistical outputs (statisztika.mnb.hu payment table set, and the institution-level Aranykönyv) that quantify the card, POS, acceptance-point and instant-payment market and can size individual competitors; and narrative publications (Fizetési rendszer jelentés, FinTech és Digitalizációs Jelentés, Felvigyázás/oversight pages, Hitelintézeti Szemle) that name infrastructure operators and describe technology adoption. Everything is public, free and official, mostly Hungarian-first with a partial English mirror, and format is predominantly HTML search UIs plus XLSX and PDF downloads — no confirmed API. The principal limitation for the A6 workstream is that MNB names legal entities and aggregate volumes but never the technology suppliers behind them, and publishes no per-acquirer market-share table, so vendor and competitor-share questions must be triangulated from the Aranykönyv, procurement sources and the GIRO/KELER infrastructure layer that MNB's oversight pages point to.

All eight Hungarian systemic banks maintain a self-published press room and an investor/annual-report section, and these are reachable, stable, and mostly crawlable without authentication — CIB and MBH even expose predictable per-release URL patterns. However, none of them discloses suppliers systematically: vendor identity appears only when the bank chooses to announce a win (OTP–iGCB Intellect Digital Core, Magyar Bankholding/MBH–Thought Machine) or when it is buried in annual-report outsourcing notes and terms-and-conditions documents. The highest-yield collection design therefore pairs each bank's own press room and annual report with three indirect confirmers: vendor-side and trade-press announcements (FinTechZone in Hungarian, FinTech Futures in English), the Portfolio Banking Technology conference programme as a bank–vendor relationship roster, and operational artefacts such as K&H's maintenance notices. Beware the false friend 'beszállítói', which in Hungarian bank content means supply-chain finance products rather than the bank's own suppliers.

Hungary's payments infrastructure layer is unusually well documented for a normally opaque niche, because its two core operators are subject to Hungarian 'üvegzseb' transparency: GIRO Zrt. (BKR, GIROInstant/AFR, qvik) publishes a dated supplier-contract register naming its IT vendors, and KELER/KELER KSZF publish a contract-disclosure section — together the highest-value primary sources in this cell. Around them sit the MNB as owner-and-overseer (payment infrastructure pages, annual Fizetési rendszer jelentés, qvik participant hub), which supplies the authoritative actor map and forward mandate signals such as AFR 2.0. The card layer is split between scheme certification regimes that function as supplier directories — Visa's Global Registry of Service Providers is openly searchable, while Mastercard's GVCP certified-vendor listing is behind Mastercard Connect — and a concentrated personalisation/processing hub set: ANY Biztonsági Nyomda (BÉT-listed, works with Thales DIS), CARDNET, Euronet, with Worldline and Nexi Central Europe pressing in from the region. The main blind spot is that no public source attributes the GIROInstant core platform to a named technology vendor; that fact must be triangulated from the contract register, recruitment adverts and MNB documentation.

Hungary's acquiring/PSP landscape is unusually well documented from primary sources: an oligopolistic acquirer core (OTP Mobil/SimplePay, Global Payments HU via the Erste channel, Worldline/SIX, Nexi Central Europe, Euronet) that publishes aggressively in Hungarian on its own sites, wrapped by an authoritative regulator layer (MNB Intézménykereső for licences, MNB Fizetési rendszer jelentés for POS/acceptance counts, and the GVH 2019 sectoral inquiry into card acceptance) and by GIRO Zrt., which effectively maintains a live participant roster for instant payment and qvik. The richest partnership signal is not press releases but structural lists: SimplePay's technical-partner directory, GIRO's GIROInstant participant list, Ecommerce Hungary's 200+ member base and webshop-platform integration catalogues. Secondary Hungarian trade media — FinTechZone above all, then Portfolio.hu, Kosárérték and Prim — function as the pointer layer and are the practical substitute where a vendor has no discoverable newsroom (Barion, Revolut HU, Wise HU). Main remaining gaps are the non-OTP bank acquirers, the softPOS/challenger tier, and public procurement, which is where the largest Hungarian acceptance contracts are actually awarded.

For Hungary, international vendor customer-reference material is highly uneven: payments software and ATM outsourcing vendors publish rich named-client evidence, while hardware terminal and security vendors publish essentially none. The two anchor sources are ACI Worldwide's IR newsroom (OTP Bank Group, Erste Bank Hungary, Takarekinfo, plus the ACI/Capsys/T-Systems claim of ~75% of HU real-time payments) and Euronet's IR archive (Budapest Bank ATM outsourcing since 1997, ABN AMRO Hungary, Bank Austria Creditanstalt); Finastra (Raiffeisen core retail) and NCR Atleos (Budapest ATM manufacturing plant) are secondary anchors, while Diebold Nixdorf, Temenos, Ingenico, Verifone, PAX, INETCO and Thales yielded no HU-named public reference in this pass. Hungarian-language search did not produce vendor case studies at all — its value was in surfacing the local delivery layer (Capsys, T-Systems Magyarország), HU legal entities of the vendors, and local trade press that names implementation suppliers. Collection should therefore centre on scheduled crawls of vendor IR/newsroom indexes plus newswire and trade-press mirrors, with local-language integrator and registry sources used to corroborate and to snowball beyond the original vendor list.

The Hungarian bank-IT competitive set splits into three tiers of source: listed or group-backed primes (4iG, T-Systems Magyarország) whose regulated disclosures and press rooms are rich and reliable; specialist domestic ISVs and payment operators (Dorsum, CARDNET, NETLOCK, Integra, Mindspire) whose own blogs and product pages occasionally name bank clients outright; and mid-size integrators (Grepton, Sysdata PSE) that publish capability language but no bank references at all. Because vendor reference lists are largely absent, the highest-value sources for this cell are structural rather than promotional: the Közbeszerzési Adatbázis/EKR procurement registry and the e-beszamolo statutory accounts portal, which together attribute contracts and size the banking revenue behind the marketing. Trade press (Bitport, HWSW, ITBusiness) and the annual Portfolio Banking Technology programme supply the connective tissue — win/loss stories and a yearly census of who is actively selling to Hungarian banks. Collection must be Hungarian-first; note that 'Sysdata' resolves to Sysdata PSE Kft. and the intended local wealth-management ISV is Dorsum Zrt., not Docler.

Hungarian bank-IT deal intelligence lives in three distinct press layers: a credible business-financial tier (Portfolio.hu, vg.hu, Forbes.hu, Növekedés.hu) that reports budgets, strategy and executive statements; an ICT trade tier (Bitport, HWSW, ITBusiness, SG.hu, Prim.hu) that is the only place where bank + vendor + integrator are actually named together — HWSW broke the MBH/Thought Machine core-platform choice, Bitport documented OTP's Hitachi storage build with reseller Invigor; and an agency-published fintech tier (FinTechZone, Fintech.hu, BankTechShow) with the highest deal density but the weakest independence. Portfolio's Banking Technology and ITBusiness's Finance & Technology conference pages, plus the Mindspire transformation blog, function as pointer sources that enumerate the supplier base and the status of core-banking renewal programmes. The practical constraint is that none of these outlets tag vendor relationships, external search indexes cover their archives poorly, and the richest reporting is paywalled — so collection must be on-site/RSS keyword-and-NER driven, with per-item provenance flags separating reported fact from vendor claim.

Hungary's association layer gives an unusually clean, three-tier actor map: Magyar Bankszövetség enumerates the demand side (~60-70 credit institutions plus infrastructure bodies GIRO, KELER, BISZ), the Hungarian Fintech Association (hunfintech.com) is the densest single page of banking/payments vendors and challengers (Barion, Revolut, Mastercard, Visa, Dorsum, Finshape, Billingo, BÉT), and IVSZ's ~19-page tagok directory captures the long tail of Hungarian IT suppliers invisible to English search. Chambers (MKIK's KNYR registry, county chambers, BKIK's financial-services section) act as the near-census fallback for entity resolution, while niche bodies — Lízingszövetség with its supplier-revealing 'pártoló tagok' page, Faktoring Szövetség, Ecommerce Hungary, DKSZ, Blockchain Hungary's ecosystem map, the AI Coalition roster — fill in adjacent segments. The main structural weaknesses are that no independent Hungarian card/PSP association exists (that agenda sits inside Bankszövetség working groups and must otherwise be sourced from the regulator), that the e-commerce bodies publish no structured member directory, and that IVSZ offers no financial-services facet, so vendor isolation requires full pagination plus per-company enrichment.

Montenegro's A2 landscape has an unusually clean primary spine: since 1 January 2021 every stage of a public procurement - plan, javni poziv, tenderska dokumentacija, decisions, complaints and contracts - must run through CeJN (cejn.gov.me), the Belit-built, EU IPA-funded national e-procurement system operated by the Directorate for Public Procurement Policy (ujn.gov.me), with the pre-2021 record still searchable free of charge on the legacy eTender portal (portalujn.gov.me). The practical obstacle is not availability but machinability: CeJN is a JavaScript-rendered application with no discovered API, feed or bulk export, and its notice text is not indexed by general search engines, so payments lots cannot be found by web search - collection will need a headless browser or the commercial mirror nabavke.com, which rehosts CeJN tender documents under /tdoc-cejn/. Three material blind spots sit outside the portal: simple procurements below EUR 15,000 (many POS/terminal orders) that surface only in semi-annual authority reports; the Central Bank's own tenderi-i-oglasi page; and IFI-financed payments infrastructure - most notably the World Bank's July 2025 EUR 8m TIPS Clone / SEPA alignment project for CBCG, which will procure under Bank rules through Finances One and STEP rather than CeJN. Outcome and dispute detail is best recovered from the appeals commission's rjesenja at kontrola-nabavki.me and from State Audit Institution reports at dri.co.me.

Montenegro's outcome side is legally centralised in CEJN (cejn.gov.me), the state e-procurement system that must carry annual plans, award decisions, concluded contracts and execution reports — but it is a client-rendered SPA that yields nothing to a plain fetch, so the practical harvesting route is the private mirror nabavke.com, which serves the same CEJN documents at stable, indexable URLs (a confirmed pattern: /tdoc-cejn/documents/{id}-...-Odluka_o_izboru_najpovoljnije_ponude_{id}.pdf). Around that core sit three complementary channels: the appeals body kontrola-nabavki.me, whose rulings expose contested awards and losing bidders; UJN's annual Izvještaj o javnim nabavkama plus DRI audits and Institut alternativa/MANS commentary, which quantify and critique the contract population (~EUR 990m contracted in 2025, 12.44% of GDP); and TED for EU/IPA-financed Montenegrin award notices in structured form. Two structural constraints define the collector design: sector-relevant awards are effectively invisible to keyword web search and must be retrieved by CPV code and by contracting authority (CBCG above all), and private commercial banks fall entirely outside the procurement law, so for them no award register exists and vendor selections can only be inferred from bank saopštenja and trade press.

Montenegro's pointer layer for A2 is unusually well-formed on the financial-regulator side: CBCG publishes a compact set of official registers (banks; payment systems; payment institutions and agents; e-money institutions and agents; plus a revoked-authorisation negative list), and CRPS — reachable via pretraga.crps.me, crps.me and efirma.tax.gov.me — is the master company register, now carrying the beneficial-ownership register inside it since 2021. On the procurement side the enumerable populations exist but sit behind the CEJN/ESJN e-procurement system (registered ponuđači and the naručilac roster) with ujn.gov.me as the policy and reporting front end; AZZK holds the state-aid and de-minimis registers but appears to gate them behind an access request. The critical miss is fiscalisation: no public list of approved fiscal-device/POS software producers surfaced despite mandatory local-language searching, so the terminal supply chain currently has to be reconstructed indirectly from vendor self-declaration sites plus procurement awards — closing that gap by going directly to the tax authority should be the next action for this cell.

Montenegro's third-party tender-aggregation layer splits into three tiers: strong local/regional commercial services (eKapija Tenderi with its twice-daily bulletin, TenderiLIVE across ME/BA/RS, and the ME-specific eTenderi.me with competitor win-rate analytics and a State Commission appeals-decision archive), a horizontal global aggregator family (MontenegroTenders, TendersOnTime, TendersInfo, GlobalTenders) of lower and largely unverified quality, and an IFI/donor tier (EBRD ECEPP, WBIF, UNGM/UNDP, DevelopmentAid) that carries the donor-financed contracts the national CeJN portal never shows in the same form. Free structured alternatives are thin: opendata.gov.me exposes tender-tagged datasets and CeJN is the official baseline, but Montenegro is absent from opentender.eu/DIGIWHIST and no open-source CeJN scraper exists, so machine-readable ME procurement history effectively has to be bought or built. Pointer sources — Balkan Tender Watch (with MANS and Institute Alternative), the OCDS Data Registry, and the 2024 MAPS e-procurement assessment — are the best free routes to actor names, data-publication limits and known transparency failures such as unreported post-award contract amendments. No banking- or payments-specific vertical exists for this market, and every commercial platform's pricing, redistribution rights and incremental recall over the free baseline remain unverified.

Montenegro's audit-and-watchdog layer for procurement is small, well-defined and unusually productive: the State Audit Institution (DRI) anchors it with a two-decade archive of regularity, performance and — importantly for this run — dedicated information-system audits that name systems, vendors and IT-governance failures, while the Agency for Prevention of Corruption (ASK) adds annual/quarterly integrity and conflict-of-interest reporting. On the civil-society side, Institut alternativa supplies the quantitative work (shares of spending via direct agreements, urgent and shortened procedures, simple purchases, per-institution breakdowns) plus two NGO-built spending databases (mojnovac.me, mojgrad.me), MANS supplies FOI-extracted contracts that surface in the gov.me and parliamentary 'approved access' archives, and CGO, CIN-CG and the regional Balkan Tender Watch coalition fill in municipal, investigative and cross-country benchmarking angles; SIGMA/OECD and the OGP IRM provide external, methodologically consistent assessment. The key practical caveats: DRI's site failed TLS verification on every fetch attempt in this pass (almost certainly a certificate-chain issue on our side, not a dead source — do not mark it dead), MANS's procurement category index renders far thinner than the organisation's real output, and no watchdog product was found that targets banking or payment-sector IT procurement specifically. For the banking/payments scope this cell should therefore be worked entity-first — pulling DRI audits of financially-relevant auditees and grepping them for 'informacioni sistem' line items and supplier names — rather than topic-first.

Montenegro's media channel for tender and contract intelligence splits into three tiers: a small set of open, directly-confirmed local publishers that actually carry procurement detail (Bankar.me for banking-sector specificity, Investitor.me for central-bank equipment tenders, Dan for aggregate procurement-accountability numbers, and MANS as the NGO watchdog whose documents feed the press), a set of flagship dailies the cell brief names (Vijesti, Pobjeda, CdM, RTCG) that my searches never surfaced directly and which must be verified by live fetch before being trusted, and a layer of paywalled aggregators and regional/English outlets (eKapija, Tenderi Live, SeeNews, Bloomberg Adria, montenegrobusiness.eu) useful for corroboration but not for unlicensed automated collection. The clearest confirmed signal patterns are cancelled and legally challenged tenders — Montenegro was reported as the Western Balkans record-holder for annulled procedures, and a worked ANPR software case shows the discriminatory-documentation complaint template — plus institutional payment-infrastructure milestones (CBCG instant payments, EPC licence, treasury equipment tender) that precede procurement. Notably, no media report of a Montenegrin commercial bank procuring an information system was found in local-language search, which supports the cell's premise that private-bank contracts surface only obliquely and suggests future queries should target vendor-side and signing-ceremony language rather than tender vocabulary. The largest remaining gaps are regional ICT/fintech trade press, the banks' own press and tender pages, and Cyrillic-script querying, none of which this run reached before the search budget ran out.

Montenegro's weak-signal layer is small but unusually legible: with roughly a dozen licensed banks, a single dominant conference (INFINTECH, run by local software house Logate at Porto Montenegro) and one dominant ATM/POS servicer (Payten/ASEE, which publicly states its installed base), the vendor-to-bank map can largely be reconstructed without any procurement notice. The highest-yield channels found are conference sponsor/agenda pages, bank careers pages plus the national job boards (zaposli.me, posao.crna.gora.me, pazar3.me, prekoveze.me) with bankar.me acting as a durable archive of expired vacancy ads, and app-store package metadata that leaks each bank's digital-banking and SmartPOS supplier. Capex intent is retrievable from CBCG's bank financial-statement repository and from SCMN/Montenegroberza issuer prospectuses, both of which require PDF mining rather than a feed. The main uncovered ground is vendor partner-locator pages for hardware and core-banking brands, the non-bank PSP population, and LinkedIn, which is login-walled and ToS-restricted and therefore must be handled by manual analyst review rather than collection.

Montenegro's procurement legal framework is unusually well centralised for a small market: the Directorate for Public Procurement Policy at the Ministry of Finance (ujn.gov.me) publishes the consolidated Zakon o javnim nabavkama (Sl. list CG 074/19, 003/23, 011/23) together with its bylaws, contracting-authority lists and annual reports, while the CEJN platform (cejn.gov.me), built by Belit under an EC/IPA-funded project, is the mandated publication venue and therefore the single point of failure for every downstream notice cell. The reform pipeline is dated and specific rather than speculative: Chapter 5 closing benchmarks have been assessed as met, with binding commitments to amend the procurement and anti-corruption laws and to add a red-flag irregularity module to the e-procurement system by Q2 2026, alongside a separate Ministry of Finance track toward mandatory e-invoicing using a Serbian-licensed centralised platform phased over 2025-2026. Interpretation of the rules is carried by the Komisija za zaštitu prava u postupcima javnih nabavki decision archive and, independently, by SIGMA/OECD assessments and Institut alternativa watchdog research; commercial legal databases (paragraf.me, me.propisi.net) and law-firm guides are useful but demonstrably lag, with the CMS Montenegro chapter still describing a superseded review body and voluntary e-procurement. The main blind spots this run leaves open are the official gazette itself, the state audit institution, primary EU Chapter 5 documents, and the exemption regimes (bilateral intergovernmental agreements, defence/security, PPP) under which contracts legally never appear on CEJN at all.

Montenegro's procurement oversight layer is unusually well-suited to source tracking: the appeals body (Komisija za zaštitu prava u postupcima javnih nabavki) publishes a filterable, full-text-searchable decision database at kontrola-nabavki.me that names contracting authorities, complainants and winning bidders in contested lots, and its decisions are appealable to the Upravni sud whose judgments are also published. The policy side sits with the Direktorat za politiku javnih nabavki (ujn.gov.me plus gov.me/mif), which issues the annual national procurement reports carrying value-by-category aggregates — though the series is split across two hosts and incomplete, and the Commission's own work reports have no stable index. The statutory publication system CEJN (cejn.gov.me) is the join key linking appeals back to tender values, but its anonymous-access depth is unverified and its legacy predecessor is login-walled. Secondary calibration comes from DRI audits (including genuine IT-system audits), SIGMA/MAPS/EC assessments, and watchdogs MANS and Institut alternativa; no source offers a banking/payments-specific filter, so CPV and sr/en keyword filtering must be built collector-side.

Montenegro's SOE and utility buyers split their publication between the legally authoritative central portal (CeJN, cejn.gov.me) and their own websites, and the split is uneven enough that neither alone gives coverage: EPCG, CEDIS, Luka Bar, Aerodromi Crne Gore and Air Montenegro all maintain live self-published tender surfaces (Air Montenegro even runs a dedicated javnenabavke.* subdomain), while Pošta Crne Gore's own procurement page appears frozen around 2020 and Monteput and several municipal utilities publish no procurement index at all. The payments-relevant signal in this cell is concentrated in four buyers — Pošta (counter payment services, card terminals, in-branch ATMs, remittance partners), Monteput (cash, card and electronic toll collection), Aerodromi Crne Gore (public calls to place ATMs at Podgorica and Tivat) and EPCG/CEDIS (bill collection and mass metering) — each of which procures under the separate 'sektorski naručilac' utilities regime defined in the Zakon o javnim nabavkama. The critical enabling sources are the UJN 'Lista naručilaca' and 'Lista službenika' PDFs, which give an authoritative buyer roster plus a named procurement officer per buyer, and CeJN itself, which is live but JavaScript-rendered and returned only an app shell to a plain fetch — a tooling obstacle to solve, not a dead end. English-language search was near-useless here, collapsing into tender-resale SEO farms; every primary source in this slice was reached through Montenegrin-language queries anchored on the buyer's proper name.

CBCG's procurement footprint is genuinely split across three channels, and only one of them is obvious. Its own site carries a live but sparse 'Tenderi i oglasi' page — the richest cell-specific find, currently listing instant-payments (TIPS Clone) connectivity, pre-implementation technical support and UX/marketing calls alongside jubilee-coin and construction notices — while offering no procurement plan, no contract register and no dedicated 'javne nabavke' section at all. The national CEJN portal is the statutory home for its domestic procedures but is a JavaScript application that yielded nothing to a plain fetch, so it is currently uncollectable without headless rendering. Most consequentially, the flagship purchases bypass both: the CBCG treasury and cash-processing equipment tender (banknote and coin processing, euro anti-counterfeiting, gold testing; deadline 7 October 2026) runs on the EU Funding & Tenders Portal under EC-ENEST references administered by the Ministry of Finance, and the TIPS Clone instant-payments build is financed by an EUR 8m World Bank loan and delivered via Banca d'Italia — meaning EU-portal, TED and World Bank project/STEP feeds are mandatory sources for this cell, and national-portal-only monitoring would miss almost everything that matters.

Montenegro's donor-funded tender channel is a small, English-dominated, well-structured set of publication venues that sits almost entirely outside the national eProcurement system: TED and the EU Funding & Tenders Portal for IPA/PRAG contracts (with the EU Delegation and the Ministry of Finance CFCU as contracting authorities), ECEPP for EBRD client procurement, projects.worldbank.org plus Finances One for World Bank operations, UNDP's Montenegro RSS feed and UNGM for UN agencies, and WBIF/EIB for blended technical assistance. The single most on-target anchor right now is the World Bank's EUR 8m Financial Infrastructure Modernization and SEPA alignment (TIPS-clone) project, whose appraisal documentation names the payments-infrastructure work that will be tendered; its procurement plan is the highest-value next extraction. Local-language coverage is genuinely thin by nature of the channel — the only meaningful Montenegrin-language surface found is the Directorate for Public Procurement Policy's 'EU tenderi' category plus CFCU and Audit Authority material, which matter for award and execution intelligence rather than opportunity spotting. Commercial aggregators (dgMarket, TendersInfo and peers) duplicate these feeds behind paywalls and should be treated as non-collectable hints, never as sources.

Montenegro's commercial banks are private and, on the evidence of this run, publish essentially nothing about their procurement: NLB, Hipotekarna and Erste were fetched directly and none has a nabavka, tender, dobavljači or RFP section, and no ME bank was found issuing a 'poziv za dostavljanje ponuda' for technology — the phrase is used locally for share issues and real-estate disposals instead. The realistic source landscape for this cell is therefore a layered indirect one: the CBCG register of banks and UBCG (plus its 'Bankar' journal PDFs) as the actor-map pointers; EBRD's ECEPP and the EU Delegation's Montenegrin award notices as the only mechanisms that can force a private bank's sourcing into public view; Montenegroberza issuer filings and bank annual reports for contractual and outsourcing disclosures; and vendor-side press plus technical fingerprinting (an Asseco package id in the NLB Klik Crna Gora app listing is the clearest ME attribution found) as the highest-yield attribution route. The single biggest operational risk is that CKB — the market leader and most important buyer here — could not be read at all due to a TLS chain failure, which should be treated as a fixable client/config problem rather than a dead source.

For the foreign parents of Montenegro's banks there is no tender-notice layer to harvest: Erste, NLB, Addiko and OTP all buy centrally and none publishes an itemised competition feed covering the Montenegrin subsidiary. What exists instead is a three-tier landscape — onboarding gates (OTP's szállítóknak page routing into SAP Ariba; Erste Group Procurement in Vienna, which explicitly manages the entire external spend of all majority-owned group entities and publishes supplier terms, NDAs, audit questionnaires and scorecards), signal channels (group newsrooms at erstegroup.com, otpgroup.info and nlbgroup.com, plus sister-bank newsrooms such as otpbanka.hr that announce Montenegro-affecting decisions in Croatian), and disclosure mining (Addiko's Corporate Governance and Group Annual Reports, which confirm a Group procurement policy binding all entities above a threshold, and the EBA's DORA Register-of-Information regime, which inventories exactly these vendor relationships for the EU-supervised parents). The one genuine parent-level tender portal found is Turkish — Ziraat's ihale.ziraatbank.com.tr — and it needs a headless-browser retry before its category coverage can be judged. Local-language search was decisive but in an unexpected direction: Serbian queries produced only the actor map and the CBCG bank register, while every direct procurement source in this slice was reachable only in Hungarian, German, Slovenian or Turkish, and the bare English acronyms OTP and NLB are unusable, returning the Ontario Tenders Portal and the Northern Lighthouse Board respectively.

Supplier-side visibility in Montenegro is thin but structurally predictable: global OEMs (NCR Atleos, Diebold Nixdorf) almost never name Montenegrin buyers, while regional distributors and integrators do — Printec Montenegro (transaction automation since 2014), Asseco SEE via its Podgorica-area entity and the MoneyGet ATM network, Halcom in e-banking, and Payten/Monri in POS and e-commerce acquiring. Domestic integrators (Logate, ČIKOM, Amplitudo) publish contract-level detail, including values, in Montenegrin on their own sites, LinkedIn and eKapija, making local-language monitoring mandatory rather than optional. Where no announcement exists at all — which is the norm for core banking and digital onboarding — supplier attribution is still recoverable from unfalsifiable fingerprints such as mobile-app package IDs (e.g. NLB Podgorica's apps carry an Asseco package prefix) and hosting/subdomain evidence. Priority next steps are browser-grade retrieval of the vendor sites (printecgroup.com returned 403), a per-bank vendor attribution grid, and dedicated Montenegrin terminology for card personalisation and switch/processing, which remain unsearched.

Montenegro's payments-buyer landscape is small, register-transparent at the top and announcement-driven below it: the CBCG maintains authoritative registers of payment institutions and their agents (seven PIs observed, including Montenegro Transfers, PaySpot ME and Alta Pay Group), of payment systems, and of revoked authorisations, while the e-money register is presently empty — making it a market-entry tripwire rather than an inventory. Independent ATM deployment is a genuinely separate landscape here, with at least two non-bank fleet operators present (Euronet with a Montenegrin-language site, and Asseco/Payten's MoneyGet), and Pošta Crne Gore doubles as the largest non-bank payments distribution network and a contracting authority that publishes its own tenders. Formal demand signals concentrate in the CEJN e-procurement portal and in the World Bank-financed SEPA/TIPS-clone modernisation project, whose IFI procurement rules will expose supplier awards that domestic partnership deals never do. The weak flank is everything cash-side and processing-side: CIT operators, cash-centre contractors and the actual card processors behind each ME bank remain unidentified, and local-language queries need .me or 'Crna Gora' constraints because Serbian and Croatian results otherwise dominate.

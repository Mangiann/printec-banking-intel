Slovenia's public banking/payments tender intelligence has a single statutory creation point: the Portal javnih naročil operated by Uradni list RS at enarocanje.si (notice publication under ZJN-3 art. 52) paired with e-JN at ejn.gov.si (where procedures are actually run and bids submitted). That stream is mirrored three ways that are far easier to harvest than the portal itself — TED for above-threshold notices via its official API, OPSI/CKAN for bulk registers and the Statist award-statistics application covering 2013 onward, and ERAR for the actual money paid to named banks and vendors including below-threshold spend the notice stream never sees. Around them sit an audit layer (DKOM decisions, which expose contested and annulled banking tenders and their requirements) and a commercial monitoring layer (UL info tok, javnirazpisi.com) best used as coverage benchmarks and glossary sources rather than collection targets. The main risks for this cell are a live eForms/portal-redesign migration that will change the notice schema, an unmapped CPV code set for payments hardware and services, and the invisibility of evidenčna (below-threshold) and utilities-sector procurement — all of which push the design toward CPV-parameterised index queries plus ERAR triangulation rather than surface web search, which returned essentially no live Slovenian banking tenders in this pass.

The EU-level notice layer for Slovenia is effectively one authoritative source — TED / OJ S — surrounded by a ring of access channels and documentation rather than by competing publishers: the TED web search in both English and Slovenian, the anonymous-access TED Search API, daily and monthly bulk XML packages plus an FTP archive for backfill, a flattened CSV subset on the EU Open Data Portal, and the eForms SDK that makes the XML parseable. SIMAP no longer exists as a separate site; it survives as TED sub-pages carrying the CPV vocabulary with Slovenian labels and the threshold rules that define exactly which Slovenian contracts must appear here at all. The layer is high-credibility, openly licensed and machine-readable, and one confirmed Slovenian banking-services award (CPV 66110000, won by SKB d.d.) was observed, but it is structurally partial: it sees only above-threshold public buyers, so commercial banks and shared processors appear as winners rather than as buyers, and buyer-chosen CPV coding is loose enough that a four-code filter will silently under-recall. The practical build is a CPV-union plus bilingual-keyword query against the TED API, reconciled against enarocanje.si and the OPSI award-decision dataset, with the EU Funding & Tenders Portal monitored separately as a possible early-warning channel.

Donor and EU-fund financed procurement touching Slovenian banking and payments splits into two structurally different landscapes. Domestically, RRF (NOO) and cohesion money is announced through a federated set of Slovenian-language channels with Uradni list as the only universal publication of record, evropskasredstva.si and URSOO as the programme-level indexes, individual ministries and agencies (MDP, MGTS, SPIRIT, ARIS) as the actual issuers, and enarocanje.si plus the OPSI award dataset as the point where the money becomes a named contract with a named vendor; crucially, none of the calls found are sector-scoped to finance, so monitoring must key on beneficiary identity and CPV rather than the word 'banka'. The IFI leg is English-only and independent of the national portals: EBRD (notice board, ECEPP, and project summary documents - one of which is a real Bank of Slovenia advisory assignment), EIF (Slovenia-specific SEGIP calls plus a published list of selected Slovenian intermediaries, effectively an award record), and EIB (SID banka intermediated operations), with the World Bank sparse because Slovenia engages it mainly through fee-paid advisory rather than lending. The highest-value and most distinctive nodes for this cell are SID banka and its Sklad skladov vehicle, which sit at the junction of both landscapes and publish their own procurement and intermediary-selection results. Main open risks are the absence of a TED probe, no coverage yet of audit bodies, and a payments-technical Slovenian glossary that is not yet built.

The SI tender-aggregation landscape splits into three layers. The non-commercial layer is the strongest and should be the collection backbone: OPSI open data (published post-award contracts, e-JN active tenders, ERAR procurement collection), ERAR itself for realised payments to suppliers, TED for above-threshold notices with a free API and XML bulk back to 1993, and Opentender.eu/DIGIWHIST for normalised, entity-resolved SI analytics (currency unverified). The Slovenian commercial layer is a handful of small alert services - javnirazpisi.com, javni-razpisi.si, informator-javnihrazpisov.si, Forum Media - of which Informator is most interesting because it claims below-threshold and private-sector tenders, plus Bisnode/D&B GVIN which uniquely joins procurement records to company financials and ownership. The international reseller layer (TendersInfo, GlobalTenders, TenderImpulse, BidDetail, TendersTech, Bidovate, OpenOpps, Mercell) is mostly repackaged TED behind paywalls and is low priority except as triangulation; notably, no SI aggregator specialises in banking or payments, so sector targeting must be done by us through CPV filtering rather than bought off the shelf.

Slovene-language press is a genuinely load-bearing A2 layer for Slovenia: the state registers publish the award, but Finance.si, Delo/Svet kapitala, Dnevnik, Večer and the STA wire carry the contested detail — why a tender was annulled, who appealed to DKOM, and which vendor a private bank quietly replaced. Two structural features shape collection: the highest-value outlets (Finance.si, Večer, parts of Delo/Dnevnik) are paywalled, while the free layer (STA, RTVSLO, IT trade press Monitor and Računalniške novice, plus forum chatter on Slo-Tech) is where vendor names and system migrations actually appear. Regulator-side pointers are unusually strong here: DKOM publishes its own media-clippings index linking coverage to rulings, and IPRS/KPK document the ZDIJZ route journalists use to extract contract values. This run was hampered by a US-biased search index that returned legal texts rather than articles, so outlet-level candidates are well grounded but per-article evidence is thin and site-native search should drive the next pass.

Slovenia's weak-signal layer for banking/payments procurement is real but fragmented and largely invisible to general web search. The strongest channels are (a) job boards — MojeDelo, Optius, plus the legally mandatory ESS/Zavod feed for state-owned employers — and first-party bank careers pages, which expose implementation and migration programmes months ahead of any notice; and (b) a small set of recurring stages: DSI (Dnevi slovenske informatike, with a durable per-year programme archive), the ZBS Bančna konferenca with its Bančni vestnik journal, training calendar and public video recordings, and commercial conference organisers Finance/Delo. Vendor and infrastructure newsrooms — Bankart (national instant-payment scheme Flik, plus annual-report supplier disclosure) and Halcom — are where private-bank contracts surface post-award when the bank stays silent, and Slovenian trade and investigative media (Monitor, Računalniške novice, Necenzurirano) confirm migrations retrospectively, sometimes only through their failures. Two constraints shape collection: LinkedIn, the richest single channel, is auth-walled and ToS-restricted so needs analyst time rather than automation, and job-ad content decays fast, so snapshotting at capture is mandatory.

Slovenia's procurement-disclosure landscape rests on two statutes, both fully readable on PISRS: ZJN-3 (what must be published, where, and above which thresholds) and ZDIJZ (what contract content can be extracted afterwards, including the absolute-public-data rules for entities under dominant public-law influence). Notices of record live on the Uradni list-operated Portal javnih naročil (enarocanje.si, with an /arhiv/ path for superseded notices) and, above EU thresholds, are duplicated to TED in eForms with English summaries — TED is the more tractable archive for structured collection. Contract texts themselves have no national register: they surface case-by-case through Informacijski pooblaščenec appeal decisions, DKOM review decisions, Court of Audit reports, and Erar's transaction-level spending data, which together also expose the sub-threshold 'evidenčna naročila' layer that notices hide. The main open risks for later rounds are the unknown crawlability of the enarocanje.si and IPRS indexes, the missing CPV scope list for banking/payments, and the fact that the NGO lane (Danes je nov dan) appears to have handed its tooling to state operators.

Slovenia's procurement oversight layer is unusually well-consolidated and almost entirely open-web: DKOM (dkom.si) publishes every review decision back to the early 2000s with the contracting authority named in the URL and a dedicated full-text search engine, Računsko sodišče (rs-rs.si) publishes full audit reports indexed both chronologically and by auditee, KPK (kpk-rs.si) publishes case-level 'zaključne ugotovitve' plus the ERAR transaction database, and MJU/Direktorat za javno naročanje (ejn.gov.si, gov.si, mirrored on podatki.gov.si) publishes the statutory annual procurement statistics. The decisive limitation is retrieval rather than availability: none of the review or audit bodies offers a sector or CPV filter, and general web search does not reach case level, so isolating banking, card-processing and payment-system disputes requires full-corpus ingestion plus Slovenian-keyword classification, with sodnapraksa.si (UPRS judgments reviewing DKOM since 2021) as a valuable second narrative on the same disputes. ERAR is the strongest indirect confirmation source, showing what authorities actually paid banks and payment suppliers rather than what was awarded. Commercial aggregators (Tax-Fin-Lex, IUS-INFO) index this material usefully but are paywalled and should be used for discovery only, with authoritative copies pulled from the institutions themselves.

Slovenia's contract-and-payment transparency layer is unusually well developed and splits cleanly into three tiers: ERAR (KPK, successor to Supervizor, rebuilt 2023-24, CC BY 4.0, monthly) exposes actual public-sector transactions to named payees; the procurement tier (enarocanje.si contract publication, the OPSI datasets Evidenca JN / odločitve o oddaji / objavljene pogodbe, and the STATIST analytics app with CSV export covering all awards since 2013) carries notices, award decisions and signed contracts; and since 1 January 2023 the UJP JN Plačila system adds statutory monthly reporting of executed payments above EUR 40,000 back onto the contract record — a near-unique contract-to-cash trail. AJPES is not a contract register despite the seed hypothesis; it is the entity-resolution and financials layer (ePRS, JOLP, eRTR, RDL) that turns a payee string into an identified, sized company, and its RDL access appears to have been restricted since August 2025. The main obstacles are technical and legal rather than availability: ERAR is a client-rendered SPA with no documented API, the OPSI contracts record carries stale metadata and an undefined licence, and no banking/payments CPV filter has yet been built. Recommended next steps are probing ERAR's XHR endpoints, resolving the Slovenian OCDS endpoint, and testing the public-sector e-invoice dataset, which would be the finest-grained vendor spend signal in the cell.

Slovenia's state-linked financial buyers publish procurement in a strongly centralised way: SID banka and Kapitalska družba explicitly state that all javna naročila go to the Portal javnih naročil (enarocanje.si / Uradni list RS) with e-JN as the submission layer, so their own sites are pointers rather than primary sources. Genuine own-site value concentrates in ZPIZ (live aktualna javna naročila plus archive and small-value procurements), Pošta Slovenije (a ZJN-3 sectoral entity that also runs non-binding informative-offer calls — the earliest demand signal in this cell), ZZZS's provider portal (partner.zzzs.si, on a roughly three-year supplier re-tender cycle), and Banka Slovenije, which appears to self-host razpisna dokumentacija under a /JAVNA NAROČILA/ folder whose landing page I could not resolve. Two cell members need re-framing: Poštna banka Slovenije no longer exists as a buyer (merged into Nova KBM in 2016, now OTP banka) and FURS procures as a unit of the Ministry of Finance rather than in its own name. Realised-spend visibility is available through ERAR and its OPSI open-data mirror, but ERAR has documented under-reporting on ZZZS-linked flows and must be reconciled against award notices.

Slovenian local-government payments procurement is a two-layer landscape: a mandatory national spine (enarocanje.si for all notices under ZJN-3, ejn.gov.si for bid submission, TED for above-threshold contracts) sitting over ~212 municipal and public-institution websites that publish, in Slovene only, the below-threshold 'evidencna narocila' and the ZJN-3 annual awarded-contract lists where most POS, validator and parking-terminal purchases actually live. The highest-yield sources for this cell are indirect rather than direct: DKOM review decisions expose full technical specs, bidders and prices for contested municipal payment procurements (confirmed case 018-027/2024 on LPT's 'Nakup parkirnih sistemov'), while ERAR reveals actual supplier payments from every obcina and javno podjetje below any notice threshold. The concrete buyer set is small and identifiable - MOL/Javni holding Ljubljana with the Urbana card across transit, parking and libraries; Maribor/Marprom, which went open-loop on Visa/Mastercard and mobile wallets in December 2024; LPT's parkomat and urbanomat estate; plus UKC Ljubljana, UKC Maribor and the splosne bolnisnice. One structural caveat materially reshapes the 'municipal banking' half of this cell: account management for municipal budget users is centralised through UJP rather than tendered to commercial banks, so the tenderable opportunity concentrates in acquiring, ticketing and cash-handling rather than transaction-account services.

Slovenia's private banks publish almost nothing about their own procurement: no bank in the cell operates a Slovenian supplier portal, and the one named channel that exists — NLB's 'Javni razpisi' page — was verified to carry receivables and asset sales rather than supplier RFPs, with Delavska hranilnica's /doc/obvestila-javne-objave/ directory being the only comparable local slot found. The actionable sourcing intelligence therefore sits at parent-group level: Intesa Sanpaolo's Portale Fornitori with its Supplier Gate qualification, Erste Group Procurement for Banka Sparkasse, UniCredit Services, and (unmapped in this pass) OTP Budapest, Addiko Vienna and AIK Belgrade. Governance texts — NLB's sustainability policy supplier-evaluation framework and OTP Slovenija's compliance policy supplier due-diligence clause — are the only local documents that expose qualification criteria. Practical monitoring should combine parent-portal registration, targeted crawls of each bank's corporate site, an e-JN name filter for opportunistic hits, and an indirect vendor-press-release sweep, since no award-notice channel exists for this cell at all.

For Slovenia, the supplier-side award record is thin at the top and rich underneath: global hardware vendors (Diebold Nixdorf, NCR Atleos) publish nothing country-specific, while the strongest direct evidence comes from mid-tier vendors that market case studies — Printec (NLB contactless ATM programme), Temenos (NLB core banking incl. the N Banka/ex-Sberbank migration), ASEE/Asseco SEE and Halcom, whose Slovenian-language newsroom is the single best local-language feed in this cell. Where no vendor pressroom exists (Comtrade, Marand, Nexi Slovenija), the practical substitutes are customer-side artefacts: Bankart's annual reports naming its ATM manufacturer mix, banks' own splošni pogoji and e-banking manuals that name Halcom products outright, and app-store package/publisher metadata that attributes bank apps to Payten and Halcom. Bankart itself is the pivot actor — jointly owned by five banks and simultaneously a buyer, a supplier and a pointer to further Slovenian institutions. Highest-value next moves are direct crawls of vendor reference sections, bank-led rather than vendor-led searching, and adding hr/sr as a collection language.

Slovenia's payments procurement landscape is bifurcated: a well-structured public layer (Portal javnih naročil / e-JN / TED, plus Erar and OPSI for actual money flows) that captures state-linked buyers such as Banka Slovenije, SID banka and UJP, and an opaque private layer where the decisive actor — Bankart, the shared processor for most Slovenian banks — procures with no public tender feed at all. Because of that asymmetry, the highest-value tracking for this cell is indirect: Bankart press releases, Flik scheme and integration documentation, ZBS payment/card committee outputs and annual reports, bank ATM-network and merger announcements, issuer T&C version diffs, and payee-level searches in Erar keyed to Bankart's registration identifiers. Local-language terms are essential and partly counter-intuitive ('javno naročilo', 'naročnik/ponudnik', 'razpisna dokumentacija', 'bankomatska mreža', 'personalizacija kartic', 'takojšnja plačila'), while the bare word 'razpis' is heavily polluted by job and training notices and 'Bankart' collides with a medical term in English. Biggest open risks: no direct source yet for HSM/tokenisation/scheme-certification buying, and an unresolved legal question of whether Bankart or the commercial banks are sectoral contracting entities at all.

Slovenia's actor universe for banking and payments is unusually well indexed by official registers: Banka Slovenije runs a single searchable 'register subjektov nadzora' (~372 supervised entities) plus dedicated statutory registers for e-money institutions, EEA passporting firms, credit intermediaries and NPL servicers, and ATVP covers the securities side. On the demand side, Združenje bank Slovenije publishes an explicit member list of banks, savings banks and leasing companies, and the Nacionalni svet za plačila minutes name the umbrella organisations of the payment market. Supply-side (bidder) enumeration is weaker: GZS ZIT publishes only its board rather than a full roster, no national fintech association with a member directory was found, and the practical fallback is AJPES/OPSI business-register extraction by SKD activity code. For the A2 tender workstream the highest-value pointers are the OPSI open-data 'informativni seznam naročnikov' under ZJN-3 (the definitive buyer list) and the e-JN active-tenders dataset, which join cleanly to the regulator and association rosters.

Slovak public procurement of banking and payments services is anchored on one statutory spine: ÚVO's Vestník verejného obstarávania (mandatory under zák. 343/2015 Z.z.), whose notices are published daily as open XML datasets on data.slovensko.sk and mirrored above-threshold to TED — together these three give near-complete, machine-readable coverage of contract and award notices with CPV 66xxx, 30144000/30233300 and 72xxx. The procedure layer sits in IS EVO / Elektronická platforma (evo.isepvo.sk), which carries the súťažné podklady where POS terminal, ATM and card-processing specifications actually live, while below-threshold and low-value purchases run through EKS/elektronické trhovisko and the resulting signed contracts surface in the Centrálny register zmlúv. A useful secondary tier of aggregators — UVOstat, Slovensko.Digital's APIs, OpenTender/OCDS and commercial monitors such as verejnesutaze.sk — makes historical and cross-country analysis far cheaper, but every figure should be re-verified against the ÚVO primary notice. The main practical constraint found is discovery, not access: individual notices are effectively invisible to web search, so collection must be built on the portals' own CPV search, the Vestník XML feed and the TED API.

The EU publication layer for Slovak banking and payments procurement is anchored on TED, which since November 2022 publishes Slovak above-threshold notices in the eForms standard and exposes them through an unauthenticated Search API, bulk XML packages and the TED Open Data Service — making it the best-structured and most machine-tractable channel in this run, and clearly superior to the national bulletin for automated collection. Slovak notices reach TED via the ÚVO eForms information system acting as eSender, so TED and the ÚVO Vestník are two renderings of the same underlying filings, with TED carrying only the above-threshold subset and the national bulletin carrying the below-threshold remainder. The EU Funding & Tenders Portal adds a distinct, smaller stream of EU-institution contracts and programme calls where Slovak financial-sector actors appear as bidders or beneficiaries. The IFI channel is thinner than the cell rationale assumed: EBRD notices for Slovakia now flow exclusively through ECEPP and the Slovak examples found are energy and transport, while EIB's Slovak book is energy, urban and SME credit lines intermediated by Eximbanka — so IFI sources are worth low-frequency monitoring mainly as leading indicators of which institutions are scaling programmes, not as a procurement feed.

Slovakia's procurement remedy and audit trail is concentrated in four institutions with well-defined but PDF-heavy public registers: ÚVO (objections, review of contracting authority acts, administrative-offence fines, inspections, the transitional Rada ÚVO appeal body, and the statutory debarment register), NKÚ SR as supreme audit institution (audit protocols, summary reports, and a forward-looking list of ongoing audits), PMÚ SR for bid-rigging/cartel decisions plus its transparency document portal, and the courts via Slov-Lex, justice.gov.sk and the commercial aggregator Judikáty.info for administrative review of ÚVO and PMÚ decisions. These channels are the main way banking and payments contract details — bid values, disqualifications, cancelled and re-run tenders — become visible after the fact, since award notices routinely omit them. The binding constraint is not availability but retrievability: none of the registers offers sector or CPV filtering, decisions are published as PDFs on legacy CMS paths, and no confirmed API or bulk export was found, so collection must be full-corpus harvest with in-house keyword classification against a Slovak banking/payments glossary. Aggregators (Uvostat, Judikáty.info) and watchdog output (Transparency International Slovensko) are useful discovery and redundancy layers but must always be resolved back to the primary decision document before anything is asserted.

Slovakia has an unusually deep open-data and analytics layer over public procurement, in three tiers: (1) official machine-readable publication — the national catalogue at data.slovensko.sk (with a DCAT-AP-SK SPARQL endpoint) carrying ÚVO Vestník datasets, the legacy CKAN portal data.gov.sk, and the CRZ contract register with predictable daily XML change-batch exports since 2011; (2) civil-society re-publication — Ekosystém.Slovensko.Digital's weekly SQL dumps and rate-limited APIs over CRZ and the company registers, Red Flags for state IT project scoring, Transparency International Slovensko's tooling and methodologies, and Nadácia Zastavme korupciu's quantitative analyses of the post-2024 collapse in competitive tendering; and (3) standardised/international mirrors — the Slovak OCDS publication in the OCP Data Registry, Opentender.eu (GTI/DIGIWHIST, which adds below-threshold national data to TED), plus TED Open Data and the EU Public Procurement Data Space. A commercial analytics tier (uvostat.sk, transparex.sk with its T-index, transpera.sk, tendermonitor.sk, tenderradar.sk) adds supplier/buyer profiling and CPV-level market metrics that the official portals cannot answer, at the cost of paywalls and restrictive terms. The main weaknesses of the cell are that the flagship beneficial-ownership tool znasichdani.sk is defunct (DNS failure plus reporting of a 2018 shutdown), no first-party ÚVO API was confirmed, and nothing in the ecosystem is sector-specific — banking and payments relevance must be carved out ourselves via a CPV shortlist and a curated buyer/supplier list.

Slovakia's actor universe for banking and payments is unusually well served by statutory registers: NBS publishes both a sector-browsable supervised-entity database and a machine-readable daily PSD2 JSON feed, while the Ministry of Justice's RPVS makes registration legally compulsory for anyone contracting with the state, and ÚVO maintains a pre-qualified list of economic operators. Together these four give a near-complete, join-ready supplier and licence-holder list keyed on IČO, with ORSR/RPO and registeruz.sk supplying identity and scale, and EBA EUCLID closing the cross-border passporting gap. The association layer is fragmented — one banking association (SBA, ~23 members covering >99% of the sector), one leasing association (ALS, ~32 members), two competing fintech bodies (Slovenská FinTech asociácia and FINAS) and ITAS for IT vendors — so no single roster covers the vendor side and several must be merged. The main outstanding work is mechanical: fetch and parse the individual membership pages and confirm bulk-access routes for ORSR/RPO, since no dedicated Slovak cards/payments trade association appears to exist to shortcut that vendor mapping.

Slovak media coverage of banking/payments procurement splits into three usable layers: paywalled business journalism (Trend, HN, Denník E, Forbes SK) that names winners and controversies; open IT trade press (Živé.sk, ITnews.sk, TOUCHIT, plus the specialist FinReport.sk) that carries technical detail and early weak signals, often PR-sourced; and a professional/watchdog layer (Slovensko.Digital Red Flags and its forum, Transparency International SK, epravo.sk, overejnomobstaravani.sk and law-firm blogs) that translates notices into assessments and acts as the best pointer set into ÚVO/CRZ records. Red Flags and Živé.sk are the highest-value nodes; the paywall on the business titles is the main structural constraint. The clearest blind spot is private commercial-bank procurement, which no Slovak outlet covers systematically and which will need vendor- and report-based sourcing instead.

Slovakia has an unusually rich set of legally mandated pre-tender artefacts: MIRRI publishes project documentation and feasibility studies for public comment, MF SR's Útvar hodnoty za peniaze must appraise IT investments above roughly EUR 10m before procurement starts, and MetaIS registers every state information system and project. Together with the Plán obnovy (Component 17) and Program Slovensko call pipelines, these name planned payment and e-government systems months to years before any award notice. Weak signals on the commercial-bank side are thinner: bank vendor days are not published, so job boards (profesia.sk, bank career pages) and third-party conference agendas — ITAPA above all, plus FinNext and NBS events — are the practical substitutes for reading which vendors and which institutions are converging. The highest-value next step is a dedicated pass on 'prípravné trhové konzultácie' and on the payment-heavy state buyers (Štátna pokladnica, Sociálna poisťovňa, Slovenská pošta, DataCentrum), which this cell did not profile.

Slovakia's signed-contract layer is unusually rich: because a mandatorily-published contract only takes effect the day after publication, the Centrálny register zmlúv (crz.gov.sk, with an institution-indexed mirror at zmluvy.gov.sk) holds the actual banking and payment contracts of the state — counterparty, price and PDF — including direct awards with no tender notice. The practical collection spine is the daily XML change-batch bulk export catalogued on the national open-data portal (migrated from data.gov.sk to data.slovensko.sk), since there is no official CRZ query API; commercial aggregators (Slovensko.Digital Datahub, uvostat) and open-source scrapers exist as accelerators. Below CRZ sits the institution-level layer that CRZ contract metadata misses: NBS (the one entity with a standing publication exemption, so its own zmluvy/objednávky/faktúry pages are the only channel), Štátna pokladnica with its account and payment-gateway terms, Sociálna poisťovňa, Finančná správa, Slovenská pošta, ministries and municipalities such as Bratislava, plus SaaS platforms (eGovZmluvy, RZOF) that carry the long tail. The main analytic caveat is legal: §5a ods. 5 of Act 211/2000 lets payment-services and financial-instrument contracts be disclosed as a bare notice of conclusion, so a missing contract must be treated as censored data, not as absence of a deal.

Slovak banking/payments demand from state buyers is fragmented across three layers: each buyer's own procurement page (which alone carries plans and prípravné trhové konzultácie, the earliest signal — Štátna pokladnica's payment-systems infrastructure consultation is the clearest confirmed example), the statutory ÚVO buyer profile that is the legal record, and a buyer-specific e-procurement platform (ERANET for Slovenská pošta, JOSEPHINE and eZakazky for ŽSR, IS EVO for treasury and tax administration) that carries below-threshold and commercial tenders never reaching the bulletin. The highest-value targets are Štátna pokladnica, Slovenská pošta and ZSSK, whose cash, counter and ticketing estates generate concrete payment-terminal and acceptance demand. Aggregators uvostat.sk and verejnesutaze.sk provide buyer-keyed historical dossiers that are far easier to query than the ÚVO portal, but should be treated as secondary. The main structural risks are ÚVO URL-scheme instability, JS-rendered pages (mfsr.sk, ERANET) defeating static scraping, and an unresolved regulatory question over whether Dôvera and Union become obliged buyers.

Slovak municipal and regional payments procurement is legally centralised but practically fragmented: above-threshold notices land in the ÚVO vestník and TED, signed contracts land in the Centrálny register zmlúv (mandatory for samosprávy since March 2022), and commodity purchases run through the state EKS marketplace — but the fastest-moving layer, zákazky s nízkou hodnotou for ticketing, e-parking and cash handling, is scattered across each buyer's own úradná tabuľa and across at least four commercial e-procurement platforms (JOSEPHINE ~22%, eZakazky ~8%, ERANET ~8%, plus a long tail). The highest-value buyers for this cell are the municipal transport companies (DPB, DPMK, DPMP, all now deploying in-vehicle contactless validators) and city parking schemes (PAAS Bratislava, Žilina, Ružomberok, Humenné), each of which also leaves traces on vendor reference pages and specialist portals such as imhd.sk. A workable collection design is therefore three-tier: bulk ingest ÚVO/TED/CRZ as the authoritative spine, poll an enumerated watchlist of a few hundred municipal, borough, VÚC and mestský-podnik noticeboards for the sub-threshold layer, and use aggregators (verejnesutaze.sk, uvostat.sk) and vendor announcements purely as pointers to be resolved back to primary records.

Slovakia's four state-owned/controlled financial institutions are all subject to Act 343/2015 Z.z., so their procurement is unusually legible: each maintains a profil verejného obstarávateľa both on its own site and on the ÚVO portal (NBS = profile 8643, SZRB = 9586; Eximbanka and SIH IDs still unresolved), with notices duplicated into the daily Vestník and, above threshold, into TED. The richest and earliest signal sits with NBS, which publishes preliminary market consultations (PTK) months before tendering and runs all above-threshold procedures in IS JOSEPHINE — where confirmed tenders include banknote-processing equipment servicing and supervision/regulation case systems. A key structural asymmetry: NBS is exempt from the Centrálny register zmlúv and discloses contracts, orders and invoices only on nbs.sk, while SZRB, Eximbanka and SIH appear in CRZ, so award-side collection needs two distinct paths. SIH is a different animal — it procures financial intermediaries rather than technology — and aggregators (uvostat.sk, sk.openprocurements.com, verejnesutaze.sk) plus SITA Financie serve as convenience indexes and narrative context, never as primary evidence.

Slovakia's systemic banks are private and outside ÚVO, and this scouting run confirms the consequence empirically: of the nine target banks, only VÚB publishes a genuine Slovak-language procurement page (vub.sk/ludia/nakup-obstaravanie.html, bot-defended with HTTP 403 to plain fetch), and only one of five parent groups - Intesa Sanpaolo - exposes a publicly addressable supplier portal with open self-registration. Erste Group Procurement, RBI, KBC and UniCredit are structurally identifiable as the real sourcing decision points for SLSP, Tatra banka, ČSOB SK and UniCredit SK, but none advertises a reachable registration endpoint, so their tender flow is effectively invitation-only. The practical bypass therefore runs through three secondary channels: Slovak commercial e-sourcing platforms (JOSEPHINE/PROEBIZ, ERANET) which private buyers also use, public-sector proxies such as Slovenská pošta for the 365.bank ecosystem, and supplier-side records in ÚVO/uvostat, CRZ and RPVS. Local-language coverage was achieved in sk (plus de/it/en at group level) but is still missing in pl for mBank and cs for the ČSOB/KBC stack.

Slovakia's non-state procurement traffic concentrates on a small number of commercial e-sourcing platforms: JOSEPHINE (PROEBIZ), which offers the richest openly browsable cross-buyer tender index; ERANET (operated by innovis, s.r.o.), which hosts per-client instances for large semi-private buyers including Slovenská pošta, Slovenské elektrárne and Allianz but generally gates listings behind supplier registration; eZakazky (eBIZ) with a queryable published-tender search and per-buyer sub-portals; and TENDERnet, which captures the sub-threshold §117 'prieskum trhu' layer that never reaches the ÚVO Vestník. A second, cheaper route is the commercial monitoring layer — Transpera explicitly aggregates JOSEPHINE and ERANET alongside state sources, while TenderRadar, Tender Monitor, ADVAL, tender.sk and the free verejnazakazka.sk offer entity- or CPV-scoped alerting that would otherwise require many bespoke scrapers. The main blind spot is the private retail banks themselves: only VÚB exposed a procurement/supplier page (WAF-blocked to automated fetch, so unverified rather than dead) and NBS confirms it runs above-threshold tenders through JOSEPHINE, so SLSP, Tatra banka and ČSOB likely source via parent-group platforms that no Slovak portal indexes.

Slovak payments-infrastructure procurement is legible almost entirely through four statutory layers — the ÚVO Vestník (notices), TED (above-threshold, structured, EN), the Centrálny register zmlúv (signed contracts with commercial terms), and EKS (low-value e-marketplace) — plus three private e-procurement platforms (JOSEPHINE, ERANET, eZakazky) that host the technical specifications for terminals, kiosks and gateways. The distinctive buyer set for this cell is not banks: it is Slovenská pošta (self-service cash kiosks, eKolok, Pay by square), NBS (cash-processing and payment-system IT, tendered via intermediary NAR marketing), and transport operators DPB and ZSSK (card-accepting vending kiosks, with DPB signalling a doubling of card-enabled machines by end-2026). The supply side is concentrated in CIT/ATM-outsourcing incumbents operating under NBS cash-processing licences (Loomis, Monilogi, SBS-licensed operators such as ProtectUs), which makes award-notice supplier tracking in UVOstat/TED a workable market-share proxy. Two structural limits must be stated up front: commercial bank and acquirer spend on POS estates and card personalisation has no public tender channel at all, and general web search does not reach inside these portals — collection must be in-portal, CPV-driven, and validated against real notice text before the query vocabulary is trusted.

For Slovak banking and payments, the supplier side is the more talkative party: Asseco CE, PosAm, DATALAN, Alanata, SOFTEC, Printec, Euronet, Global Payments and Loomis all publish reference lists, case studies or service pages that reveal buyer relationships Slovak banks themselves keep quiet — a pattern explicitly documented in Slovak business press. Disclosure is split by tier: international vendors (Euronet, Worldline, Nexi, Diebold Nixdorf) announce deals in English on group IR/press pages with scope and duration, while Slovak integrators publish SK-language 'referencie' and 'prípadové štúdie' that often anonymise the bank as merely 'banka'. Practical collection therefore needs a browser-grade crawler (Asseco returned 403), group-level EN press polling plus SK media wires (SITA, TOUCHIT BrandIT) since local entities have no press feeds, and Wayback snapshots to catch references pruned when contracts lapse. The main residual weakness is verification: vendor claims are promotional and frequently buyer-anonymised, so this cell's output should be joined to award/tender records via each vendor's Slovak legal entity resolved through FinStat/ORSR.

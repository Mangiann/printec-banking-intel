Slovak public procurement is unusually well-instrumented for source scouting: a statutory journal (ÚVO vestník) plus the IS EPVO/EVO tendering system carry above- and standard-threshold notices, EKS carries below-threshold marketplace buys with unit prices, and the Central Register of Contracts (CRZ) publishes the actual contract text because publication is a condition of legal effect. Together these give named vendors, values, terms and renewal dates for ATM, payment-terminal, cash-handling and IT-security lots at state bodies, Slovenská pošta and municipalities, with RPVS resolving the beneficial ownership behind each winning entity. The practical constraint is discovery mechanics, not access: notice bodies are poorly indexed by web search, so collection must run in-portal by CPV plus Slovak keywords, and must not rely on TED, which only mirrors above-EU-threshold awards. Third-party layers (UVOstat, verejnesutaze.sk, Transpera, Transparency International Slovensko) are best used as tripwires and entity-discovery aids, with every figure re-verified against ÚVO/CRZ primaries.

Slovakia's contract-transparency layer is unusually rich for this cell: because a mandatorily-published contract only becomes effective on publication, the Centrálny register zmlúv (crz.gov.sk, ~5.8M contracts, run by Úrad vlády SR) holds full signed PDFs — vendor legal entities, prices, annexes — for ministries, state agencies and state-owned companies including Slovenská pošta, SZRB and Eximbanka, and it is searchable by supplier, IČO, subject and price band. A second, institution-level tier sits beside it: NBS publishes its own contract/order/invoice register outside CRZ, while VÚC and cities publish via their own portals or platforms such as eGovZmluvy, zverejnovanie.bratislava.sk and Košice's line-item-searchable system, and third parties (uvostat with a paid API, FinStat, TIS) provide better query surfaces and entity resolution over the same corpus. The main constraints are structural rather than access-related: there is no official REST API (bulk XML dumps plus selective PDF fetching instead), bulk dumps exclude the documents where prices and SLAs live, Štátna pokladnica's financial and payment-services contracts are statutorily exempt from publication, and the term 'bankomat' is dominated by ATM-siting leases rather than servicing contracts. Next priority is a vendor-side sweep by supplier IČO plus enumeration of the remaining VÚC, cities and payments-relevant state buyers such as Finančná správa.

Slovakia's statutory disclosure stack is unusually strong for vendor sizing: ORSR gives the legal entity and its statutory bodies, RÚZ (registeruz.sk) publishes full financial statements for every accounting unit from 2013 with a documented Open API, and RPVS discloses ultimate beneficial owners plus narrative verification documents for anyone taking public money — together enough to size local players, unmask foreign-vendor subsidiaries and confirm which entity signs SK contracts. Risk and change signals come from Register úpadcov and the daily Obchodný vestník, while RPO (Statistical Office) supplies the canonical IČO/SK NACE backbone that joins everything. The main constraints are that no register is sector-filterable (a vendor watchlist must be built elsewhere first), that free bulk UBO data exists only for public-sector partners, and that the convenient normalised views (FinStat, Foaf.sk, Slovensko.Digital) are commercial and should be licensed rather than scraped. All entries are pointer/verification-grade; none of them will yield case-study or partnership narrative beyond incidental detail in verifikačné dokumenty and poznámky.

Slovakia's association layer is unusually well-suited to actor enumeration: SBA covers ~24 banks and ~99% of the sector with 14 expert commissions plus a public projects-and-standards section, while ITAS (100+ ICT members, filterable by systems integration / cybersecurity / vendor-distributor) and SAPIE (~200 innovation-economy members) between them enumerate most of the vendor universe selling into SK finance. Adjacent collective bodies extend the map into non-bank buyers — ALS SR (~32 leasing companies), SLASPO (insurers plus associated service-provider tiers), SASS, FINAS, and the two credit bureaux SBCB/SRBI (~17 bank members, CRIF-backed) and NBCB/NRKI (~32 non-bank members) — with the chambers (AmCham, Britcham, AHK Slowakei) and Digitálna koalícia adding sponsor- and commitment-level signals of who partners with whom. Most member rosters are published as open HTML and are diffable quarterly; the main gaps are that sponsor lists live in images and PDFs, a few bodies (Slovenská FinTech asociácia, AHK) publish no full roster and must be reconstructed via the state register of interest associations or FinStat, and credit-bureau register content is legally off-limits even though the corporate pages are not. Note also that AmCham Slovakia has no financial-services committee (the Digital and Tax committees are the relevant substitutes), and 'SIBA'/'SAPI' are false-friend acronyms in this market.

Slovakia has no standalone banking-technology trade fair, so the paid-declaration signal this cell targets is distributed across four layers: a general IT congress with a partner logo wall and its own project award (ITAPA / Cena ITAPA), one genuinely on-target fintech-banking conference with tiered commercial sponsors and an expo tier (Finweek Bratislava, 10th edition Oct 2026, with Visa, Thunes, CRIF and NBS/MF SR patronage), an association-run seminar circuit for cards and payments (ZBK, with SBA and NBS alongside), and a cluster of annual awards whose shortlists name specific bank products — Zlatá minca (nominations published, not just winners, since 2005), the scheme-run Mastercard Awards Slovensko carried by Forbes SK, TREND Banka roka and Finparáda. The highest-value and least-exploited item found is the narrowly targeted 'IT vo finančníctve' conference for banks and insurers, whose organiser domain remains unresolved and should be the first action on the next pass. Because sponsor walls are overwritten each edition, the practical risk to this cell is temporal rather than access-related: Wayback snapshots of partner and nomination pages are needed to turn one-off rosters into the multi-year competitive-timing series that makes the signal worth anything.

Slovakia's banking/IT trade-media landscape splits into three tiers: paywalled quality business press (Trend, HNonline/Finweb, Dennik E, SME, Forbes Slovensko) that carries the investigative tender, migration and executive-move reporting but resists automated collection; open specialist finance portals (Finsider, FinReport, oPeniazoch.sk, Peniaze.sk, Banky.sk) that reliably capture operational change — branch and ATM/vkladomat network shifts, cashless branches, sector AI/digitalisation round-ups — and are the most collectable high-yield layer; and IT/tech media (Zive.sk, TOUCHIT, ITnews.sk, TechByte, FinTechHub) plus English trade press (Finextra, FinTech Futures' dedicated Slovakia region page, The Paypers) for product launches and vendor-named announcements. The single most valuable non-obvious finding is that Slovak banks systematically do not name their IT suppliers, so the integrators do it for them: vendor 'Napisali o nas' press-clipping and reference pages (Asseco CE, Soitron, PosAm, EEA) republish paywalled trade-press articles and case studies and are the practical workaround for both the paywall and the attribution gap. SITA's per-actor tag pages and PRservis wire sit upstream of most SK media and should anchor the collection design; commercial bank procurement is private, so tender-dispute signal exists only for state-adjacent institutions and must be sourced from the register cells instead.

Slovakia's recruitment-signal layer is highly concentrated: Profesia.sk is the dominant channel and, critically, exposes a full-text URL parameter (search_anywhere=) that turns it into a queryable corpus of ad bodies, while the state's legally-mandated vacancy register at sluzbyzamestnanosti.gov.sk offers an official, less commercially filtered cross-check. The highest-signal source per unit of effort is the contractor marketplace join.titans.eu, whose listings are project-shaped and disclose sector, platform and contract duration - a direct read on where a bank programme sits in its lifecycle - complemented by first-party career portals of the four large banks (Tatra banka, SLSP, VUB, CSOB) and nearshore integrator hubs. Two structural constraints shape any collector: vendor brand names return zero hits on demand (live checks for 'Temenos' and 'Flexcube' both came back empty, while Slovak functional phrases like 'platobne systemy' returned named employers such as Tatra banka and the processor DanubePay), so collection must be a standing longitudinal alert on Slovak-language functional terms rather than one-shot brand queries; and LinkedIn - the only source supporting title mining on people rather than ads - is login-gated and ToS-restricted, so it belongs to a licensed or manual analyst workflow, never a scraper. The main open gaps are the unmapped SK integrator layer, the absence of ad back-history, and unreviewed per-domain robots.txt/ToS.

Slovakia's non-bank cash and self-service buyer layer is documented across four distinct source families: operator/vendor sites (Euronet SK, Loomis SK, G4S/Securitas SK), retailer service and newsroom pages (COOP Jednota's miniBANKOMAT/cashback network of ~2,000 stores, Lidl SK press releases on self-checkout), public-sector procurement records for transport and postal buyers (DPMK Košice's own verejné obstarávanie and contract register, DPB news, ÚVO's Vestník), and statistical/regulatory anchors at NBS and the SBA. The sharpest asymmetry is disclosure regime: transport operators and Slovenská pošta leave a full tender trail naming suppliers and values, while private retail chains publish deployment counts but never vendors — so retail self-checkout and terminal suppliers will have to be inferred from trade press, job ads and integrator references. Community documentation (imhd.sk) is unusually valuable for fare-technology history in Bratislava and Košice and should be treated as a lead generator to be corroborated, not as a primary. Key open items are the SK-specific size of Euronet's ATM estate, the supplier chain behind Bratislava's open-loop 'Tapni sa' ticketing, and the entirely unscouted petrol-network segment.

The EU-level procurement and funding trail for SK banking/payments rests on three stacked layers. The mandatory-disclosure layer is strongest: TED plus its no-auth Search API and bulk XML packages, mirrored nationally by the ÚVO Vestník in Slovak, which together give CPV-coded, vendor-named award data including below-threshold contracts. The EU-funds layer (planobnovy.sk, MIRRI, ITMS2014+/ITMS21+, eurofondy.gov.sk, cohesiondata and data.gov.sk) gives the money-side context and beneficiary names for digitalisation projects, while third-party aggregators (Opentender.eu, OCP registry) offer a cleaned shortcut worth validating against originals. The IFI layer (EIB project register, EIF Slovakia guarantee disclosures, EBRD country pages and ECEPP) names intermediary banks and some suppliers but is English-only and partly registration-gated, and commercial SK banks remain structurally invisible to this trail — that blind spot must be covered by vendor-side sources.

Slovakia's regulator-side source landscape is unusually strong on actor identity and weak on vendor identity. NBS publishes a statutory PSD2 register backed by a documented, daily-refreshed JSON API (subjekty.nbs.sk/api/psd2) plus a nine-sector supervised-entity database with a bulk export, which together give a near-complete, machine-readable SK payments and banking competitor universe including the new MiCA CASP layer. Decision publications (výroky právoplatných rozhodnutí, the decisions archive and Vestník NBS), the supervision activity reports and the innovation-hub/sandbox reports add licensing, ownership-change, enforcement and fintech-pipeline signal, while the oversight section (PFMI, ECB SIPS Regulation, PISA, CROE) and the 2025 opening of SIPS/TARGET2-SK to non-bank PSPs flag a live structural shift in SK clearing. The critical limitation for A6 is that vendor relationships stay behind the supervisory wall: DORA registers of information and the outsourcing/ICT annexes to licence applications are mandated and detailed but never published, so specific technology suppliers must be reconstructed from procurement and vendor-side sources in other cells.

Slovak systemic banks are wholly foreign-owned and privately procured, so their disclosure landscape splits cleanly in two: locally-hosted mandatory documents (výročné správy, ročné finančné správy and Pillar 3 'zverejňovanie informácií' under NBS Opatrenie č. 16/2014) on each bank's own site, and richer strategic detail in the parent groups' filings (Erste, RBI, KBC, Intesa Sanpaolo). Statutory registers — registeruz.sk, ORSR and the NBS CERI issuer repository, the last carrying structured ESEF filings — form the compliant fallback for entities such as UniCredit SK and mBank SK that publish nothing locally, and they also expose captive IT arms like Erste Digital's Slovak branch. The key finding of this pass is that the bank disclosures themselves reliably show that transformation is happening and what it costs, but rarely name suppliers; the two concrete vendor attributions obtained (Temenos payment hub and PosAm end-user-services outsourcing, both at Tatra banka) came from vendor and integrator reference libraries instead. Collection should therefore pair regulatory disclosure with systematic crawling of Slovak integrator reference pages, and must extend beyond sk/en into cs and pl to reach the branch-form banks.

Slovak bank, insurer and state-institution newsrooms are open, well-structured and free of paywalls, making this one of the cheapest high-yield cells: every large bank (Tatra banka, Slovenská sporiteľňa, VÚB, ČSOB, Poštová banka/365.bank, J&T, Fio) runs a Slovak-language 'tlačové správy' or 'aktuality' listing, and the largest insurer (Allianz-SP) runs an equivalent 'tlačové centrum' documenting digital go-lives in detail. Vendor names do surface voluntarily in these releases — Tatra banka's kids-app release credits GoodRequest and ISW by name, and BLIK's own newsroom mirrors its Tatra banka/VIAMO partnership — so the highest-value collection pattern is pairing each bank release with the named supplier's own press or case-study page (Soitron's Tatra banka case study is the template). Three aggregation layers make the cell durable: SBA for cross-bank infrastructure programmes, tlacovespravy.sme.sk and omediach.com as verbatim press-release archives that outlive pruned bank pages, and SITA/FinReport/TOUCHIT as trade coverage that catches ATM-fleet and channel changes banks understate. The main weakness is terminological rather than structural: queries for biometric onboarding returned nothing, so the Slovak glossary must be extended (overenie totožnosti na diaľku, video overenie, digitálna peňaženka, obnova bankomatovej siete) before any conclusion of absence, and newsroom paths for Prima banka, UniCredit SK, mBank and several insurers still need to be resolved from sitemaps.

For Slovakia, international banking-tech and payments vendors publish very little country-specific evidence: their case-study hubs (Diebold Nixdorf, Auriga, NCR Atleos, Comarch, Backbase, Worldline/Ingenico, IDEMIA) are worth monitoring as pointer surfaces but yielded almost no SK-named story in this pass, and none of them maintain Slovak-language content. The genuinely SK-specific vendor evidence sits with Slovak-domiciled or Slovak-operating vendors that publish local references — above all Asseco Central Europe's sk-language 'Referencie' and press sections, and Printec Slovakia's country page — plus partner locators (Verifone's, which appears to list Printec Slovakia) that expose the local integrator layer no register captures. The strongest find of this cell is bank-side rather than vendor-side: Slovenská sporiteľňa's GDPR processor-list PDF names actual suppliers including Diebold Nixdorf s.r.o., a pattern that should be replicated across every SK bank and paired with PMÚ/EU merger decisions and public procurement records. Priority next steps are querying the vendor partner locators with a Slovakia filter and harvesting each SK bank's 'zoznam sprostredkovateľov' disclosure.

The Slovak bank IT supply chain is documented mainly through vendor-published Slovak-language 'referencie' pages, which are open, low-friction and frequently name bank clients outright — Asseco Central Europe, TEMPEST, ANASOFT/FINUS, SOFTEC, Millennium (Soitron Group), LYNX, EEA and Centire all expose usable lists, with EEA offering a segment-filtered URL. These marketing claims should be triangulated against statutory and derived data: registeruz.sk annual reports, FinStat/FinReg company financials, and uvostat's consortium analytics over public procurement, which expose prime/subcontractor relationships that no reference page admits. Two structural blind spots remain: compliance-component suppliers such as Ditec publish products but never clients, and bank-owned hubs like Erste Digital (2,000+ IT staff in Bratislava and Košice) insource work invisibly behind a LinkedIn login wall, so hiring data and conference programmes are needed as proxies. Gratex, DATALAN, Slovanet and Trask are currently under-evidenced in the banking context and should be treated as unconfirmed rather than dropped.

The SK payments landscape has an unusually strong regulatory spine: NBS's PSD2 register (with a daily-updated JSON API and a signed variant), the SIPS participant list and NBS payment statistics together give a machine-readable, authoritative frame of every licensed SK PSP, e-money institution and clearing participant, complemented by the Slovak Banking Association's monthly card issuing/acquiring statistics and its ownership of the PAY by square QR standard. The commercial layer splits into international acquirers with SK-localised estates (Global Payments s.r.o., distributed partly through Slovenská sporiteľňa; Worldline with sk-SK merchant terminal and FI acquiring/ATM lines), domestic licensed PSPs (Besteron/Seyfor, TrustPay, 24-pay, SKPAY) and CZ/HU entrants (ComGate, GoPay, Barion), with bank-owned gateway brands (CardPay, eCard) forming a distinct fourth layer that is currently visible mainly through third-party comparisons. Indirect and financial sources are strong — registeruz.sk and FinStat expose local revenue for each SK-registered payments entity, and e-commerce platform integration lists plus SAEC/MNFORCE payment-method studies reveal the gateway estate from the merchant side. The main gaps are terminal-OEM channel visibility (no SK Ingenico/PAX/Verifone distributor surfaced), the absence of any public-procurement source, unconfirmed Nexi presence in SK, and vendor case studies that will need direct site crawling rather than search.

The SK fintech/paytech entrant layer is unusually pointer-rich but fragmented: two rival associations (FINAS at finas.sk claiming 70+ members incl. VISA and 365.bank, and the older Slovenská FinTech asociácia at slovakfintech.sk, IČO 51910497) each publish a member roster, and SAPIE plus innovationmapslovakia.sk cover the surrounding accelerator/VC layer, so four directories between them expose most of the cohort. On the official side NBS supplies the innovation hub, the regulatory sandbox, the PI/AISP licence listing and the subjekty.nbs.sk register, with MF SR owning national fintech strategy; the hub and sandbox document the pipeline but do not appear to name participants, so early-entrant detection has to lean on the NBS activity reports plus Slovak trade press (Forbes.sk, FinReport.sk, Startitup.sk), which reliably named recent paytech rounds such as Veslo (EUR 2m with SIH) and Tapaya (EUR 1.2m, Passion Capital + DEPO Ventures). Commercial startup databases (Tracxn's 222-company SK fintech slice, Crunchbase SK hubs) give the broadest name lists but are paywalled and should be licensed rather than scraped, with FinStat/Transparex used to resolve every discovered name to a legal entity. The clearest structural gap is inbound EU passporting: only Revolut was evidenced with SK-specific sources, and no NBS list of cross-border-passported payment or e-money institutions was found, so EBA and home-regulator registers must be scouted next.

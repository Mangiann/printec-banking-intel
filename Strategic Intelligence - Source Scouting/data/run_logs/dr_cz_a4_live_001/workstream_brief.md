The Czech National Bank is an unusually dense and well-structured single source for this cell: it is simultaneously the statistical compiler (ARAD time series plus the semi-annual payment-statistics release covering cards, ATMs and POS acceptance devices), the operator of the only CZK interbank system (CERTIS, with monthly volumes and instant-payment data), the sole currency issuer (peněžní oběh / oběživo statistics), and the licensing authority whose JERRS register maps every payment institution. Machine access is realistic but bespoke — ARAD offers a documented REST API rather than SDMX, and the JERRS web services provide structured register snapshots; the published open-data catalogue covers registers, not statistical series. The two hard limits are granularity (national aggregates only; institution-level payment and terminal reporting sits inside the credentialed SDAT platform) and comparability (a 2022 methodology break under ECB Reg. (EU) 2020/2011 and Decree 401/2021 Sb.). All URLs here are unverified by this agent and several are deep PDF gallery paths that should be re-resolved from their index pages rather than treated as dead on a single failed fetch.

For Czechia, the supranational statistical layer is dominated by the ECB: the ECB Data Portal's PAY dataset (post-2022, Regulation (EU) 2020/2011) plus the discontinued PSS dataset give the only harmonised, machine-readable, long-run CZ series on cards, terminals, ATMs and payment volumes, accessible via SDMX API and accompanied by semi-annual press releases and an EBA/ECB payment-fraud report with CZ country breakdowns. Normalised access metrics come from the IMF Financial Access Survey (ATMs and branches per 100,000 adults), re-published through World Bank WDI, DBnomics and FRED, while demand-side behaviour comes from the World Bank Global Findex 2025 and, for structural/regulatory context, the World Bank GPSS and the EBA payment-institution and credit-institution registers. Two coverage holes matter strategically: Czechia is outside both the BIS CPMI Red Book jurisdictions and the ECB SPACE cash-attitudes survey, so BIS and SPACE serve only as comparator and methodological benchmarks, and no supranational source measures CZ point-of-sale cash shares. The layer is effectively English-only — Czech-language discovery returns ČNB and ČSÚ bridge documents (notably the ČNB commentary on payment-statistics reporting), which are nonetheless essential for interpreting the 2022 definitional break in the ECB CZ series.

The Czech cash ecosystem splits cleanly into three source layers. Official statistics (ČNB circulation data, payment-system commentary, and the Act 136/2011 register of licensed banknote processors) are authoritative and machine-collectable but deliberately exclude independent ATM deployers, leaving a structural blind spot on the non-bank ATM estate. That gap is filled only by commercial disclosure — Euronet's Czech fee/network pages and its parent's SEC filings, plus Sbírka listin accounts for Loomis, Brink's and G4S — and by the industry association SBK, which remains the sole public quantifier of cash-back at POS. The third layer is politics and media: the recurring constitutional right-to-cash bills, iROZHLAS/Peníze.cz branch-and-cash-desk trackers, and Česká pošta's card-withdrawal rollout are where municipality-level cash-access evidence actually gets published. Priority next steps are enumerating the full processor register and hunting a municipality-level ATM/branch accessibility dataset.

The Czech acceptance-side landscape has three usable layers: trade associations (APEK, with its E-commerce Study and the newer data.apek.cz portal, plus SOCR ČR for merchant card-cost advocacy), platform/marketplace operators (Shoptet's ceska-ecommerce.cz real-time portal and Heureka Group's quarterly eCommerce Insider, which is the only genuine recurring time series found), and payment service providers themselves (Comgate, GoPay, ThePay, PayU CZ, Shoptet Pay, Besteron, plus BNPL players Twisto and Skip Pay). Almost none of the PSPs publish structured statistics; their method-mix numbers — Comgate's ~20% QR and ~20% bank-transfer shares, ThePay's volume disclosures — appear only inside blog posts, press clippings and journalist interviews, so the series must be assembled datapoint-by-datapoint with heavy provenance tagging. Czech-language search was essential and materially outperformed English here: 'platební brána', 'platební metody', 'dobírka' and 'srovnání platebních bran' surfaced actors and figures that English queries returned only as vendor country-profile estimates (PPRO, NORBr). The main open risks are the membership gate on APEK's full study, unverified access terms on the two portals, and an unscouted acquirer/A2A tier (Global Payments/KB SmartPay, Worldline, Air Bank 'Cvak') that Comgate's own numbers suggest is already significant.

The third-party analyst layer for CZ payments/banking is highly asymmetric: Deloitte CZ dominates the Big Four presence with recurring Czech-language press releases and the Digital Banking Maturity and CEE Banking M&A studies, KPMG CZ maintains a lighter publication series, while PwC CZ and EY CZ produced no locatable current banking/payments output in Czech-language search. The most reliable recurring numbers come from bank research houses — Raiffeisen Research's annual CEE Banking Sector Report and Erste Group Research's Czech country and CEE financials desks — whose free press releases carry the CZ headline figures even when full reports are client-restricted. Consumer-side payment behaviour is served by Ipsos CZ (financial-literacy index with the Czech Banking Association), STEM/MARK, the Index prosperity Česka payment-behaviour chapter and NIQ/GfK syndicated retail studies, though the same handful of survey figures recirculate through Czech business media under inconsistent attribution and must be traced back to their originating study. The commercial market-sizing layer (GlobalData/Research and Markets, Mordor Intelligence, MarketDataForecast, Statista) is English-only and paywalled but publishes unusually detailed free abstracts — notably Mordor's CZ POS terminals and e-commerce payment-mix pages — which are the practical free route to card, POS and forecast figures, always requiring triangulation against ČNB data.

Czech trade and business press is a genuinely productive layer for CZ payments intelligence: E15, Měšec.cz, Peníze.cz, Aktuálně.cz and Banky.cz publish dated, quotable national figures on card counts, contactless share, ATM fleets and terminal acceptance, almost always sourced from SBK and ČNB, while HN, Ekonom and the specialist magazine Bankovnictví supply the executive interviews and deal reporting where competitive and fleet detail actually surfaces. Retail trade titles (Zboží&Prodej, Retail News) add the merchant-side POS view that bank-centric outlets miss, and Lupa.cz / CzechCrunch cover fintech, acquiring economics and merchant fees. The binding constraint is access, not existence: the most numerically valuable titles are subscription-walled, so open crawling yields headlines and secondary restatements while per-operator ATM/POS fleet shares stay out of reach without a licensed press archive. English-language discovery returns no CZ press at all — only global report vendors — confirming that Czech-language querying is mandatory for this cell.

The Czech weak-signal landscape for payments is anchored by two durable institutional hubs: Sdružení pro bankovní karty (SBK), which runs cardforum/cardsession and publishes cardmag and whose member list is the best available actor map of the CZ card industry, and the Česká fintech asociace with ~80 members and regular networking events. Award programmes are unusually informative here — Mastercard Banka roku (24th edition, Nov 2025) and Zlatá koruna both run payment-card, mobile-app and Fintech-of-the-Year categories whose winners track platform and vendor position year on year. Conference agendas are the strongest forward-looking channel: 'Trendy v platebním styku ČR & SR 2026' foregrounds PSD3/PSR, tokenisation, the EU identity wallet and AI-agent payments, while Retail Summit (Mar 2026, 1300+ attendees) covers the merchant acceptance side; scheme newsrooms localised to cs (Mastercard's ČSOB AI-payment first, Apr 2026) date pilots months ahead of statistics. Hiring signals are real but fragmented — employer ATS portals (KB, ČSOB, Global Payments Europe, Worldline) are usable, whereas LinkedIn and the large aggregators are login-walled or ToS-restricted and need manual monitoring.

The CZ archival/aggregation/translation layer is unusually strong on the corporate-filings side and unusually weak on the preservation side. ARES (ares.gov.cz), the public registers (or.justice.cz) and their bulk open-data exports (dataor.justice.cz, data.gov.cz) together give a free, authoritative route to sizing private payment, ATM and POS actors that publish nothing themselves, with commercial layers (Merk/Imper, Hlídač státu) removing the OCR burden at a licensing cost. Preservation, by contrast, rests mainly on the Internet Archive plus ČNB's own 'archiv' sections: the national Webarchiv exists and crawls the .cz domain, but most of it is not remotely accessible and no finance-themed harvest was found, so retired payment-statistics series should be actively pushed to Wayback rather than assumed archived. The English translation layer (Expats.cz, ČTK English, Radio Prague International, regional trade press) is real but opportunistic — good for surfacing and cross-checking headline figures, never a substitute for the Czech-language primary and registry sources.

Czechia's non-central-bank statistical infrastructure is unusually well developed and largely machine-readable: ČSÚ (csu.gov.cz, migrated from czso.cz) publishes retail turnover, household budget (SRÚ/SILC) and Eurostat-harmonised ICT surveys covering internet banking and online purchasing, exposed through the VDB database, a DataStat JSON-STAT API and an LKOD open-data catalogue. Discovery is best driven top-down from the National Open Data Catalogue at data.gov.cz (DCAT-AP with SPARQL/TPF/GraphQL endpoints) and from MF ČR's own portal data.mf.gov.cz (1,100+ datasets, EET topic area), with the annual Zpráva o vývoji finančního trhu and the quinquennial financial-literacy survey supplying cash-holding and behavioural context. The critical live policy driver in this cell is EET 2.0 at Finanční správa, whose POS/cash-register technical guidelines and 2026 timeline directly shape terminal demand. The principal limitation is that payment-instrument counts (cards, ATMs) and point-of-sale tender mix are not produced here at all and must come from the central-bank and industry-association cells.

The Czech trade-body layer is unusually productive for payments intelligence: Sdružení pro bankovní karty (SBK) publishes quarterly and annual card statistics back to 2000 — merchant transactions, ATM withdrawals, cashback, e-commerce volumes and cards in issue, aggregated directly from issuers and schemes — making it the single highest-value source in this cell and the only association-level route to ATM/POS estate figures in CZ. Alongside it, ČBA supplies the banking actor map (~31-35 members, ~99% of the sector) plus a genuine data portal at cbamonitor.cz, while ČLFA, AKAT and AČSS each own their sub-sector's aggregate statistics (non-bank credit and factoring, assets under management, building savings) with member directories that collectively enumerate nearly every regulated financial actor in the country; the Czech Fintech Association's 80+ member grid adds the fintech, PSP and terminal-vendor tail, and APEK contributes the e-commerce payment-mix and cash-on-delivery series. The main practical obstacles are structural rather than legal: ČBA has no coherent publications index (its survey series is only reachable by search), the fintech member list is JavaScript-gated, APEK's flagship study is paywalled, and several association series are re-publications of Ministry of Finance or ČNB data that must be provenance-tagged rather than counted as independent corroboration. Everything worth collecting here is Czech-language, so an English-only collector would capture almost none of it.

For the Czech Republic the international card schemes split cleanly into two source layers: a compelled-disclosure layer and a marketing layer. The compelled layer is the most valuable and most stable — Regulation (EU) 2015/751 forces Visa and Mastercard to publish dated Czech domestic multilateral interchange rate cards (visa.co.uk CZ PDFs; mastercard.com 11Czech.pdf), and both schemes publish Europe-Region rulebooks governing CZ acquiring; all of it is English-only. The marketing layer is where CZ-specific volumes actually appear — Mastercard's Czech Tiskové centrum and visa.cz's newsroom carry contactless share, tokenisation share, mobile-payment penetration and terminal-acceptance milestones that never enter CNB aggregates, but they are episodic, unversioned, quietly pruned, and frequently sourced upstream from the Czech Bank Card Association rather than the scheme itself. Amex and Diners are effectively residual in CZ (Diners issuing ended August 2022; Amex acceptance passed to Global Payments Europe in 2013), while UnionPay is the surprise quantitative outlier, publishing hard CZ merchant and ATM acceptance counts. Scheme fees, as opposed to interchange, are disclosed by nobody in the scheme layer and must be triangulated from CZ acquirer rate cards such as Worldline's.

Czech payment-infrastructure intelligence is unusually well centralised: the ČNB operates CERTIS itself and publishes participant registers (including a separate instant-payments participant list), rules, bank code lists and harmonised payment statistics, supplemented by frequent press releases carrying instant-payment adoption milestones. Standards live outside the central bank — QR Platba/SPAYD is owned by the Czech Banking Association (Standard ČBA no. 26) with per-bank implementation specs, while bank-based national e-ID adoption is published by Bank iD and corroborated by the state NIA/eIdentita node and DIA/MV releases. Card, ATM and POS series come from the industry association SBK (free quarterly PDF/XLS back to 2000), with processing concentrated in Global Payments Europe (ex-MUZO) and Worldline/Cataps, whose figures must be inferred from corporate news and company filings. The main blind spots are QR-payment usage volumes, ATM-network operators and cash-cycle data, and the near-total absence of English-language versions of the domestic rail documentation.

CZ systemic-bank operational metrics live almost entirely in voluntary IR and annual reporting, and the landscape splits cleanly in two: quarterly, English-friendly disclosure from the listed/parent-backed actors (KB, MONETA on the PSE; Erste and KBC group reports carrying Czech-segment breakouts), versus annual, Czech-only PDF reporting from the unlisted ones (Air Bank/PPF, Fio, Raiffeisenbank CZ, UniCredit CZ). Card issuance counts, ATM/deposit-ATM fleets, branch networks and mobile-banking user counts are all obtainable — Česká spořitelna's annual report and press releases are the single richest file for cards and ATMs, while the four-bank shared-ATM alliance (KB/MONETA/Air Bank/UniCredit) press stream is an unusually good source of hard CZ cash-usage numbers. The ČNB registry of banks' mandatory-disclosure URLs is the right pointer to keep this cell's seed list self-maintaining. The clearest hole is POS acquiring: no CZ bank was found reporting merchant turnover or terminal counts, so that must be chased through acquirers and PSPs in a separate cell.

The CZ technology-supplier layer is unusually legible because the demand side is silent: Czech banks publish almost nothing about ATM/POS procurement, while their vendors publish named case studies with hard fleet numbers - Diebold Nixdorf on CSOB (1,025 devices under managed services), KAL on Ceska sporitelna (1,600+ ATMs, cash recyclers, QR cardless), Temenos on Komercni banka's core migration with Syncordis. The processing tier splits between Prague-based Global Payments Europe, Nexi Central Europe's CEE hub, Worldline via KB SmartPay/GoPay, and bank-owned BANIT inside CSOB, with Euronet as the dominant independent ATM deployer. Czech-language material adds what English does not: PR-wire contract announcements on Protext, acquirer terminal catalogues and manuals that fingerprint the Ingenico/Verifone/PAX installed base, and CNB's E-ZAK procurement portal for priced, named supplier records. The main weaknesses are single-sourced vendor fleet claims of inconsistent vintage, an unmapped cash-in-transit tier, and the absence of any public installed-base figures for domestic SoftPOS vendors.

Czech public-tender routes are unusually rich for this sector because the official layer is fully digital and mandatory: VVZ (vvz.nipez.cz) plus its analytical/open-data twin ISVZ, the state e-procurement platform NEN, the EU duplicate on TED, and — uniquely valuable — the Registr smluv, which publishes the signed contracts and annexes themselves, including acquiring contracts and merchant fee manuals. Around those sit contracting-authority profiles (E-ZAK instances for ČNB and Správa železnic, Tender Arena for Česká pošta and Dopravní podnik hl. m. Prahy) and two strong secondary layers: Hlídač státu, which indexes contracts, tenders and ÚOHS decisions behind one documented API, and ÚOHS itself, whose review decisions and competition analyses restate tender volumes and ATM-network economics in detail. The hard numbers this cell can yield are cash-processing volumes at ČNB, CIT frequencies and site counts at hospitals/post/municipalities, and terminal fleet counts at transport operators; the structural blind spot is that commercial banks are outside the procurement regime entirely, so their ATM and acquiring estates never appear here. Priority next steps are pinning a CPV code set and running a 24-month recency sweep, since the confirmed examples found so far are mostly 2017-2021.

The CZ regulatory-register landscape for payments is unusually well consolidated: the ČNB's JERRS application is the single authoritative enumeration of banks, payment institutions, e-money institutions and small-scale providers, mirrored and cross-checkable against the EBA's PSD2 and credit-institution registers, with a documented but certificate-gated XML web service (WS JERRS) as the only official machine channel. The supervisory-output layer is rich and openly published — ČNB final sanction decisions with attached PDFs, annual supervision reports back to roughly 2010, and the Ministry of Finance's twenty-one-edition annual market report. The adversarial-disclosure layer is the highest-value and least tidy part: ÚOHS decision collections, merger notifications and press releases carry interbank ATM-withdrawal fee, card-scheme and ATM-network facts, and the Financial Arbitrator's published findings plus its annual reports quantify payment-services and card-fraud disputes at named institutions. Main weaknesses are the absence of any register-side ATM/POS estate data, the lack of a curated interchange case index, and dual live domains for the Ministry of Finance and the Arbitrator that require canonicalisation rather than dead-link conclusions.

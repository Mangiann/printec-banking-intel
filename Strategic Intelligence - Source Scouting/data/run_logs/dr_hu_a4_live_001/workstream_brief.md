The MNB source landscape for Hungarian payment, card, ATM/POS and cash statistics is bifurcated between two hosts: statisztika.mnb.hu carries the machine-oriented statistical releases (the quarterly 'Tájékoztató - Pénzforgalom' with cards/ATM/POS/accounts/fraud/revenues, the VIBER-BKR-KELER settlement release, the monthly balance-sheet release holding currency in circulation, plus calendar, methodology and glossary), while www.mnb.hu carries the analytical and narrative layer (Fizetési rendszer jelentés / Payment Systems Report, Pénzforgalom 2030, MNB-tanulmányok and MNB-szemle, the bankjegy-és-érme cash pages, the Annual Report and the year-indexed press releases). Both HU and EN editions exist for the flagship report and the payments release, but legacy mnb.hu/en/statistics deep links are stale (404 observed) and should be canonicalised to statisztika.mnb.hu. No MNB open API for these series was found, so file-level acquisition will need rendering-capable fetching of the release pages, with the ECB Data Portal PTT dataset as the structured, API-accessible mirror of the same MNB-reported figures. Highest-value tracking targets are the quarterly payments release, the annual Payment Systems Report, the monthly currency-in-circulation series, and the 2001-2013 archive for long-run back-series.

Hungary's supervisory/registry layer is unusually concentrated: the MNB is the single integrated authority, so one institution (mnb.hu plus the statisztika.mnb.hu and intezmenykereso.mnb.hu subdomains) holds the definitive index of every bank, e-money issuer, payment institution and PSD2 third-party provider operating in the country. The strongest pointer assets are the Intézménykereső search register, the open-banking ASPSP and AISP/PISP lists, and the Határozatok és végzések decision database, which together give both a current actor map and an entry/exit signal; the EBA PSD2 central register is the machine-readable mirror when automation matters. On the analytical side the Aranykönyv provides per-institution financials as a time series, while the Fizetési rendszer jelentés, the FinTech és Digitalizációs jelentés, Hitelintézeti Szemle and the MNB-tanulmányok series carry aggregate card, POS/ATM and cash-usage statistics with methodology. The main structural gap is that no register enumerates POS acquirers or ATM operators as such — that landscape must be derived from licence-activity fields cross-referenced with the payment reports.

Hungary's payments/banking trade-media layer is small, concentrated and unusually quantitative: Portfolio.hu's Bank desk, the VG group's bonline.hu, and the two fintech specialists (FinTechZone, Fintech.hu) between them convert almost every MNB pénzforgalmi release into charted analysis within days, and add acquirer- and vendor-level figures obtained by interview — qvik volumes from SimplePay, the first-ever decline in physical card-accepting locations, PSP revenue and fraud-loss totals. A second, structurally different tier consists of comparison portals (Bank360, Bankmonitor, Biztosdöntés, Money.hu) that compile bank-by-bank account, card and cash-withdrawal fee data from hirdetmény documents; this pricing data is genuinely unavailable in aggregated form anywhere else and is best treated as a direct source, subject to ToS checks. Consumer-facing volume outlets (Pénzcentrum, Origo, the county-daily network) are low-credibility on their own but give early and geographically granular signals on ATM-network restructuring and cash-access rule changes. Nothing in this cell publishes machine-readable data, so collection must be HU-language text extraction with mandatory reconciliation back to statisztika.mnb.hu; note also that 'POS terminál' is a heavily vendor-spammed query term and that neither napi.hu nor vg.hu produced any article-level hits in this pass.

Hungary's consultancy/academic layer on payments splits into three clusters: Big-4 and strategy firms (Deloitte HU with its Digital Banking Maturity local cuts, PwC HU's own 'Digitális Pénzügyi Körkép' consumer survey, KPMG HU financial services, McKinsey Budapest) publishing lead-generation thought leadership; commercial research houses (GKID/GKI Digital, NRC, eNET, plus the Mastercard-sponsored GKID 'Digitális Kereskedelmi Körkép') which own the only regular non-regulator series on online payment-method mix and cash-on-delivery share; and an academic tier centred on Corvinus, whose full texts sit in the open unipub and REAL repositories and whose payment-habits and cash-network work is often published through MNB paper series, Közgazdasági Szemle or the Banking Association's Gazdaság és Pénzügy. Discovery is best driven by pointer media (FinTechZone, Portfolio.hu, HWSW's deep eNET/GKI archive), since consultancy studies are announced through the press before or instead of being indexed anywhere. Main weaknesses of this cell: full research-house reports are commercial, EY Hungary shows no payments-specific output, and nothing found addresses ATM/POS acceptance-network economics — that topic needs its own targeted Hungarian-language sweep.

Hungary's cash-cycle intelligence splits cleanly into a regulator layer and a commercial layer. MNB is the anchor: it licenses cash processors (with a downloadable register), owns the banknote printer Pénzjegynyomda (which publishes audited accounts), sets cash-handling methodology, and publishes the ATM/cash time series in Pénzforgalmi adatok and the Fizetési rendszer jelentés. The 2025 ATM Act plus MNB decrees on minimum per-bank ATM counts create a compliance-reporting driver, though no public per-settlement ATM compliance register was found — deployment must be inferred from MNB statistics, government press, bank disclosures and independent deployer Euronet. The CIT/cash-processing market itself (G4S Készpénzlogisztikai, Criterion, Prompt Security, Risec, TrezEx and a long tail) is opaque by design, so the highest-yield collection routes are e-beszámoló filings, company registries, public procurement award notices, and occasional investigative reporting.

Hungary's legislative and fiscal channel for payments intelligence is highly centralised and well-digitised: Magyar Közlöny plus njt.hu carry every binding threshold (transaction levy rates under Act CXVI of 2012, the 2025 merchant cash-acceptance obligation, and the free-ATM-withdrawal limits raised to HUF 300k from February 2026), while parlament.hu bill reasonings expose the fiscal-yield and payment-volume assumptions behind each change. The tax side is strong on rules but weak on published data: NAV issues an annual guidance leaflet on the levy and maintains a public-data/FOI route, but no per-instrument levy revenue series was found, so the levy-as-payment-volume proxy must be rebuilt from budget aggregates plus statutory rates or requested under FOI. The consumer-protection side is the most quantitatively usable: MNB's annual Pénzügyi fogyasztóvédelmi jelentés and the Pénzügyi Békéltető Testület annual reports give consistent multi-year complaint and case counts broken down by product, an indirect read on card, ATM and account usage frictions. Main open gaps are the Ministry of Finance monthly budget release, the MNB supervisory decision register (IFR/payments enforcement), and GVH competition cases on card fees.

Hungary's payments weak-signal landscape is unusually concentrated: a handful of Hungarian-language hubs — Portfolio.hu's conference arm, FinTechZone (which also runs the FinTechShow/TechShow event brand), the Mastercard Év Bankja awards and the MNB's Fizetési Rendszer Fórum — carry most of the forward-looking disclosure, with named MNB, GIRO and bank executives repeatedly presenting transaction volumes and roadmap dates on stage well before official statistics appear. Infrastructure and operator sites (GIRO plus its career portal, Euronet's ATM newsroom, bank ATM-migration notices) and public procurement notices for card acceptance and POS services supply the hardest quantitative early signals, while job boards (Profession, Indeed, Jooble) reveal acquiring, POS and instant-payment build programmes through vacancy text. Funding and market-entry signals exist but only as scattered prose across trade and general media — there is no structured Hungarian funding dataset. The main uncovered channel is LinkedIn, which is where much of this community announces first and which cannot be collected compliantly at scale; the vendor/ISO layer of the POS and ATM market also still needs systematic enumeration.

For Hungary, the archival layer of payment/banking statistics is unusually well anchored: the MNB itself operates an explicit 'Archív tartalmak' section under Pénzforgalmi adatok that preserves 2001-2013 card-business, ATM/POS acceptance, chip-migration, interbank-turnover and card-fraud series that no longer appear in the live tables, and the ECB Data Portal keeps the discontinued PSS/Blue Book dataset with harmonised pre-2014 HU card and terminal series (API-only). Beyond these two anchors, recovery depends on web archives — the Internet Archive for mnb.hu and giro.hu (whose BKR turnover PDFs rotate off opaque storage URLs), since the Hungarian national web archive at OSZK is largely closed for copyright reasons. The Hungarian open-data layer is a cadastre/metadata-search stack (kozadatportal.hu, opendata.gov.hu, kozadat.hu) rather than a dataset store, so it functions as a pointer to holders rather than a download source, while KSH's STADAT archive, yearbooks and Statisztikai Szemle archive supply the historical and methodological trail. Priority actions are a file-level manifest of the six MNB archive sub-pages and a Wayback CDX sweep of the core HU payment domains.

Hungary's non-central-bank payments telemetry sits in two very different places: KSH, which publishes rich open retail-turnover and household-ICT series but exposes payment-method splits only inside narrative ICT/e-commerce publications rather than as maintained tables; and NAV, which holds by far the deeper asset — receipt-level online cash register and e-receipt data covering practically the whole retail till population, now extending through the e-pénztárgép/eNyugta regime — but publishes no aggregate payment-mode series and scopes machine access to each taxpayer's own registers. The practical collection strategy is therefore open scraping of KSH STADAT (bel* and ikt* families) plus PDF extraction from KSH ICT publications, combined with regulatory monitoring of NAV's eNyugta specification and Online Számla schema to know what fields the state actually captures. The highest-value unexplored move is a formal public-interest data request to NAV for anonymised aggregate cash-register turnover by payment mode, and retrieval of the cash-register/e-receipt decrees to confirm that payment mode is a mandatory reported field.

Hungary's payment-infrastructure operator layer is concentrated in three entities: GIRO Zrt. (BKR clearing plus the GIROInstant platform behind the AFR instant scheme and the qvik QR overlay), KELER Zrt. (CSD) and KELER KSZF/CCP — all overseen by the MNB, which is also their main statistical publisher. The operators themselves publish unevenly: GIRO has no statistics section and releases its 'BKR tranzakciók száma' series as hashed PDF assets whose current vintages could not be located in this pass, while KELER publishes daily/weekly/monthly/annual statistics including machine-readable daily CSVs. The most reliable structured series for BKR, VIBER and KELER turnover therefore sits on statisztika.mnb.hu (payment and securities settlement systems operation, plus the quarterly pénzforgalmi táblakészlet that also carries qvik adoption metrics), with the ECB Data Portal providing harmonised CCP/payment series for cross-country benchmarking. Practical collection for this cell should be document-scraping-first against giro.hu/dokumentumok and keler.hu, with MNB statistics as the authoritative fallback and MNB press releases plus Hungarian fintech trade press as the fast early-signal layer.

Hungary's association layer for banking and payments is concentrated in four bodies: Magyar Bankszövetség (the banking peak association, whose ~67-member 'Tagjaink' page is the master actor map including infrastructure members KELER and BISZ, and which publishes the quarterly journal Gazdaság és Pénzügy plus communiques rather than a statistics series), EFISZ (the Electronic Payment Service Providers Association, founded by GIRO, Magyar Posta, Magyar Telekom, OTP and others, and the only association running its own electronic-payment behaviour research), the Magyar Fintech Szövetség for the non-bank provider ecosystem, and OKSZ for the merchant/acceptance side, where roughly a third of Hungarian retail turnover sits and where the public fight over mandatory cash acceptance versus card acceptance is being argued. A second tier of associations does publish genuine member-aggregated numbers - the Leasing Association's quarterly placement volumes and the Factoring Association's turnover and GDP-penetration series are the cleanest quantitative assets in this cell - while MKIK and its research institute GVI supply long-running enterprise surveys with financial-sector breakouts. The important negative result is that no association publishes a recurring HU banking, card or ATM statistics series and no dedicated cards/ATM association exists, so A4's quantitative core must come from the central bank cell; associations here are best treated as pointer and positioning sources. Coverage is overwhelmingly Hungarian-language (only EFISZ was seen with an English track), so English-first collection would miss most of this landscape.

Hungary's commercial card/ATM/POS disclosure landscape is dominated by two card schemes with active HU press rooms — Mastercard (newsroom.mastercard.com/eu/hu, home of the 15-year 'Bankkártya Használati Szokások' survey and the Doppio acceptance-expansion programme) and Visa (visa.hu 'Visa szerkesztőség') — which publish HU-specific card penetration, contactless, mobile-wallet and acceptance-point figures earlier and more granularly than official statistics. On the acquiring/PSP side the richest operational numbers come from OTP Mobil/SimplePay via OTP Bank's news page (transaction counts, >23k partner merchants, average basket value), with Barion as the only actor publishing a transparent public price list; bank acquirers (OTP, K&H via khpos.hu, MBH, Erste via Global Payments) and independent terminal operators (Worldline/Bizpay, Nexi Central Europe, Fizetési Pont/OFSZ, Cardnet) disclose product detail but almost no volumes. ATM coverage is the weakest link: Euronet is the key independent deployer but its HU press page appears stale (counts dated 2016/2022), parent-company SEC filings give no Hungary breakout, and the most datable signals arrive indirectly through bank ATM-outsourcing notices and business-media reporting. Practical strategy: event-driven monitoring of scheme and operator press rooms as leading indicators, trade outlets (FinTechZone, Fintech.hu) as discovery feeds, and filed annual accounts from the Hungarian company register as the hard, legally-mandated proxy where operators publish no volumes.

Hungary's systemic bank issuers/acquirers split into two disclosure regimes: three genuine investor-communicating banks (OTP, MBH, Gránit) that publish quarterly reports and KPI-bearing investor decks, and five foreign-owned subsidiaries (K&H, Erste HU, Raiffeisen HU, UniCredit HU, CIB) that publish only statutory annual and Pillar 3 reports, with their Hungarian digital/card KPIs surfacing instead in parent-group segment reporting. Three pointer hubs make the cell tractable and should be the collection backbone: the BÉT/BSE kibinfo archive, the MNB tőkepiaci közzétételek portal, and the Ministry of Justice e-beszámoló register, each of which mirrors bank filings more durably than the banks' own CMS. The operational metrics this workstream wants — card counts, ATM fleet size, POS/acquiring volumes, mobile-wallet adoption — exist only as prose or chart values inside PDFs, with per-bank definitions, so the pipeline needs PDF/deck extraction and a normalisation map rather than any feed. Acquiring-side and cash-throughput figures were not found on the bank side at all and will have to come from the regulator and PSP cells.

Hungary's procurement channel for payments intelligence is unusually well-structured: EKR (ekr.gov.hu) carries the full procedure documentation for essentially every domestic tender, the Közbeszerzési Hatóság publishes the Közbeszerzési Értesítő and a CPV-searchable database of notices and contracts, and above-threshold contracts duplicate onto TED with a free, authentication-free Search API and monthly XML bulk packages — making a deterministic CPV-driven pipeline entirely feasible. The richest quantitative disclosures for our topics come from state-linked buyers: Magyar Posta (cash transport and cash processing, incumbent MPT Security), the MNB (banknote-processing systems with multi-year maintenance), and transport operators BKK and MÁV, whose ticket-vending-machine tenders quantify unattended card-acceptance fleets and even the cash-vs-card mix (card share above 70% of BKK automat purchases; dozens of card-only devices). Demand is currently being driven by statute: the 2025. évi XVIII. törvény and 19/2025 (VI.26.) MNB rendelet mandate ATM installation in every settlement of 1,000+ residents and force joint installation/operation contracts between payment service providers, so an ATM procurement wave should be visible in these channels. The main weaknesses of this cell are POS terminals and card personalisation, which in Hungary are mostly bought by private banks and processors outside the Public Procurement Act, and the absence so far of a systematic CPV sweep — both should be closed before treating procurement as a complete indicator of fleet size or vendor share.

Hungary's private-bank supplier ecosystem is legible mainly from the vendor side: international vendors (ACI Worldwide, Tietoevry, Diebold Nixdorf) publish named HU bank deployments with occasional unit counts, while the domestic layer (Capsys, T-Systems Magyarorszag, Laurel, Takarekinfo, 4iG, CARDNET) exposes bank relationships through Hungarian-language reference and news pages that search engines index poorly. The highest-value quantified anchors are not vendor marketing at all but regulator-forced disclosure — the GVH 2019 card-acceptance sectoral inquiry and MNB decree 19/2025, which allocates 1,039 mandated ATMs bank-by-bank (OTP 428, MBH 195, K&H 117, Erste 104) and thereby telegraphs the 2025-2026 ATM procurement pipeline. Cash-cycle suppliers are visible as CIT operators (G4S, MPT Security, Criterion) but the banknote-equipment vendor layer and the POS hardware vendor layer remain unmapped. Recommended next step is direct sitemap-level crawling of vendor case-study libraries plus profiling of GIRO/BISZ, since search-engine discovery systematically under-represents this cell.

For Hungary, the supranational layer is dominated by the ECB Data Portal, which carries the harmonised MNB-reported card, ATM and POS terminal series (PSD/PAY/PTN/PTT dataflows) with a clean SDMX API and is the single highest-value target in this cell. The IMF Financial Access Survey and World Bank Findex/WDI add long annual ATM-density panels and the only cross-country comparable demand-side survey evidence for Hungarian card and digital-payment behaviour, while the EBA registers map the HU payment-institution actor landscape. Two structural exclusions matter: Hungary is outside CPMI (so BIS Red Book carries no HU country table) and outside the euro area (so ECB SPACE cash-usage data exclude HU), and the 2025 EBA/ECB payment fraud report drops Hungary for incomplete data. Eurostat contributes only demand-side digital-economy indicators, not payment statistics, and is the sole aggregator in this cell with any Hungarian-language surface.
